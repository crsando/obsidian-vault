[俄罗斯](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/russia) 正在强征更多适龄男性入伍，以充实日渐疲弱的军队，因为自愿征兵陷入停滞，且莫斯科在乌克兰前线的主动权也在动摇。

律师、维权人士和目击者称，在俄罗斯的较小城镇，一些征兵人员已开始在街头、工作场所外或借例行文件检查之名拘留男性。他们表示，一旦进入征兵办公室，男性可能会遭到威胁或殴打，直到签署同意参战。

这场施压运动让人联想到俄罗斯总统弗拉基米尔·普京在2022年秋季实施的局部动员，当时俄罗斯前线阵地因缺乏人力而摇摇欲坠。这也引发了俄罗斯分析人士和政界人士的猜测，即这是否预示着另一次征兵。

俄罗斯当局向那些准备签约参战的人提供了改变人生的巨额资金——通常超过一整年的工资。但在俄罗斯军方内部的虐待传闻和[乌克兰日益致命的作战能力](https://www.wsj.com/world/ukraines-drones-are-now-reaching-siberia-and-imperiling-russian-energy-assets-e0d2caa7?mod=article_inline)之后，征兵变得更加困难，这些因素已将前线士兵的平均寿命缩短至几分钟、几天或几周。

“当局已经转向暴力手段，他们抓住男子并把他们送走，”阿尔乔姆·克利加说，他是一名律师，试图帮助那些被迫服役的人。

这些措施与[乌克兰](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/ukraine)在自身人力短缺情况下进行的强制征兵形成了对照。

俄罗斯正艰难地补充其在乌克兰损失的人员。谢尔盖·卡尔普欣/塔斯社/祖玛新闻社。

知情人士称，虽然俄罗斯的征兵表面上仍是自愿的，但执法官员有时会与军方征兵人员合作，为这些男子设下陷阱，如果他们不屈从于压力，就使用暴力或威胁逮捕他们。

在西南部的奔萨地区，征兵人员更加激进地追求目标，当地居民弗拉季斯拉夫·列昂尼多夫称，5月份，一名熟人失踪数小时后，他在军事征兵办公室找到了此人。

这名男子以核实个人数据为由被叫到当地警察局。列昂尼多夫说，当他到达时，两名军事征兵人员正在等他，并把他带到征兵办公室，在那里他的肝脏和肾脏遭到殴打。他只能在该男子被转去接受体检时介入，迫使其获释。

“如果我们晚来两个小时，他可能已经被带走了，”他说。“他们行动非常迅速。”

执法官员或征兵人员在奔萨的突袭行动引起了当地人的关注，他们在网上发布视频抗议这种做法。

一段在军事征兵办公室外拍摄的视频显示，身穿便服的男子坐在一辆军用货车内，被哭泣的女子围堵。

“他们打你，他们逼你！”其中一名女子哽咽着说道。

多年来一贯得意洋洋地指出乌克兰强制征兵的俄罗斯当局，此次却淡化这些事件，并威胁要打击传播相关视频的人。

![A Russian serviceman prepares an attack drone.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b181cc90b1481ffb708e5a8a281589f/3b181cc9-0b14-8105-8c11-d68d43ef12e5-a4c26984ee696c9d4ccc67c1ccc054ef.jpg)

在这张由俄罗斯政府发布的图片中，一名俄罗斯军人正在准备一架无人机。图片来源：俄罗斯国防部新闻处/EPA/Shutterstock

奔萨征兵办公室负责人安德烈·苏尔科夫在政府简报会上表示，该地区任何突袭行动针对的都是逃避兵役者。俄内务部奔萨分局否认突袭行动是将男性送往战场，并威胁将对发布或传播相关视频的人追究法律责任。

内务部声明称：“这些视频发布与向‘特别军事行动’区派遣人员没有任何关系。”其中使用了俄方对乌克兰战争的官方委婉说法。“传播虚假信息者将根据俄罗斯联邦法律承担法律责任。”

博主斯坦尼斯拉夫·莫罗佐夫曾通过纪录片引起人们对这一问题的关注。他说，他正受到令人畏惧的俄罗斯联邦安全局的调查。“他们没有禁止我继续，但他们已经明确表示，不继续才对我最有利，”他说。

他说，在他交谈过的数十名亲属中，大多数人在执法人员威胁要在他们身上栽赃毒品并当场逮捕他们后，同意签署合同。

部分压力源于俄罗斯不同地区的地区性项目，这些项目向第三方提供现金（多数情况下为10万卢布，约合1300美元），以让他们带人到征兵办公室。

莫罗佐夫表示，奔萨各地住宅楼的广告上写着：“带一位朋友到征兵中心，即可获得10万卢布。”

类似的运动在其他地方也存在，例如俄罗斯生产大部分无人机的鞑靼斯坦，以及阿穆尔州和雅罗斯拉夫尔州，还有卡卢加、沃罗涅日和阿尔汉格尔斯克等城市。克莱加和维权工作者表示，强制征兵的案件数量也有所上升。

分析人士称，这些措施很可能已得到克里姆林宫和国防部的批准，但各地区执行程度不一。

这些战术在很大程度上与[俄罗斯在前线不断增加的损失](https://www.wsj.com/world/russias-war-is-going-badlyon-the-ground-and-in-the-air-447ce204?mod=article_inline)有关。据军事分析人士称，目前俄军每月阵亡和受伤人数估计达3万至4万人。

“正因为有这么多人必须以某种方式征召入伍，而愿意签订合同的人越少，他们就越要施加压力，”帮助俄罗斯人逃避兵役的反战非政府组织 Get Lost 的创始人格里戈里·斯韦尔德林（Grigory Sverdlin）表示。

美国官员表示，这些努力可能在前线增加了一些兵力，但可能不足以取得任何重大突破。他们反而表示，克里姆林宫仍在权衡再次进行强制动员的可能性，这可能会再向前线输送数千名士兵。他们称，此举可能会在定于9月20日举行的俄罗斯议会选举之后实施。

任何新的动员都将给俄罗斯总统弗拉基米尔·普京带来政治风险。图片来源：Alexander Zemlianichenko/POOL/AFP/Getty Images

[俄罗斯的经济问题](https://www.wsj.com/world/russia/drone-attacks-on-russias-biggest-online-retailer-bring-war-home-to-middle-class-2e98507e?mod=article_inline)以及乌克兰日益增强的能力，使得动员问题更加紧迫。如果普京选择坚持其[拿下乌克兰东部顿巴斯全部地区的诉求](https://www.wsj.com/world/putin-russia-ukraine-ceasefire-proposal-0021453b?mod=article_inline)，那么“他可能别无选择，只能进行动员”，莫斯科防务智库战略与技术分析中心主任鲁斯兰·普霍夫表示。

虽然第一轮动员旨在召集预备役军人和有军事经验的人员，但另一轮动员可能将缺乏经验或战斗意愿的人员送上战场。

此类举措涉及的政治风险可能超过前线的军事收益。一些俄罗斯人表示，他们会以暴力反抗这样的举动。

“一旦发给我武器，我会射杀那些动员我的人，”53岁的俄罗斯预备役军官德米特里·罗金说，他是有可能被征召的人员之一。

## **The Kremlin Is Bullying More Russians Into Fighting in Ukraine**

### Measures increasingly include beatings and manipulation, fueling expectations of a second forced mobilization

By

Thomas Grove

and

Daria Matviichuk

Aug. 2, 2026 10:00 pm ET

- Russia is forcing more fighting-age men into military service through detentions and threats as voluntary recruitment stalls.**View more**
[Russia](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/russia) is strong-arming more fighting-age men into military service to boost the flagging ranks of its army as voluntary recruitment stalls and Moscow’s initiative on the front line in Ukraine falters.

In Russia’s smaller towns and cities, some military recruiters have resorted to detaining men on the street, outside their places of work or under the pretext of routine document checks, say lawyers, rights activists and eyewitnesses. Once in a military recruitment office, men may be threatened or beaten until they sign up for the war, they say.

The pressure campaign carries echoes of the partial mobilization Russian President Vladimir Putin instituted in the fall of 2022 when Russia’s front-line positions were buckling due to a lack of manpower. It has also caused speculation among Russian analysts and the country’s political class over whether it preludes another draft.

Russian authorities have offered life-changing sums of money—often more than an entire year’s salary—to those ready to sign up and fight. But recruitment has become more difficult following tales of abuse inside the Russian military and [Ukraine’s increasingly lethal capabilities](https://www.wsj.com/world/ukraines-drones-are-now-reaching-siberia-and-imperiling-russian-energy-assets-e0d2caa7?mod=article_inline), which have shortened the average soldier’s lifespan on the front to a matter of minutes, days or weeks.

“Authorities have switched to a method of violence, when they catch men and send them off,” said Artyom Klyga, a lawyer who tries to help those who have been forced into service.

The measures offer a counterpoint to the forced enlistment [Ukraine](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/ukraine) has carried out in the face of its own manpower shortage.

Russia is struggling to replace the soldiers it is losing in Ukraine. Sergei Karpukhin/TASS/ZUMA Press

While Russia’s recruitment is still officially voluntary, law-enforcement officials are at times working with military recruiters to set a trap for the men and use violence or threats of arrest if they don’t yield to the pressure, the people say.

In the southwestern region of Penza, where recruiters have more aggressively pursued their aims, local resident Vladislav Leonidov said he found an acquaintance at a military recruitment office after the man went missing for several hours in May.

The man had been called into the local police station on the pretext of checking his personal data. When he arrived, two military recruiters were waiting for him and carried him off to the recruitment office where he was beaten on the liver and kidneys, Leonidov said. He was able to intervene and force the man’s release only as he was being transferred to a medical exam.

“If we had come two hours later, he would have already been taken away,” he said. “They move very quickly.”

Raids in Penza by law-enforcement officials or recruiters have caught the attention of locals who have posted videos online in protest of the practice.

One video outside a military recruitment office shows men in civilian clothes sitting inside a military van barricaded by crying women.

“They beat you, they forced you!” one of the women says between tears.

Russian authorities, who for years had gleefully pointed out Ukraine’s forced enlistment, have played down the incidents and threatened a crackdown on those circulating videos of them.

A Russian serviceman prepares a drone in this image released by Russia’s government. Russian Defense Ministry Press Service/EPA/Shutterstock

The head of Penza’s recruitment office, Andrey Surkov, told a government briefing that any raids in the region were targeting those avoiding service. The Penza branch of the Interior Ministry has denied that raids are sending men to the front and has threatened legal consequences for those who post or publicize them.

“These video publications have no relation to sending people to the zone of the Special Military Operation,” a ministry statement said, using the official euphemism for the war in Ukraine. “Those spreading false information will face legal consequences in keeping with the laws of the Russian Federation.”

Blogger Stanislav Morozov, who has worked to bring attention to the issue in a documentary, said he is the subject of an investigation by Russia’s feared Federal Security Service. “They haven’t forbidden me from continuing, but they’ve made it clear it’s in my best interests not to,” he said.

Of the dozens of relatives he’s spoken to, he said, most agreed to sign a contract when law-enforcement officials threatened to plant drugs on them and arrest them on the spot.

Part of the pressure stems from regional programs in different parts of Russia that offer third parties cash, in most cases 100,000 rubles, around $1,300, to bring someone to the recruitment office.

Morozov said advertisements hang in residential buildings across Penza promising: “Bring a friend to the recruitment center and get 100,000 rubles,”

Similar campaigns exist elsewhere, such as Tatarstan, where Russia produces the bulk of its drones, as well as in the Amur and Yaroslavl regions, and in cities such as Kaluga, Voronezh and Arkhangelsk. The number of cases of forced recruitment has also risen, said Klyga and rights workers.

Analysts say the measures have likely been blessed by the Kremlin and Defense Ministry but are implemented to varying degrees by region.

The tactics are largely tied to [accelerating Russian losses on the front](https://www.wsj.com/world/russias-war-is-going-badlyon-the-ground-and-in-the-air-447ce204?mod=article_inline), which now reach an estimated 30,000 to 40,000 killed and wounded each month, according to military analysts.

“Just as many people have to somehow be brought into service, and the fewer there are who are ready to sign a contract the more pressure they have to use,” said Grigory Sverdlin, founder of Get Lost, an antiwar nongovernmental organization that helps Russians avoid service.

U.S. officials say the efforts have likely added some numbers at the front though likely not enough to make any major breakthroughs. Instead, they say, the Kremlin is still weighing the possibility of calling another round of forced mobilization, which could bring another several thousand soldiers to the front. Such a measure, they say, would likely come after Russian parliamentary elections, slated for Sept. 20.

Any new mobilization would carry political risks for Russian President Vladimir Putin. Alexander Zemlianichenko/POOL/AFP/Getty Images

[Russia’s economic problems](https://www.wsj.com/world/russia/drone-attacks-on-russias-biggest-online-retailer-bring-war-home-to-middle-class-2e98507e?mod=article_inline) and Ukraine’s growing capabilities have added urgency to the question of mobilization. If Putin chooses to [stick to his demands to take all of Ukraine’s eastern Donbas region](https://www.wsj.com/world/putin-russia-ukraine-ceasefire-proposal-0021453b?mod=article_inline), “he may have no other choice than to call a mobilization,” said Ruslan Pukhov, director of the Center for the Analysis of Strategies and Technologies, a Moscow-based defense think tank.

While the first round of mobilization was meant to round up reservists and men with military experience, another round would likely bring men with little experience or will to fight to the front.

The political risks involved with such a move could outweigh the military benefits at the front. Some Russians say they would resist such a move with violence.

“As soon as I’m given a weapon, I will shoot those who mobilized me,” said Dmitry Rogin, a 53-year-old Russian reserve officer who is among those who could potentially be called up.
