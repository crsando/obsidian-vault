### 中文译文

## 中国官员痴迷于安全剧场

### 公众喜欢所有这些管控——除非它们造成不便

![illustration: cornelia li](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148108a7dcfdfe07cad276/3b981cc9-0b14-8161-87c0-da2b90bb1872-e9c636377fd751549251212c80587660.jpg)

插图：Cornelia Li

在中国东部城市济南的新地铁站，乘坐自动扶梯充满危险。警示牌也这样暗示。它总共禁止了14种行为，每种都配有一条红斜线：不要在扶梯上走动，不要面朝错误方向，当然也不要玩耍。还有七项指示，包括使用扶手、紧紧握住幼儿，穿软鞋时要小心。

带着忐忑不安的心情，Chaguan 踏上了扶梯——并安然无恙地到达底部；实际上，这台扶梯和其他任何扶梯一样。大多数乘客几乎没有注意到这个标志。他们也从容地通过了地铁入口的安检。安检点看起来像机场一样，有金属探测门、行李 X 光机，以及手持扫描仪的保安。还有一个巨大的防爆罐，用于放置可疑物品。但保安们很放松。一位保安愉快地说，这个罐子从未用过。通过这里轻而易举，就像中国各地的车站一样。

但偶尔并非如此。7月下旬，南方大都市深圳的交通部门突然加强了地铁安检。保安不再随意地向乘客挥动扫描棒，而是仔细扫描每个人。当行李 X 光机发现打火机或超过允许限度的香水瓶时，这些物品就会被没收。安检队伍越拉越长，一些旅客等了半个小时。明显的理由是深圳将在今年晚些时候主办亚太经合组织（APEC）峰会。习近平将出席，绝不能出任何差错。

值得称赞的是，当居民抱怨时，深圳迅速做出了调整。‘我更担心在人群中被挤伤，而不是恐怖主义，’这是网上一条典型的评论。几天之内，深圳简化了检查流程，并安装了额外的金属探测器，让乘客几乎不费什么麻烦就能恢复通勤。

即便如此，这一事件也揭示了中国在安全与自由之间每天的平衡之举。中国以外的评论者常常关注反乌托邦元素。监控摄像头无处不在，社交媒体帖子受到严密监控，敏感地区检查站常见。然而在中国国内，很少有人因这些措施而陷入困境，而且民众对它们的支持率似乎非常高。研究人员一再发现，许多中国公民认为安全应高于隐私。在济南，老年人最热情。‘人这么多。需要安保。这让我们安心，’一位正走向地铁的白发女士任女士说。

一种看待中国的方式是将其视为一个安保国家，其国内治安和‘维稳’开支大约与军事预算相当。其中很大一部分针对政治威胁，反映了共产党对自己民众的恐惧。但交通管控指向了不同的方面：中国是一个安全国家，党高度重视预防事故和日常暴力。这种冲动值得赞扬。但在实践中，它可能令人窒息。

深圳的经历揭示了有时会浮现的紧张关系——与其说是安全与个人自由之间的紧张，不如说是与便利性之间的紧张。当安检几乎无摩擦时，人们会支持；当摩擦增加时，态度就会改变。直白地说，人们喜欢安全国家，直到它妨碍到他们，特别是当控制与实际威胁不成比例时。一个巨大的例子是新冠清零政策。公众最初支持政府的限制措施，接受武汉封城以及随后对特定人员和地区的隔离，认为这是少数人付出的代价。但到2022年底，人们已经筋疲力尽，爆发了罕见的抗议。在北京，这被视为一个教训：官员们必须在要求放松的呼声变得更加政治化之前，警惕舆论的转变。

这种转向不会轻易发生。问题不在于官员天生痴迷于安全。相反，安全是他们职业的核心。在所谓的“一票否决”制度下，他们可能因为一次安全漏洞或严重事故而失去晋升机会。在习近平先生的领导下，这一否决权得到加强：官员不仅会在坏事发生时受到惩罚，而且会在未能执行安全规程时受到惩罚。检查组突然出现在地铁站检查X光机，或者出现在街头确保巡逻到位。可见的控制措施激增：日托中心前戴头盔的警卫，流行音乐会和足球比赛中的警察方阵（远远超过看管和平人群所需的数量），以及银行和购物中心随时待命的防暴盾牌。济南地铁上的自动扶梯警示是所有作秀中最滑稽的，是一种“遮屁股式”家长作风。

### **但代价是什么？**

官员们还面临另一个权衡，这一权衡会随着时间的推移而更加棘手。所有安全措施都花费金钱。仅警卫费用就约占地铁运营预算的十分之一。他们的工资并不高。但随着劳动力储备的缩减，工资肯定会随着时间的推移而上涨，这对负债累累的地方政府来说是一个令人不安的前景。

客观地说，层层叠叠的安全措施是过度杀伤。毫无疑问，中国是一个非常安全的国家。该国地铁系统日均乘客近1亿人次，约占全球总量的一半。地铁上无论大小犯罪都极为罕见。

但这是否归功于所有那些检查站？当然，普通中国人的有序行为值得称赞。而且，就安全措施预防犯罪而言，窥视街道、车站和列车的摄像头比警卫和X光机更有用，也更便宜。然而，这并非官员们的考量，对他们来说，安全与其说是关于效率，不如说是关于表明他们不放过任何偶然。请记住：不要在自动扶梯上行走。

### English Original

## China’s officials are obsessed with safety theatre

### The public likes all the controls—until they get in the way

Illustration: Cornelia Li

TAKING THE escalator is full of danger at the new metro station in Jinan, a city in eastern China. Or so the warning sign suggests. It bars 14 actions in all, each illustrated with a red line through it: no walking on the escalator, no facing the wrong way and certainly no playing. There are also seven instructions, including to use the handrail, hold young children firmly and be careful in soft shoes.

With trepidation, Chaguan stepped on—and made it to the bottom unscathed; the escalator, indeed, was like any other. Most passengers barely even noticed the sign. They also took the security at the metro’s entrance in their stride. The checkpoint looked like that in an airport, with a metal detector gate, an X-ray machine for bags and guards wielding scanners. There was also a giant explosion-proof pot for suspicious items. But the guards were relaxed. One cheerily said the pot had never been used. It was a breeze to pass through, just as in stations across China.

Until, on occasion, it isn’t. In late July, transport authorities in Shenzhen, a southern metropolis, suddenly tightened metro security. Instead of waving their wands desultorily at passengers, guards carefully scanned everyone. When bag X-rays revealed cigarette lighters or perfume bottles above permitted limits, these were confiscated. The security lines stretched and stretched, with some travellers waiting half an hour. The apparent rationale was Shenzhen’s hosting of the APEC summit later this year. Xi Jinping will attend, and nothing must go wrong.

To Shenzhen’s credit, it quickly adjusted when residents complained. “I’m more worried about getting crushed in the crowds than about terrorism,” was a typical comment online. Within days, Shenzhen had streamlined checks and installed extra metal detectors, letting riders go back to commuting with little bother.

Even so, the episode illuminated a daily balancing-act in China between safety and freedom. Commentators outside China often dwell on the dystopian elements. Surveillance cameras are everywhere, social-media posts are closely monitored and checkpoints are common in sensitive areas. Yet inside China, few people end up on the wrong side of these measures, and popular approval of them appears very high. Researchers consistently find that many Chinese citizens think safety should trump privacy. In Jinan, the elderly were most effusive. “There are so many people. The security is needed. It puts us at ease,” said Ms Ren, a grey-haired woman heading for the metro.

One way to view China is as a security state, with spending on domestic policing and “stability maintenance” roughly as high as its military budget. Much is aimed at political threats, reflecting the Communist Party’s fear of its own subjects. But the transport controls point to something different: China as a safety state, where the party places a high priority on the prevention of accidents and everyday violence. That impulse is commendable. In practice it can be stifling.

The Shenzhen experience illustrates the tension that sometimes surfaces—not so much over safety versus personal liberty as over convenience. When security checks are nearly frictionless, there is support for them; when friction mounts, attitudes change. Bluntly, people like the safety state until it gets in their way, especially when controls are out of proportion to the actual threat. A huge example was the zero-covid policy. The public initially supported the government’s restrictions, accepting the lockdown of Wuhan and subsequent quarantines of particular people and areas as a price paid by the few. But by late 2022 exhaustion had set in and rare protests erupted. In Beijing it was seen as a lesson in why officials must be alert to shifts in opinion before the clamour for relaxation becomes more political.

These pivots do not happen readily. The issue is not that officials are naturally obsessed with safety. Rather, safety is core to their careers. In what is known as the “one-vote veto” system, they can lose out on promotion because of a single security breach or serious accident. Under Mr Xi that veto has been strengthened: officials are punished not just when something bad happens, but when they fail to enforce safety protocols. Inspection teams rock up at metro stations to check X-ray machines, or show up on streets to make sure patrols are in place. Visible controls have proliferated: helmeted guards in front of day-care centres, phalanxes of police at pop concerts and football matches (far more than are needed to watch over the peaceful crowds), and anti-riot shields at the ready in banks and shopping malls. The escalator warnings on the Jinan metro are at the comic end of all the theatre, a case of cover-your-*pigu* paternalism.

### **But at what cost?**

There is a further trade-off for officials, one which will bite more over time. All the safety costs money. The bill for the guards alone runs to about a tenth of metro operating budgets. Their salaries are not high. But a shrinking labour pool will surely push wages up over time, an uncomfortable prospect for indebted local governments.

Objectively, the layer upon layer of safety is overkill. China is, without question, a very safe country. The country averages nearly 100m riders a day on its metro systems, about half of the world’s total. Crimes, big or small, are vanishingly rare on them.

But is that thanks to all the checkpoints? Surely ordinary Chinese deserve credit for their orderly behaviour. And to the extent that security measures prevent crime, the cameras that snoop on streets, stations and trains are more useful—and less expensive—than the guards and X-rays. Yet that is not the calculation of officials, for whom safety is less about efficacy than about showing that they leave nothing to chance. And please remember: no walking on escalators.
