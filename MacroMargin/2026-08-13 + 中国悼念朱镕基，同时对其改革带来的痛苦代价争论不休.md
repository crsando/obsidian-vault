### 中文译文

## 中国悼念朱镕基，同时对其改革带来的痛苦代价争论不休

中国前总理朱镕基去世，在社交媒体上引发了一场关于他强力改革真实代价的激烈辩论，这些改革帮助国家转变为经济强国，但同时也让数千万人被这些政策抛在了后面。

尽管官方媒体将朱镕基铭记为一位强硬的、技术官僚式的领导人，他推动实施了必要的改革，为快速增长铺平了道路，但中国社交媒体用户描绘了一幅更为复杂的图景。评论者称赞他的领导力，同时也哀叹他的决定带来的痛苦影响，尤其是国有企业重组导致中国工业重镇的大规模下岗。

![Zhu Rongji in 2002. Photographer: Frederic J. Brown/AFP/Getty Images](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3bb81cc90b1481e4bb75c9d7bb420ecf/3bb81cc9-0b14-8109-b20a-f1b6268e5cc0-f79a07748d9284af315e94902e32779d.png)

20世纪90年代末，朱镕基领导了一场大规模运动，关闭或合并了非战略行业中数千家此类不盈利的国有企业。这次改革提高了生产率，帮助清理了银行体系，并使中国得以在2001年加入世界贸易组织，加速了长达二十年的出口导向型经济扩张。

但社会代价是巨大的：多达4000万工人失去了工作，同时也失去了随国有就业而来的住房和福利待遇。

“朱的政策招致批评，因为他让社会中最脆弱的群体承受了国企改革的主要冲击，”一位用户在社交媒体平台Threads上用中文写道。该帖子补充说：“在没有社会保障体系的情况下，这无异于一个勇士砍断别人的手臂。”这个说法扭曲了一个著名的中国比喻，该比喻通常用来赞扬改革者愿意做出痛苦牺牲的精神。

这些评论突显了那个时代的下岗潮在中国集体记忆中留下了深深的伤痕，尽管整个国家在此后经历了现代史上最快的增长爆发之一，直到近年来才放缓。

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3bb81cc90b1481e4bb75c9d7bb420ecf/3bb81cc9-0b14-8145-a93f-f4d133473417-aac34ad0e0da04513bfe0302473c0974.png)

在微博上，一条获得数百个赞的帖子回忆了那个时期成长起来的一代人所承受的心理创伤。该用户写道：“我很小的时候就学会世界是灰色的，因为我总是听说有人下岗后死了。”“所有大人都害怕自己会被下岗——他们总是显得忧心忡忡。”

其他帖子则突显了当今重新评估中一些人认为存在的幸存者偏差。

从房地产繁荣、私营企业和出口制造业中获益的中国中产阶级常常称赞朱镕基是自信向上、不断攀升的中国的代表。而在那些从未找到新增长引擎的地区，人们仍然愤愤不平，因为他们几乎没有看到国家整体繁荣真正惠及自己的证据。

诚然，在中国，朱镕基仍被广泛视为一个以改革为核心主题的过往时代的象征。许多帖子也称赞朱镕基是一位愿意承担巨大风险重塑经济的领导者。

即使在直接受其改革议程影响的人群中，观点也不尽相同。一些下岗工人及其子女如今承认，在朱镕基任内中国加入世贸组织，开启了一个微博用户所称的“中国的黄金时代”，为许多其他人带来了就业、收入增长和前所未有的机遇。

### English Original

## China Mourns Zhu Rongji While Debating His Painful Reform Costs

The death of China’s former Premier Zhu Rongji has set off a heated debate on social media over the true costs of his forceful reforms that helped transform the nation into an economic powerhouse, including the tens of millions of people those same policies left behind.

While state media has remembered Zhu as a tough, technocratic leader who pushed through needed changes that paved the way for rapid growth, Chinese social media users have painted a more mixed picture. Commenters have praised his leadership but also lamented the painful effects of his decisions, especially the restructuring of state-owned enterprises that led to mass layoffs across China’s industrial heartlands.

In the late 1990s, Zhu led a sweeping campaign to close or merge thousands of such unprofitable SOEs in non-strategic sectors. The overhaul boosted productivity, helped clean up the banking system and positioned China to join the World Trade Organization in 2001, turbocharging two decades of export-led economic expansion.

But the social cost was enormous: As many as 40 million workers lost their jobs, and with them the housing and welfare benefits that came with state employment.

“Zhu’s policy drew criticism because he let the society’s most vulnerable groups bear the brunt of SOE reforms,” one user wrote in Chinese on social media platform Threads. “Without a social safety net in place, this is no different from a warrior cutting off someone else’s arm,” the post added, twisting a famous Chinese metaphor that usually praises a reformer’s willingness to make painful sacrifices.

Such comments underline how the layoffs of that era have left a deep scar in China’s collective memory, even as the country as a whole went on to enjoy one of the fastest growth spurts in modern history before slowing in recent years.

On Weibo, a post that drew hundreds of likes recalled the psychological toll on a generation who grew up in that period: “I learned the world is gray from a very young age, because I always heard that someone had died after being laid off,” the user wrote. “All the adults were afraid they would be laid off — they always looked worried.”

Other posts highlight what some see as survivor bias in today’s reassessments.

Middle-class Chinese who prospered from housing booms, private business and export manufacturing often praise Zhu as the face of a confident, upwardly mobile China. Those in regions that never found a new growth engine remain embittered, seeing little evidence that the country’s overall prosperity ever truly reached them.

To be sure, Zhu is still widely remembered in China as the symbol of a bygone era with reform being the central theme. Many posts also hail Zhu as a leader who appeared willing to take big risks to reshape the economy.

Even among those directly affected by his reform agenda, views aren’t uniform. Some laid-off workers and their children now acknowledge that the country’s entry into the WTO under Zhu’s watch ushered in what one Weibo user described online as “a golden era for China,” bringing jobs, rising incomes and unprecedented opportunities for many others.
