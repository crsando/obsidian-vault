## 海湾战争削弱了美国的两个阿拉伯盟友

## 海湾战争削弱了美国的两个阿拉伯盟友

### 埃及承诺参战，约旦承诺保持中立。两者都未兑现

![photograph: ap/imago](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b14811383b3f2de809a107c/3b981cc9-0b14-8153-b367-c835bc69a4ea-cef1fa5167ee9073f931f718cd5bfe36.jpg)

图片来源：AP/Imago

大约两年前，约旦国王阿卜杜拉二世告诉伊朗外交部长阿巴斯·阿拉格希，他的国家不会成为“地区冲突的战场”。十年前，阿卜杜勒-法塔赫·塞西在竞选埃及总统时向海湾国家做出了相反的承诺：如果他们的安全受到威胁，埃及将“离他们只有一步之遥”。

然而今天，约旦正处于其最亲密的盟友美国与阿拉格希先生领导的政府之间地区冲突的中心。至于埃及，它也许只有一步之遥——但一直停在原地。当海湾地区面临35年来最大的威胁时，塞西先生除了口头表态外几乎没有提供任何实质支持。

这些未兑现的承诺很能说明问题。约旦正在为一场几乎无法控制的战争付出代价。埃及作为调解人和军事强国已被边缘化。长期以来，它们把自己标榜为地区秩序的支柱。但这一秩序看起来摇摇欲坠——两国都显得茫然无措。

约旦周围是更强大的邻国，且缺乏自然资源，因此它视与美国保持密切关系为生存关键。作为回报，美国使该国成为其最大的外援受援国之一。数千名美军部署在东部沙漠的穆瓦法克·萨尔蒂等空军基地，这些基地还驻扎着战斗机、空中加油机和其他对唐纳德·特朗普与伊朗的战争至关重要的战机。

接纳这些美军使约旦成为目标：伊朗仅开战第一个月就向其发射了至少262枚导弹和无人机。这也很不得人心。数百名政界人士、退休军官和其他约旦知名人士最近签署了一封公开信，呼吁美国撤军。然而，国王无法承担撤军的代价，尤其是因为他依赖美国的援助。去年约旦获得了超过16亿美元的援助（占GDP的3%）。本月美国又承诺提供3.55亿美元，用于基础设施、教育和其他各类项目。

约旦比以往任何时候都更需要这笔钱，因为战争冲击了其经济。导弹袭击导致航班取消，吓跑了游客；旅游业收入本已因加沙战争而遭受重创，如今进一步下滑，2026年前四个月比2025年同期下降了近10%。约旦依靠以色列的天然气来生产约一半的电力。战争爆发时，以色列因担心伊朗袭击其钻井平台而暂停了出口。这迫使国有电力公司改用柴油、重油和现货液化天然气，这些都比以色列的天然气更贵。尽管出口在四月恢复，但国际货币基金组织预计约旦今年的能源进口账单将比2025年高出36%。

伊朗试图加剧政府与公众之间的分裂。即使伊斯兰革命卫队（IRGC）向王国发射导弹和无人机，它也发出信息声称对约旦人民“没有敌意”，并说是美国而非伊朗“侵犯了你们的领土完整”。这种呼吁可能收效甚微：许多约旦人将伊朗视为威胁。尽管如此，这场战争对已经难以应对国内不满的政府来说是一个两难困境。

埃及经济表现较好。预计今年将增长4.6%。国际货币基金组织刚刚发放了一项四年期贷款协议中的最新一笔18亿美元款项。旅游业占就业的13%，仍然强劲。今年上半年有超过900万人访问埃及，比2025年同期增长3%。

乍一看，埃及其他主要硬通货来源——苏伊士运河收入和侨汇——看起来也很稳健。前者在2026年上半年从18亿美元增长30%至24亿美元，而后者在截至6月的一年中创下历史新高。然而，这两者都可能是滞后指标。伊朗支持的对红海航运的袭击可能削弱运河收入。至于侨汇，大量来自海湾地区的埃及人。该地区长期衰退意味着汇款人数减少。与此同时，政府已将一些电价提高了12%。

不过，就目前而言，埃及最大的弱点是地位，而非偿付能力。在2023年10月7日哈马斯领导的以色列大屠杀之后，塞西发现自己成为抢手人物。由于与加沙接壤，他的情报主管与哈马斯领导层有着长期关系。美国前国务卿安东尼·布林肯在大屠杀后的一年内访问了该国七次，除以色列外，比其他任何国家都多。最终停火谈判于2025年10月在埃及度假胜地沙姆沙伊赫举行。

在与伊朗的战争中，埃及的介入程度远不如前。它作为调解人的地位已被巴基斯坦、土耳其和海湾国家取代。阿拉伯世界最大的军队也没有在保卫邻国方面发挥多大作用——尽管自2013年塞西通过政变夺取政权以来，海湾国家已向埃及提供了数百亿美元的援助。

开罗官员辩称，海湾国家正在从其他盟友那里获得帮助，而埃及在边境还有其他威胁。尽管如此，它的不作为已经激怒了其朋友。三月，阿联酋总统外交顾问安瓦尔·加尔加什抱怨说，在伊朗袭击海湾地区时，一些“主要”阿拉伯国家坐视不管。“在困难时刻，你们在哪里？”他问道。

埃及确实向阿联酋部署了一个阵风战斗机中队——尽管它直到五月才宣布这一消息，恰好是在美国和伊朗之间初步停火生效一个月后。8月7日，沙特阿拉伯与巴基斯坦和土耳其签署共同防御条约时，埃及明显缺席，但沙特官员暗示埃及可能稍后加入。然而，置身战争之外并未给它带来保护。它从海湾进口能源的成本也激增。上个月，一架无人机袭击了埃及地中海沿岸达米埃塔的一处天然气设施。

三方协议预示了中东的未来。由于担心无法再依赖美国，地区国家将需要对自己的防务承担更多责任。这对埃及和约旦来说都将是一个不舒服的位置，正如他们违背承诺所证明的那样。■

## War in the Gulf has weakened two of America’s Arab allies

## War in the Gulf has weakened two of America’s Arab allies

### Egypt promised to fight and Jordan promised to stay neutral. Neither delivered

Photograph: AP/Imago

Nearly two years ago King Abdullah of Jordan told Abbas Araghchi, the Iranian foreign minister, that his country would not be a “battleground for regional conflicts”. A decade earlier, while campaigning to be Egypt’s president, Abdel-Fattah al-Sisi made the opposite vow to Gulf states: should their security ever be threatened, Egypt would be “one step away”.

Yet today Jordan is at the centre of a regional conflict between America, its closest ally, and Mr Araghchi’s government. As for Egypt, it may be one step away—but it has stayed there. While the Gulf faces its greatest threat in 35 years, Mr Sisi has offered little beyond words.

The broken promises are revealing. Jordan is paying a price for a war over which it has little control. Egypt has been sidelined as a mediator and military power. They have long sold themselves as pillars of the regional order. But the order looks wobbly—and both countries look lost.

Surrounded by stronger neighbours, bereft of natural resources, Jordan views close ties with America as key to its survival. In return, America has made the kingdom one of its largest recipients of foreign aid. Thousands of American troops are deployed at air bases such as Muwaffaq Salti, in the eastern desert, which also host fighter jets, aerial-refuelling tankers and other warplanes vital to Donald Trump’s war with Iran.

Hosting them has made Jordan a target: Iran fired at least 262 missiles and drones at it in the first month of the war alone. It is also unpopular. Hundreds of politicians, retired army officers and other prominent Jordanians recently signed an open letter calling for the withdrawal of American troops. Yet the king cannot afford to comply, not least because of his reliance on American aid. Last year Jordan received more than $1.6bn of it (3% of GDP). This month America pledged another $355m for infrastructure, education and a variety of other projects.

Jordan needs that money more than ever as the war batters its economy. Missile attacks forced flight cancellations and scared off visitors; tourism revenue, already battered by the war in Gaza, has fallen further, dropping by almost 10% in the first four months of 2026 compared with the same period in 2025. Jordan relies on natural gas from Israel to produce around half of its electricity. Israel suspended exports when the war began, fearful of Iranian strikes on its rigs. That forced the state-owned electric company to switch to diesel, heavy fuel oil and spot cargoes of liquefied natural gas, all costlier than Israeli gas. Though exports resumed in April, the IMF expects Jordan’s energy-import bill to finish the year 36% higher than in 2025.

Iran has tried to exacerbate a schism between the government and the public. Even as the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) fires missiles and drones at the kingdom, it writes messages claiming it has “no hostility” towards the Jordanian people and that America, not Iran, has “violated your territorial integrity”. Such appeals may find little purchase: many Jordanians view Iran as a threat. Still, the war is a dilemma for a government already struggling with domestic discontent.

Egypt’s economy is holding up better. It is expected to grow by 4.6% this year. The IMF just unlocked the latest $1.8bn tranche of a four-year loan agreement. Tourism, which accounts for 13% of jobs, remains strong. More than 9m people visited Egypt in the first half of this year, a 3% increase on the same period in 2025.

At first glance Egypt’s other big sources of hard currency, revenue from the Suez canal and remittances, look solid as well. The former rose by 30% from $1.8bn to $2.4bn during the first half of 2026, while the latter reached a record high in the year through June. Both could be lagging indicators, however. Iran-backed attacks on shipping in the Red Sea could dent canal revenue. As for remittances, a lot comes from Egyptians in the Gulf. A prolonged slump in that region would mean fewer workers to send money. Meanwhile, the government has raised some electricity tariffs by 12%.

For now, though, Egypt’s biggest weakness is status, not solvency. After the Hamas-led massacre in Israel on October 7th 2023, Mr Sisi found himself in high demand. His spy chiefs have a long relationship with the Hamas leadership in Gaza, owing to their shared border. Antony Blinken, America’s previous secretary of state, visited the country seven times in the year after the massacre, more than any other country bar Israel. The final negotiations for a ceasefire took place in the Egyptian resort town of Sharm el-Sheikh in October 2025.

Egypt has been far less relevant in the war with Iran. It has been eclipsed as a mediator by Pakistan, Turkey and Gulf states. Nor has the Arab world’s largest army played much role in the defence of its neighbours—even though the Gulf has given Egypt tens of billions of dollars in aid since Mr Sisi seized power in a coup in 2013.

Officials in Cairo argue that Gulf states are receiving help from other allies, and that Egypt has other threats on its borders. Still, its inaction has irked its friends. In March Anwar Gargash, the diplomatic adviser to the president of the United Arab Emirates (UAE), complained about “major” Arab countries sitting idle as Iran attacked the Gulf. “Where are you today in a time of hardship?” he asked.

Egypt did deploy a squadron of Rafale fighter jets to the UAE—although it only announced this in May, exactly a month after an initial ceasefire between America and Iran took effect. It was noticeably absent on August 7th, when Saudi Arabia signed a mutual-defence pact with Pakistan and Turkey, although Saudi officials suggest it could join later. Yet staying out of the war has not bought it protection. Its costs for energy imports from the Gulf have surged, too. Last month a drone struck a natural-gas facility in Damietta, on Egypt’s Mediterranean coast.

The tripartite pact is a glimpse of the Middle East’s future. Fearing they can no longer rely on America, regional countries will need to take more responsibility for their own defence. That will be an uncomfortable place for both Egypt and Jordan, as their broken promises demonstrate.■
