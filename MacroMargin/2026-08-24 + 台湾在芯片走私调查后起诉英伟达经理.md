### 中文译文

## 台湾在芯片走私调查后起诉英伟达经理

6月3日，台北Computex 2026英伟达产品展示会上的AI服务器机架。

*摄影：I-Hwa Cheng/法新社/盖蒂图片社*

据知情人士透露，台湾检察官起诉了[英伟达公司](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/NVDA%20US%20Equity)的一名高级经理，该经理所在的一个团体涉嫌将先进AI芯片走私至中国，此举推进了这座岛屿民主政体首次已知的对此类加速器黑市交易的打击。

英伟达的这名员工姓张，据称与其他八人一起组织了将包含高端B300芯片的74台服务器经日本和印度尼西亚运往中国，从而规避了美国的贸易限制。在他上个月最初被拘留后，彭博新闻社未能联系到他。

根据起诉书，该团伙还试图走私另外56台服务器，但被台湾当局查获。据要求匿名的知情人士称，除了英伟达员工，台湾还起诉了[超微计算机公司](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/SMCI%20US%20Equity)的两名高级员工，该公司制造了涉案硬件。这些知情人士要求匿名，是为了指认起诉书中未完整点名的被告。

基隆地方检察署周一在一份声明中表示，该团伙“为牟取暴利纵横勾结，非法出口高端服务器，增加了企业合规成本，并严重损害了台湾的国际形象。”

英伟达和超微的代表未回应置评请求。

华盛顿长期以来一直限制中国购买美国AI芯片，但中国企业依然通过复杂的黑市设法获得了这些备受青睐的加速器。美国官员称，过去几年里，价值数十亿美元的英伟达硬件已流入中国。

台湾的这起案件，尽管规模小得多，但可能是已知第一起英伟达员工因涉嫌参与影子贸易而面临法律诉讼的案例。该岛对英伟达的芯片制造和产品开发不可或缺，其密切合作伙伴[台积电](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/2330%20TT%20Equity)位于庞大的供应链生态系统之首。

据知情人士透露，检察官正寻求对英伟达经理张（Chang）判处五年监禁，理由是他涉嫌伪造和背信，他们将其描述为该计划的“核心人物”。

台湾尚未指控英伟达公司本身有任何不当行为。虽然其首席执行官黄仁勋（Jensen Huang）此前曾表示没有发现AI芯片走私的证据，但随着从美国到亚洲的检察官将多起案件提交法院，这家全球市值最高的上市公司语气有所缓和。

“走私是不可行的，”英伟达发言人在张于7月首次被拘留后发表声明说，但没有特别评论他的拘留或雇佣状况。“我们主要向知名合作伙伴（包括OEM）销售产品，他们帮助我们确保所有销售符合美国出口管制规则，”他们当时说，指的是原始设备制造商。

像超微（Super Micro）这样的OEM将英伟达芯片组装成服务器，这些服务器被成千上万地安装在用于训练和运行AI模型的数据中心中。今年夏初，台湾检察官在调查中拘留了超微的两名员工，并限制另外两人离岛后，超微表示“对任何违法者零容忍”，并已将全部四名员工停职，等待台湾调查结果。

据知情人士透露，除英伟达和超微员工外，台湾还起诉了另外四家公司的高级员工。这些公司包括超微设备经销商[青云科技](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/5386%20TT%20Equity)、据称是走私英伟达硬件最终用户的飞虎（Flying Tiger），以及另外两家协助安排中国客户并促成实际运输的公司。前两家公司未立即回应置评请求，而彭博社无法联系到后两家。

据熟悉起诉书中未具名当事人的知情人士透露，检察官怀疑该计划如下运作。彭博社尚未独立确认这一事实模式，在法庭证明之前，这仍是一项指控。

起诉书称，该计划始于2025年2月，当时飞虎的高管——公司所有者兼总经理——在日本成立了一家公司，“旨在作为走私硬件的转运点”。据该文件，他们随后通过“不明手段”使飞虎进入英伟达批准购买其芯片的公司白名单。

这两人与美超微（Super Micro）的一位高级销售总监、Albatron的总经理以及台湾数据中心运营商是方电讯（Chief Telecom）的一位项目经理合作，制造了“虚假表象”，即Flying Tiger已租下是方电讯设施中足够的空间来存放他们计划购买的硬件。他们联系了英伟达（Nvidia）的张（Chang）以申请额度。

但起诉书称，他们必须跨过一个大障碍：美超微的台湾子公司不会向未经其员工和英伟达员工核查的公司出售服务器——包括对销售八台及以上产品的现场核查。因此，9月，英伟达的张和一名被起诉的美超微员工陪同各自台湾办公室中“不知情”的员工造访了是方电讯，并刻意隐瞒了Flying Tiger实际上并未租用足够空间存放服务器的事实。

张随后告诉英伟达高管，现场检查已经完成，并寻求批准Flying Tiger的订单。美超微同意向Flying Tiger销售130台服务器，分三批交付。

2026年初，一群被告安排向一位未具名的中国客户出售其中40台服务器，一部分直运中国，另一部分经由印度尼西亚转运——全部以Flying Tiger的名义进行。

另外34台服务器由一家名为Long Wins的公司出口，该公司通过第二名被起诉的美超微经理的关系牵涉进来。这批硬件在运往第二家中国公司的途中被拆分为发往印度尼西亚和日本，而Long Wins安排了该中国公司的这笔采购。

在Flying Tiger试图以虚假文件将剩余56台服务器出口至日本之前，台湾当局就识破了这一计划。

那项行动几天后，黄仁勋抵达台北。当时，台湾当局仅拘留了三名人员，并表示已查获数量不详的搭载英伟达芯片的美超微服务器。

英伟达首席执行官在被记者问及其公司能否采取任何措施防止此类情况发生时表示，英伟达在解释规则方面是“严谨的”——并称责任在于美超微加强自身作为。美超微回应称，其政策与英伟达等公司的政策一致，并呼吁行业范围内的解决方案。

## Taiwan Indicts Nvidia Manager Following Chip Smuggling Probe

AI server racks during the Nvidia Product Showcase at Computex 2026 in Taipei on June 3.

*Photographer: I-Hwa Cheng/AFP/Getty Images*

Taiwanese prosecutors indicted a senior [Nvidia Corp.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/NVDA%20US%20Equity) manager as part of a group that allegedly smuggled advanced AI chips into China, according to people familiar with the matter, advancing the island democracy’s first known crackdown on the black-market trade of such accelerators.

Nvidia’s employee, surnamed Chang, is alleged along with eight others to have organized the shipment of 74 servers containing high-end B300 chips into China via Japan and Indonesia, circumventing US trade restrictions. Bloomberg was unable to reach him after he was initially detained last month.

The group attempted to also smuggle another 56 servers, which were seized by Taiwanese authorities, according to the indictment. Beside the Nvidia worker, Taiwan indicted two senior employees of [Super Micro Computer Inc.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/SMCI%20US%20Equity), which manufactured the hardware in question, said the people, who asked not to be named in order to identify defendants that the indictment didn’t name in full.

The group “colluded vertically and horizontally for exorbitant profits, illegally exporting high-end servers, increasing corporate compliance costs, and severely damaging Taiwan’s international image,” the Keelung District Prosecutors Office said in a statement on Monday.

Representatives for Nvidia and Super Micro did not respond to requests for comment.

Washington has long restricted China from buying American AI chips, but companies there have still managed to get their hands on the coveted accelerators through a sophisticated black market. US officials have alleged that billions of dollars’ worth of Nvidia hardware has made its way to China over the past few years.

Taiwan’s case, while on a much smaller scale, may be the first known example of an Nvidia employee facing legal action for their alleged role in the shadow trade. The island is indispensable to Nvidia’s chipmaking and product development, with close partner [Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/2330%20TT%20Equity) at the head of a sprawling supply chain ecosystem.

Prosecutors are seeking five years for Nvidia manager Chang — who they described as the “core figure” in the scheme — for forgery and breach of trust, according to the people.

Taiwan hasn’t accused Nvidia as a company of any wrongdoing. While its Chief Executive Officer Jensen Huang has previously said he’s seen no evidence of AI chip smuggling, the world’s most valuable publicly traded enterprise has softened its tone as prosecutors from the US to Asia take multiple cases to court.

“Smuggling is a nonstarter,” an Nvidia spokesperson said in a statement after Chang was first detained in July, without commenting on his detention or employment status in particular. “We primarily sell our products to well-known partners, including OEMs, who help us ensure that all sales comply with US export control rules,” they said at the time, referring to original equipment manufacturers.

OEMs like Super Micro assemble Nvidia chips into servers, which are installed by the thousands in data centers used to train and run AI models. Earlier this summer, after Taiwan prosecutors detained two Super Micro employees and restricted two others from leaving the island as part of their probe, Super Micro said that it “zero tolerance for anyone who violates the law” and had placed all four employees on leave pending the outcome of Taiwan’s investigation.

Beyond the Nvidia and Super Micro staff, Taiwan has indicted senior-level employees from four other firms, according to the people. Those are [Albatron Technology Co.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/5386%20TT%20Equity), a reseller of Super Micro equipment; Flying Tiger, the purported end user of the allegedly smuggled Nvidia hardware; and two additional companies that helped arrange Chinese customers and facilitate the actual shipment. The former two companies didn’t immediately respond to requests for comment, while Bloomberg was unable to reach the latter pair.

Prosecutors suspect the scheme worked as follows, according to people familiar with the unidentified parties from the indictment. Bloomberg has not independently confirmed this fact pattern, which remains an allegation until proven in a court of law.

The scheme began in February 2025, when top executives at Flying Tiger — the company’s owner and general manager — set up a company in Japan “intended to serve as a transit point” for smuggled hardware, the indictment said. They then managed through “unidentified means” to get Flying Tiger on a whitelist of companies that Nvidia has approved to purchase its chips, according to the document.

The duo worked with a senior sales director at Super Micro, the general manager of Albatron, and a project manager at Taiwanese data center operator Chief Telecom to create the “false appearance” that Flying Tiger had leased sufficient space at Chief Telecom’s facility to house the hardware they planned to buy. They contacted Nvidia’s Chang to apply for the allocation.

But there’s a big hurdle they had to clear, the indictment said: Super Micro’s Taiwan subsidiary won’t sell servers to a company that hasn’t passed checks by its staff and Nvidia’s — including an in-person audit for sales of eight units or more. So, in September, Nvidia’s Chang and one of the indicted Super Micro employees accompanied “unsuspecting” staff from their respective Taiwan offices on a visit to Chief Telecom, and deliberately concealed that Flying Tiger had not, in fact, leased sufficient space for the servers.

Chang then told Nvidia executives that an on-site inspection had been completed, seeking approval for Flying Tiger’s order. Super Micro agreed to sell 130 servers to Flying Tiger, which were delivered in three phases.

In early 2026, a group of the defendants arranged to sell 40 of those servers to one unnamed Chinese customer, split between a direct shipment to China and one routed via Indonesia — all under Flying Tiger’s name.

Another 34 servers were exported by a company called Long Wins, which had gotten involved via a relationship with the second indicted Super Micro manager. This hardware was split between Indonesia and Japan on its way to a second Chinese company whose purchase Long Wins had arranged.

Taiwan authorities uncovered the scheme before Flying Tiger tried to export the remaining 56 servers to Japan with false documents.

Jensen Huang landed in Taipei a few days after that action. At the time, Taiwan authorities had only detained three individuals and said that they’d seized an unspecified number of Super Micro servers containing Nvidia chips.

Nvidia’s CEO, asked by a reporter whether there was anything his company could do to prevent situations like this from happening, said Nvidia is “rigorous” in explaining the rules — and that the burden fell on Super Micro to step up its game. Super Micro responded that its policies are aligned with the likes of Nvidia and called for industrywide solutions.
