### **Takeaways**

- China's property market has been in a downward spiral for five years, with real estate values plummeting and households in financial distress being forced to sell properties.
- The government has implemented measures to revive the struggling property sector, including easing homebuying rules for non-residents in major cities, to limit the damage to developers and stem the bleeding to other vulnerable parts of the economy.
- A prolonged property slump could deepen deflationary pressures, with estimates suggesting that 3.3 million homes in China could be underwater by 2027, meaning they would be worth less than the debt secured against them.
Once one of the country’s biggest growth drivers, China’s property market has been in a downward spiral for five years. Real estate values continue to plummet, households in financial distress are being forced to sell properties, and apartment developers that racked up enormous debt on speculative projects are on the brink of collapse.

There was some optimism that government measures to end the crisis had been working to reinvigorate the market, but** **government-linked developer [China Vanke Co.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/2202%20HK%20Equity) reported a record 89 billion yuan ($13 billion) [annual loss](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-03-31/vanke-posts-12-8-billion-annual-loss-as-bond-maturities-loom) last year, bringing the company’s combined loss in the past two fiscal years to more than 130 billion yuan and** **showing just how deep the problems run. That’s after the nation’s property sector hit a grim milestone in August 2025** **when property giant [China Evergrande Group](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/3333%20HK%20Equity) delisted from the Hong Kong stock exchange, making the shares effectively worthless.

China has now implemented further measures,** **including easing homebuying rules for non-residents in the major cities, to revive its struggling property sector. The slump has heightened concerns that further weakening could destabilize the country’s financial system. It has already forced the country’s banks to confront a thorny issue: sinking real estate values are pushing millions of [mortgages underwater](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-04-06/china-s-housing-crisis-forces-banks-to-confront-underwater-mortgages), increasing the risk of losses for lenders.
中国现已采取进一步措施，包括放宽大城市非本地人的购房限制，以期振兴陷入困境的房地产行业。市场的持续低迷引发了人们更大的担忧——情况若进一步恶化可能会动摇该国的金融体系。这已经迫使国内银行面临一个棘手问题：不断下跌的房地产价值使得数百万笔抵押贷款出现负资产状况，从而增加了贷款机构的损失风险。

### How did some Chinese developers get into huge financial trouble? 一些中国房地产开发商为何会陷入严重的财务困境？

In 1998, China created a nationwide housing market after tightly restricting private sales for decades. Back then, only a third of its people lived in towns and cities. That’s risen to two-thirds, with the urban population expanding by 480 million. The exodus from the countryside represented a vast commercial opportunity for construction firms and developers.
1998 年，在长期严格限制私人房产销售之后，中国建立了全国性的房地产市场。当时仅有三分之一的中国人居住在城镇中，这一比例现已上升至三分之二，城市人口增加了 4.8 亿。大量人口从农村涌入城市，为建筑公司和开发商带来了巨大的商业机遇。

Money flooded into real estate as the emerging middle class leapt upon what was one of the few safe investments available, pushing home prices up sixfold over the 15 years ending in 2022. Local and regional authorities, which rely on sales of public land for a chunk of their revenue, encouraged the development boom. At its peak, the sector directly and indirectly accounted for about a quarter of domestic output and almost 80% of household assets. Estimates vary, but counting new and existing homes, plus inventory, the sector was worth about[ $52 trillion](https://amcham-shanghai.glueup.cn/resources/protected/organization/1682/event/33929/dd5395f4-eb4b-4d6e-9ccd-88c95998f5ea.pdf) in 2019 — about twice the size of the US real estate market.
随着新兴中产阶级将房地产视为为数不多的安全投资选项，大量资金涌入该行业，使得截至 2022 年的 15 年间房价上涨了六倍。而那些依赖土地出让收入维持财政运转的各地政府也纷纷推动房地产发展。在巅峰时期，房地产行业直接或间接贡献了国内生产总值的约四分之一，以及家庭资产的近 80%。虽然具体数字存在差异，但若将新建住宅、现有住宅以及库存都计算在内，2019 年该行业的价值约为 52 万亿美元，大约是美国房地产市场规模的两倍。

China’s property market is in crisis. Home prices are falling, developers are defaulting and people are angry. The worry is that a total collapse will bring down an already faltering economy. Bloomberg Originals explores how the real estate sector became such a mess and what the implications could be for the global economy. Source: Bloomberg
中国的房地产市场正处于危机之中。房价不断下跌，开发商纷纷出现违约行为，民众也充满不满。人们担心的是，一旦市场彻底崩溃，本已疲软的经济将会进一步恶化。《彭博原创》将探讨房地产行业为何会陷入如此困境，以及这可能对全球经济带来怎样的影响。来源：彭博社

The property craze was powered by debt as builders rushed to satisfy expected future demand. The boom encouraged speculative buying, with new homes pre-sold by developers who turned increasingly to foreign investors for funds. Opaque liabilities made it hard to assess credit risks. The speculation led to astronomical prices, with homes in boom cities such as Shenzhen becoming less affordable relative to local incomes than those in London or New York. In response, the government moved in 2020 to reduce the risk of a bubble and temper the inequality that unaffordable housing can create.
房地产热潮的背后是债务驱动——开发商急于满足预期的未来需求。这种繁荣状况催生了投机性购房行为，开发商纷纷提前销售新房，并越来越多地寻求外国投资者提供资金支持。由于负债情况不透明，人们很难准确评估信用风险。投机行为导致了房价飞涨，在深圳这样的热门城市，房价相对于当地居民的收入而言已变得与伦敦或纽约的房价相当。为此，政府在 2020 年采取行动，旨在降低泡沫风险，并缓解高房价带来的不平等问题。

Anxious to rein in the industry’s debts and fearful that serial defaults could ravage China’s financial system, officials began to squeeze new financing for developers and asked banks to slow the pace of mortgage lending. The government imposed stringent rules on debt ratios and cash holdings for developers that were called the “three red lines” by state-run media. The measures sparked a cash crunch for developers that was exacerbated by the impact of aggressive measures to contain Covid-19, such as the suspension of construction sites.

Many developers were unable to adhere to the new rules as their finances were already stretched. In 2021, Evergrande defaulted on more than $300 billion, triggering the beginning of China’s property crisis. Two more property giants defaulted — [Sunac China Holdings Ltd](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/1918%20HK%20Equity) in 2022 and [Country Garden Holdings Co.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/2007%20HK%20Equity) in 2023.

### **What happened to Evergrande?**

Evergrande’s downfall was by far the biggest corporate collapse in China’s property crisis. Founded in 1996 by Hui Ka Yan, Evergrande’s rapid expansion was fueled from the outset by heavy borrowing. It became the most indebted borrower among its peers, with total liabilities reaching about $360 billion at the end of 2021.

For a time, it was the country’s biggest developer by contracted sales and was worth more than $50 billion at its peak in 2017. Founder and chairman Hui became Asia’s second-richest person. Over the years, the company also invested in the electric vehicle industry and bought a local football club.

But when Beijing started to crack down on the property sector in 2020, the new measures put a cap on the developer’s borrowing capacity, effectively cutting off its lifeline to credit markets. Following failed restructuring attempts, Evergrande was given a winding-up order in Hong Kong in 2024. After a long trading suspension, the Guangzhou-based company was [delisted the following year.](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-08-12/evergrande-stock-to-delist-in-china-property-crisis-milestone)

On Aug. 20 this year, Hui was sentenced to life in prison after he pleaded guilty to charges including fundraising fraud and bribery.** **The court sentenced another 56 people with charges related to Evergrande, including Hui’s sons Xu Zhijian and Xu Tenghe, and his former companies were fined 15.82 billion yuan ($2.4 billion).

### **How did the crackdown affect the property market?**

After years of insatiable demand from buyers, the market ground to a halt. In addition to the government’s lending restrictions, the economic shock of Covid lockdowns[ reinforced a culture of frugality](https://www.bloomberg.com/features/china-economy-2025-consumption/), and a deteriorating job market meant people were suddenly facing layoffs and salary cuts.

Property prices began to fall in late 2021. As of July, used homes in top-tier cities had lost more than a third of their value, while those in smaller cities had fallen even further.

With [household debt](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-06-06/china-s-homeowners-and-banks-are-trapped-in-a-mortgage-mess)** **near a record high of 137% of disposable income per capita at the end of 2025,** **homeowners are under increasing financial pressure. Some homeowners are being forced to sell their properties at a discount, adding to downward pressure on prices.

The weakness has continued to shake more cash-strapped developers. Mid-sized builder [China South City Holdings Ltd.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/1668%20HK%20Equity) was ordered to liquidate by Hong Kong’s High Court in August 2025 after a default more [than two years ago](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-02-14/china-south-city-partially-owned-by-a-city-unit-warns-of-possible-default). Hong Kong’s courts have issued [at least nine wind-up orders](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-01-15/chinese-builder-jingrui-ordered-to-liquidate-by-hong-kong-court) for Chinese developers since the crisis began in 2021.

### **How is the government trying to prop up the market?**

In 2022 authorities realized the rules to rein in the market had gone too far. Aiming to avoid a “[Lehman](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/LEHMQ%20US%20Equity) moment” — when the failure of the US bank in 2008 sent shockwaves through global markets — the government unveiled measures centered on boosting equity, bond and loan financing for developers to alleviate the liquidity crunch.

Developers were allowed to access more money from apartment pre-sales, the industry’s biggest source of funds, and 200 billion yuan ($27 billion) was advanced as special loans to complete stalled housing projects. The government tweaked financial rules, allowing the central bank to increase support for distressed developers and instructing banks to ensure growth in both residential mortgages and loans to developers in some areas.

Since mid-2024, the government has cut borrowing costs on existing [mortgages](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-09-29/china-moves-to-cut-mortgage-rates-to-revive-housing-market), relaxed buying curbs in big cities and lowered [taxes](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-11-13/china-cuts-taxes-for-home-purchases-in-fiscal-policy-support-m3frbinm) on home purchases. It also trimmed purchasing [costs](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/terminal/SN5L3TDWLU68) for people seeking to upgrade dwellings in some big cities. In August 2025, Beijing authorities removed a cap to allow eligible families to buy an unlimited number of homes in outer suburban areas, and** **Shanghai and Shenzhen soon followed.

Despite these measures, the property market continues to deteriorate. Bloomberg reported in November last year that Chinese policymakers were now weighing [a new round of measures](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-20/china-weighs-new-property-stimulus-package-as-crisis-drags-on). Proposals include subsidizing mortgage interest payments to lure back wary homebuyers into a market still in free fall. Other ideas being discussed include bigger income tax rebates for borrowers and lowering transaction fees on home sales.

### **What’s at stake if China’s property market worsens?**

Government officials are clearly eager to bring the property crisis under control. They aim to limit the damage to developers and stem the bleeding to other vulnerable parts of the economy. This includes banks with heavy exposure to real estate; the construction industry, which employs [51 million people](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/business/international/2024/12/26/chinas-finance-property-firm-workforces-shrink-for-first-time/); and local governments that rely on land sales to developers to sustain their public spending.

Chinese banks’ bad debt — loans they no longer expect to recover — hit a [record](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/CNNPTT%20%20%20Index) 3.7 trillion yuan at the end of June. [Fitch Ratings](https://www.bloomberg.com/profile/company/0426045D:US) has [warned](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-10-29/china-home-sales-slump-to-deepen-despite-stimulus-fitch-says) the situation could deteriorate further in 2026 as households struggle to repay mortgages and other loans.

UBS Group AG [estimates](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2026-04-06/china-s-housing-crisis-forces-banks-to-confront-underwater-mortgages) that 3.3 million homes in China** **could be underwater by 2027, meaning they would be worth less than the debt secured against them.** **In other words, selling those properties would not raise enough to fully repay their outstanding mortgages.** **UBS estimates as much as 232 billion yuan of mortgages and business loans collateralized by those homes could be at risk. While that’s relatively small compared with China’s overall volume of outstanding loans, the scale of underwater loans would be unprecedented in China’s history.

A prolonged property slump could also deepen [deflationary pressures](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-09-06/china-ex-central-bank-governor-makes-rare-nod-to-deflation-worry). Former finance minister [Lou Jiwei recently](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-11-14/china-s-ex-finance-chief-warns-property-bust-to-worsen-deflation) warned that households’ worsening outlook — driven by falling home values — will affect consumption levels and intensify price declines. According to economists at [Morgan Stanley](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/MS%20US%20Equity) and Beijing-based think tank CF40, the property sector’s drag on inflation could even be greater than official data suggest.
