### 中文译文

## 胡塞武装如何成为伊朗最令人生畏的盟友

### 也门与红海均受威胁

![photograph: ap](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c881cc90b1481218609e37097fbf1c0/3c881cc9-0b14-811d-9474-ff5f08f87b96-2eeca95b66902f8681cc6ddbcc5faa0c.jpg)

图片来源：美联社

四年来，也门一直维持着一种令人不安的停火状态，使其长期内战得以搁置。但现在公开冲突再次爆发。胡塞武装是伊朗支持的武装叛乱组织，他们指责也门国际承认的政府及其最重要的盟友沙特阿拉伯，打算发动大规模进攻。7月30日，他们的领导人[阿卜杜勒-马利克·胡塞](https://www.economist.com/the-world-in-brief/2026/08/02/ba73a3f0-9f03-4964-9b05-d54c311d9314)表示，他的部队将“以我们自己的全面升级来回应他们的全面升级”。整个8月，他们持续对政府阵地发动无人机和导弹袭击，造成数十名士兵和平民死亡。也门军队已予以猛烈还击。

胡塞武装认为，重启战争能给他们带来巨大好处。自7月中旬以来，该民兵组织一直在红海袭击[与沙特相关的船只](https://www.economist.com/middle-east-and-africa/2026/07/30/saudi-arabia-is-locked-in-a-dangerous-stand-off-with-the-houthis)，以及港口、机场和炼油厂。据数据提供商Kpler称，本月几乎没有沙特油轮穿越红海南端的曼德海峡。胡塞武装的袭击有两个目的。第一，要挟沙特王国支付也门公务员工资，并解除对航班和航运的限制。第二，掌握主动权。如果沙特及其盟友正在计划进攻，胡塞武装已经抢先一步。

![map: the economist](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c881cc90b1481218609e37097fbf1c0/3c881cc9-0b14-812d-993e-da1a8d080203-36b1c4bf96714d3a801994befcba2f62.png)

地图：《经济学人》

他们可能还有更大的目标。自2014年以来，胡塞武装一直控制着也门西北部，包括首都萨那，以及西海岸的大部分地区。约70%的也门人生活在他们的统治之下。但叛乱分子长期以来想要的更多。8月15日，胡塞导弹袭击了曼德海峡附近的穆哈港，造成约1600万美元的损失，并迫使该港口关闭。这些袭击似乎旨在削弱国家抵抗力量，这是一支以穆哈为基地的政府支持的精英战斗部队。迫使该部队撤出该市将使胡塞武装能更多地控制通过海峡的航运。

胡塞武装还希望控制该国的油田。2022年初，他们几乎占领了萨那以东马里卜的油井，但被巨人旅击退，这是一支由阿联酋训练的、与政府结盟的民兵组织。那年晚些时候，他们轰炸了沿海的石油终端，以阻止敌人出口燃料。但今年7月，政府表示将恢复出口。此后，胡塞武装对马里卜进行了猛烈打击。他们的战斗人员已在该地区动员起来，可能会对其资源发动又一次进攻。即使他们没有占领油井，也可能希望迫使政府分享其收入。

作为回应，政府也一直在鼓吹战斗。四年前的休战本应达成和平协议，但胡塞武装反而巩固了权力，加强了与伊朗的伙伴关系。给他们一个沉重的打击可能有助于政府——其领导人大多在沙特首都利雅得——在也门重新获得信誉。8月20日，政府副总统阿卜杜拉·阿利米表示，收复萨那是"板上钉钉的事"。官员们援引2024年巴沙尔·阿萨德在叙利亚的伊朗支持政权垮台作为胡塞武装可能遭遇的先例。

这类言论可能主要是为了鼓舞士气。但反胡塞力量在冬季大部分时间里都在内斗，至少现在展现出更加团结的阵线。政府解散了由阿联酋支持的分裂组织南方过渡委员会，将其部分部队纳入军队控制。今年早些时候，胡塞武装控制的焦夫地区爆发部落骚乱，让人看到叛军权威削弱的希望。政府军在使用无人机方面变得更加熟练，而无人机使用长期以来一直是胡塞武装的主要优势之一。

尽管如此，他们面对的是强敌。沙特和政府军的有限打击未能吓阻叛军。胡塞武装过去曾经历过更大规模、更有针对性的行动。去年夏天，以色列的袭击打死了该组织文职政府中的大多数人，包括其总理，但它[迅速恢复](https://www.economist.com/middle-east-and-africa/2026/03/28/the-houthis-attack-on-israel-may-point-to-escalation-in-the-iran-war)。杀死胡塞先生，他们的领导人，也未必能改变什么。他是在其兄弟侯赛因2004年战死后上台的。

击败胡塞武装很难。在伊朗的盟友中，胡塞武装以胆大妄为著称。2014年他们横扫萨那，是违抗了伊朗资助者的意愿，后者敦促克制。现在他们的自信来自实力。2025年美国对他们发动打击时，该组织击落了七架美国"收割者"无人机，并险些击中数架战斗机。上个月，他们声称击落了一架由沙特操作的土耳其"旗手"无人机。他们也很善于适应。胡塞武装拥有无法被干扰的光纤无人机，这将有助于他们瞄准前线的政府军。

坚韧的供应网络帮助他们维持库存。该组织过去从伊朗进口成品武器，现在其大部分武器库是由通过阿曼和阿联酋走私的小型零部件或藏在从非洲之角驶来的*三角帆船*中的零件制造而成。一些胡塞无人机几乎可以完全不用伊朗零件制造。这种走私系统使叛军免受旨在削弱其库存的袭击，例如美国的袭击。"他们重建受损或被毁能力的速度远快于人们的预期，"纽约哥伦比亚大学的彼得·索尔兹伯里说。

地理是另一个优势。叛军控制的高地易于防守，特别是他们大量使用地雷。胡塞武装接管时还夺取了先前由军队控制的洞穴和碉堡系统。卫星证据表明这些设施正在扩建。政府军最近缴获了运往胡塞港口的隧道挖掘设备。

所有这些都应让政府担忧。沙特或美国的支持可以增强地面上的政府军。美国总统唐纳德·特朗普威胁要对胡塞武装进行"重大军事惩罚"。但代价会很高。胡塞先生津津乐道于也门作为"入侵者坟墓"的声誉。他可能被证明是对的。■

## How the Houthis have become the most fearsome of Iran’s allies

## How the Houthis have become the most fearsome of Iran’s allies

### Yemen and the Red Sea are both threatened

Photograph: AP

FOR FOUR YEARS an uncomfortable truce has prevailed in Yemen, putting its long civil war on hold. But now open conflict is breaking out once again. The Houthis, Iran-backed armed rebels, have accused Yemen’s internationally recognised government and Saudi Arabia, its most important ally, of planning a big offensive. On July 30th their leader, [Abdul-Malik al-Houthi](https://www.economist.com/the-world-in-brief/2026/08/02/ba73a3f0-9f03-4964-9b05-d54c311d9314), said his forces would "respond to their full-scale escalation with one of our own". Throughout August they have launched drone and missile attacks against government positions, killing dozens of soldiers and civilians. The Yemeni army has struck back hard.

The Houthis think they have much to gain from restarting the war. Since mid-July the militia has been firing at [Saudi-linked ships](https://www.economist.com/middle-east-and-africa/2026/07/30/saudi-arabia-is-locked-in-a-dangerous-stand-off-with-the-houthis) in the Red Sea, as well as ports, airports and refineries. Hardly any Saudi tankers have sailed through the Bab al Mandab strait, at the sea’s southern tip, this month, according to Kpler, a data provider. The Houthis’ attacks have two aims. First, to blackmail the kingdom into paying Yemeni civil servants and lifting restrictions on flights and shipping. Second, to seize the initiative. If the Saudis and their allies were planning an offensive, the Houthis have beaten them to it.

Map: The Economist

They may also have bigger goals. The Houthis have controlled Yemen’s north-west, including the capital, Sana’a, and much of the west coast, since 2014. Some 70% of Yemenis live under their rule. But the rebels have long wanted more. On August 15th Houthi missiles hit the port of Mokha, near Bab al-Mandab, causing damage worth some $16m and forcing it to close. The attacks seem designed to weaken the National Resistance Forces, an elite group of government-backed fighters that use Mokha as a base. Forcing them from the city would give the Houthis more control of shipping through the strait.

The Houthis also want control of the country’s oilfields. In early 2022 they nearly captured oil wells in Marib, east of Sana’a, but were repelled by the Giant Brigades, a government-aligned militia trained by the United Arab Emirates (UAE). Later that year they bombed oil terminals on the coast to prevent their foes exporting the fuel. But this July the government said it would resume shipments. Since then the Houthis have struck Marib heavily. Their fighters, who have mobilised in the region, could make another push for its resources. Even if they do not capture the wells, they may hope to force the government to share its revenues.

In response, the government has also been talking up a fight. The truce four years ago was supposed to lead to a peace deal, but instead the Houthis have consolidated their power and strengthened their partnership with Iran. Giving them a bloody nose could help the government—whose leaders are mostly in the Saudi capital, Riyadh—regain credibility in Yemen. On August 20th Abdullah al-Alimi, the government’s vice-president, said that recapturing Sana’a was "a settled matter". Officials have invoked the collapse of Bashar al-Assad’s Iran-backed regime in Syria in 2024 as a precedent for what could happen to the Houthis.

Such talk may mostly be intended to boost morale. But the anti-Houthi forces, who spent much of the winter fighting among themselves, are at least showing a more united front. The government has disbanded the Southern Transitional Council, a secessionist outfit backed by the UAE, bringing some of its troops under the army’s control. Earlier this year tribal unrest flared in the Houthi-controlled region of Jawf, kindling hopes that the rebels’ authority is weakening. Government troops have become more adept at deploying drones, whose use has long been one of the Houthis’ main advantages.

Still, they face a formidable foe. Limited strikes by Saudi and government soldiers have failed to deter the rebels. The Houthis have endured bigger and more targeted campaigns in the past. Last summer Israeli strikes killed most of the group’s civilian government, including its prime minister, but [it quickly recovered](https://www.economist.com/middle-east-and-africa/2026/03/28/the-houthis-attack-on-israel-may-point-to-escalation-in-the-iran-war). Nor would killing Mr Houthi, their leader, necessarily change much. He came to power after his brother, Husayn, died in battle in 2004.

Defeating the Houthis would be hard. Among Iran’s allies, the rebels are known for their audacity. When they swept into Sana’a in 2014, they did so in defiance of their Iranian sponsors, who urged restraint. Now their confidence comes from a position of strength. When America targeted them in 2025, the group shot down seven American Reaper drones and nearly hit several fighter jets. Last month they claimed to have downed a Turkish-made Bayraktar drone operated by the Saudis. They are also adaptable. The Houthis have access to fibre-optic drones that cannot be jammed, which would help them target government troops on the front line.

Resilient supply networks help them keep up their stocks. Whereas the group used to import finished weapons from Iran, it now builds much of its arsenal from small components smuggled through Oman and the UAE or hidden in *dhows* sailing from the Horn of Africa. Some Houthi drones can be built almost entirely without Iranian parts. This smuggling system has insulated the rebels from attacks, such as America’s, which were supposed to degrade their stockpiles. "They have been able to reconstitute capabilities that were damaged or destroyed much more quickly than people anticipated," says Peter Salisbury of Columbia University in New York.

Geography is another advantage. The highlands that the rebels control are easy to defend, especially given their heavy use of landmines. When the Houthis took over they also seized a system of caves and bunkers previously held by the army. Satellite evidence suggests these are being expanded. Government forces recently seized tunnelling equipment destined for Houthi ports.

All this should worry the government. Saudi or American support could bolster government soldiers on the ground. Donald Trump, America’s president, has threatened the Houthis with a "major military punishment". But the costs would be high. Mr Houthi speaks fondly of Yemen’s reputation as a "graveyard of invaders". He may yet prove correct.■
