### **美国陆军22亿美元的核微反应堆推动计划**

随着特朗普政府向期限紧迫的试点项目投入数十亿美元，部署和运行西方世界首座小型模块化核反应堆的竞赛正在加剧。

周三，美国陆军向五家公司授予了价值22亿美元的合同，用于在全国五个军事基地建造和运营一批首创型核微反应堆。

这些合同是特朗普政府计划的一部分，旨在重振美国的工业能力和技术领先地位，并增强陆军在全球投射力量的能力。

微反应堆是一种小型、工厂制造的核裂变反应堆，可装入标准集装箱，旨在于生产清洁电力和热能。其设计发电量约为1至20兆瓦，足以为多达2万户家庭供电。

目前唯一已建成并运行的小型模块化反应堆位于俄罗斯和中国，这给华盛顿在该行业带来了战略挑战，而美国历来是该行业的全球领导者。

“雅努斯计划”旨在满足特朗普于2025年5月[发布](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/05/deploying-advanced-nuclear-reactor-technologies-for-national-security/)的行政命令的要求，该命令要求陆军在2028年9月30日前在美国军事设施运行一座先进微反应堆。陆军和国防部创新部门将为供应商提供技术和后勤支持，以及资金以支持快速部署。

陆军部长丹·德里斯科尔表示：“授予这些合同将增强我们直接向军事设施提供安全、可靠基荷电力的能力。我们正在构建必要的能源韧性，以便在全球范围内投射作战力量，同时不依赖可能脆弱的外部电网。”

入选的公司和军事基地包括：位于北卡罗来纳州布拉格堡的安塔瑞斯核能公司（Antares Nuclear）、位于肯塔基州坎贝尔堡的BWXT先进技术公司（BWXT Advanced Technologies）、位于得克萨斯州胡德堡的通用原子电磁系统公司（General Atomics Electromagnetic Systems）、位于佐治亚州本宁堡的Radiant公司，以及位于纽约州德拉姆堡的西屋政府服务公司（Westinghouse Government Services）。

陆军在新闻发布会上告诉记者，这些公司预计将利用联邦资金筹集数十亿美元的额外私人资本，以在未来在其他军事基地建造20多个微堆。

陆军将监管其军事基地上反应堆的部署和运行，这一有争议的举措限制了美国核管理委员会（NRC）在这一过程中的咨询作用。忧思科学家联盟认为，近期加速部署核反应堆的努力可能会削弱NRC和整个行业的安全标准。

特朗普政府表示，其改革不会对安全产生负面影响。

陆军设施、能源和环境首席副助理部长杰夫·瓦克斯曼表示：“这些反应堆由陆军许可，而非NRC许可。因此，没有正式的NRC程序。然而，我们的总体想法是，最终我们想要一个商业产品……我们正努力从实验和原型过渡到实际的商业产品。”

商业反应堆的部署和运行将由NRC监督。

首批五个微堆部署在军事基地，这些基地已接入电网。未来，陆军可能会部署该技术，为无法获得其他电力来源的偏远地区供电。然而，目前尚不清楚核反应堆能否从通过潜艇和航空母舰在海战中发挥作用，扩展到陆基作战。

“我认为没有人会把核反应堆投放到真正的战术区域。我们不会很快拥有核动力坦克或类似的东西，”瓦克斯曼说。“但核电当然在偏远和艰苦的地方很有意义，比如电力难以获取、补给线漫长或电价昂贵的地方。”

他补充道：“看看我们在阿拉斯加或岛屿上的情况，我们的能源状况往往非常脆弱……在我们许多太平洋岛屿上，我们依赖悬挂外国国旗的外国船只运来的燃料，如果未来发生冲突，这不会给我们带来多少安慰。因此，能够带来可靠的能源，并且知道它可以在不加油的情况下提供多年的电力，将完全改变游戏规则。”

陆军与核能有着悠久且时而坎坷的历史。1957年，它在华盛顿特区外的贝尔沃堡启用了第一座核反应堆。这座10兆瓦热容量的设施在一年后美国第一座商业核电站建成之前，就接通了北弗吉尼亚的电网。

陆军于1973年关闭了该反应堆，并在1973年至1974年的部分退役期间移除了核燃料和控制棒。但由于污染，它直到2026年才被完全拆除并恢复现场。

与此同时，陆军在爱达荷沙漠委托建造的实验反应堆——固定式低功率反应堆一号——于1961年1月发生蒸汽爆炸和堆芯熔毁，造成三名操作员死亡，这是美国历史上唯一一次致命的反应堆事故。

### **The US Army’s $2.2bn nuclear microreactor push**

The race to deploy and operate the first small modular nuclear reactor in the western world is intensifying, as the Trump administration pours billions of dollars into pilot projects with tight deadlines.

On Wednesday the US Army awarded contracts worth $2.2bn to five companies to build and operate a fleet of first-of-a-kind nuclear microreactors at five military sites across the country.

The contracts are part of a Trump administration programme that aims to revitalise America’s industrial capacity and technology leadership, as well as strengthen the army’s ability to project power globally.

A microreactor is a small, factory-built nuclear fission reactor that can fit inside a standard shipping container and is designed to produce clean electricity and heat. They are designed to generate roughly one to 20 megawatts of electricity, which is enough to power up to 20,000 homes.

The only SMRs that have been built and are operating today are in Russia and China, creating a strategic challenge for Washington in an industry where it has historically been a global leader.

The Janus Program aims to meet the terms of an executive order [issued](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/05/deploying-advanced-nuclear-reactor-technologies-for-national-security/) by Trump in May 2025, which called on the army to operate an advanced microreactor at a US military facility by September 30 2028. The army and Department of Defense’s Innovation Unit will provide technical and logistical support to the vendors, as well as funding to support a fast-track deployment.

“Awarding these contracts accelerates our ability to deliver safe, reliable baseload power directly to our installations. We are building the energy resilience necessary to project combat power globally, without relying on potentially vulnerable external grids,” said Dan Driscoll, secretary of the army.

The successful companies and military bases are: Antares Nuclear at Fort Bragg in North Carolina; BWXT Advanced Technologies at Fort Campbell, Kentucky; General Atomics Electromagnetic Systems at Fort Hood, Texas; Radiant at Fort Benning in Georgia; and Westinghouse Government Services at Fort Drum in New York.

These companies were expected to leverage the federal funding to raise billions of dollars of additional private capital to build more than 20 microreactors at additional military sites in the future, the army told journalists at a press briefing.

The army will regulate the deployment and operation of the reactors on its military bases, a controversial move that limits the US Nuclear Regulatory Commission to a consultative role in the process. The Union of Concerned Scientists has argued recent efforts to fast-track deployment of nuclear reactors risk undermining the NRC and safety standards across the industry.

The Trump administration has said its reforms will not negatively impact safety.

“These are army-licensed, they are not NRC-licensed. So, there is no formal NRC process. However, the whole idea here is that at the back end of this we want a commercial product . . . we’re trying to transition from experiments and prototypes to actual commercial products,” said Jeff Waksman, principal deputy assistant secretary of the army for installations, energy and environment

Deployment and operation of commercial reactors would be overseen by the NRC.

The initial deployment of five microreactors is at military bases, which already have connections to the electric grid. In the future, the army could deploy the technology to provide power to remote sites cut off from alternative sources of electricity. However, it is unclear whether nuclear reactors could evolve from playing a role in maritime warfare through submarines and aircraft carriers to land-based combat.

“I don’t think anyone is going to put a nuclear reactor into a real tactical zone. We’re not going to have nuclear power tanks or anything like that anytime soon,” said Waksman. “But certainly where nuclear power makes a lot of sense is in remote and austere locations, so places where electricity is difficult to get, where there are long supply lines or where electricity is expensive.”

He added: “If you look at the situations that we have, say, in Alaska or on islands, we often have very tenuous energy situations . . . On a lot of our Pacific islands we are dependent on foreign ships’ fuel from foreign-flagged vessels and that doesn’t give us a lot of comfort if there were ever to be a future conflict. So being able to bring reliable energy that we know can provide power for years without refuelling would be a complete game-changer.”

The army has a long and, at times, troubled history with nuclear energy. In 1957 it activated the first nuclear reactor at Fort Belvoir, just outside Washington DC. The 10-megawatt thermal capacity facility was connected to the power grid in northern Virginia before the nation’s first commercial plant was built a year later.

The army shut down the reactor in 1973 and removed the nuclear fuel and control rods during a partial decommissioning in 1973–1974. But it was only fully demolished and its site restored in 2026 due to contamination.

Meanwhile, an experimental reactor commissioned by the army in the Idaho desert — Stationary Low-Power Reactor Number One — suffered a steam explosion and meltdown in January 1961, killing three operators in the only fatal reactor accident in US history.
