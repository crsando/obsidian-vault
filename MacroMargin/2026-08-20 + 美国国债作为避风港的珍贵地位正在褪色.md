## 美国国债作为避风港的珍贵地位正在褪色

## **美国国债作为避风港的珍贵地位正在褪色**

### 两项新研究发现，有迹象表明投资者不再因为国债的安全性而愿意接受低收益率

![Greg Ip](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c281cc90b14818da0c6d2e2f55bf148/3c281cc9-0b14-8164-9bc1-cfba26787795-4bbfc30fc8d6c772d35c334707ad60d6.png)

2026年8月20日 东部时间上午5:30

国债被用于为无数跨市场交易定价、对冲和提供抵押。Jeenah Moon/路透社

今年债券收益率的上行由众多因素驱动，从顽固的通胀到与人工智能相关的企业大量借贷。

然而，背后潜藏着一个更令人不安的可能性。长期作为全球首选“安全”资产的美国国债，看起来不再那么安全。相对于其他证券，其收益率已不再那么低，而在压力时刻，它们的表现也不像避风港。

主要原因是，自疫情以来，美国向市场投放了大量额外债务，[本周达到40万亿美元](https://www.wsj.com/economy/u-s-debt-just-topped-40-trillion-how-we-got-here-c1c6c9d2?mod=article_inline)，其中包括政府内部债务。没有迹象表明这股洪流会减弱。

美国[财政部周三的意外公告](https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/u-s-to-buy-back-more-longer-term-bonds-39524410?mod=article_inline)称将加大回购流动性较差的长债，这不过是治标不治本。此举虽然短期内能压降收益率，但从长远看，可能会因削弱美国在稳定性和可预测性方面的声誉而推高收益率。

美国国债长期以来在全球享有独特地位。它由美国无与伦比的经济和金融实力作为支撑，以世界首选储备货币计价，并受到强大而稳定的法律和制度监管，尤其是[美联储](https://www.wsj.com/topics/subject/federal-reserve)和财政部本身。

美国国债不仅仅为美国政府提供资金。它们是投资者和政府存放多余现金的地方，例如，用于捍卫本国货币。它们还被用于对无关市场的无数交易进行定价、对冲和抵押。

这种地位带来了“安全溢价”，使美国能够享有较低的日常借贷成本以及几乎无限的融资能力。

这些好处在全球金融危机之后尤为明显。美国借贷飙升，但收益率却下降。原因是：私人借贷崩溃，曾经被认为安全的证券（如AAA级抵押贷款）被证明并不安全。美联储和其他央行开始购买本国政府的债务。

这导致了麻省理工学院经济学家里卡多·卡瓦列罗所称的“安全资产短缺”：当投资者对超级安全资产的需求与有限供应相冲突时，收益率暴跌。

这些条件随着疫情而结束，当时通胀和利率上升，赤字飙升。在最近的一项研究中，卡瓦列罗发现，投资者并非因为美国国债非常诱人而接受较低的收益率，而是要求更高的收益率；安全溢价已变成吸收溢价。

卡瓦列罗推断这一点的一种方法是将美国国债收益率与互换利率进行比较，互换是用于管理利率风险的衍生品，可作为某种基准公司借贷利率。历史上，美国国债收益率低于互换利率。但最近，它们却更高。他认为，这反映出交易商现在在交易、仓储和分销债务方面承受着更大的压力。

另一个线索来自期限溢价，即投资者为持有长期债券而非波动性较小的国库券所要求的额外回报。这一期限溢价已在稳步上升。

将这两者结合起来，卡瓦列罗推断，从安全溢价转向吸收溢价，可以解释自2015年以来收益率约2.5个百分点涨幅中的约0.75个百分点。

斯坦福大学的汉诺·卢斯蒂格进行的另一项[研究](https://www.economicstrategygroup.org/publication/americas-risky-debt-what-markets-see-that-policymakers-dont/)得出了类似结论。从历史上看，即使在调整违约风险后，美国国债的收益率也低于AAA级公司债券，但自2022年以来，这一优势已经消失。在对冲成美元后，美国国债收益率也曾与其他主权债券相当或更低，但自疫情以来普遍更高。

这项为阿斯彭经济战略集团（我是其成员）准备的研究列出了种种迹象，表明投资者不再像过去那样在压力时期逃向美国国债的安全。当股市下跌时，收益率现在往往上升——例如，在去年特朗普总统的“解放日”关税之后。卢斯蒂格发现，赤字扩大的消息往往给收益率带来上行压力。他认为，所有这些都表明美国国债被视为不那么安全了。

对于这些发展，可能还有其他解释，例如与市场运作或通货膨胀有关。

![Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent in a blue suit and striped tie, flanked by two aides.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c281cc90b14818da0c6d2e2f55bf148/3c281cc9-0b14-815b-a8ed-ec39927e6bb5-ecc9e3adcc33a0ace9b734e0838571ea.jpg)

财政部长斯科特·贝森特 朱莉娅·德马雷·尼金森/美联社

这些动态不能完全归咎于特朗普，因为它们在他就职之前就已经在发生。但他也没有扭转这些趋势。早些时候，财政部长斯科特·贝森特表示，特朗普的优先事项是[降低债券收益率](https://www.wsj.com/economy/10-year-treasury-yields-bonds-trump-explained-d988bd4e?mod=article_inline)，因为债券收益率影响抵押贷款利率。他说，这将通过增加能源生产、人工智能驱动的生产率以及降低预算赤字来实现通胀下降，而2024财年的预算赤字为1.8万亿美元。

相反，伊朗战争推高了能源价格和通货膨胀。与此同时，国会预算办公室预计今年赤字将达到2.1万亿美元。占国内生产总值的比重，是贝森特曾经设想的3%目标的两倍。同时，根据巴克莱的数据，今年公司将以创纪录的1.9万亿美元发行投资级债券，其中很大一部分用于为人工智能建设融资。所有这些都推高了债券收益率，而不是使其下降。

因此，贝森特没有解决高收益率背后的基本面问题，而是转向干预债券市场本身。他没有扩大债券拍卖，而是用更多国库券为赤字融资，并暗示一些债券的拍卖实际上可能会缩减。他介入以推高日元，以阻止日本利率上升（并传导到美国），同时建议日本[从美联储借款](https://www.wsj.com/economy/central-banking/why-bessent-is-leaning-on-the-fed-to-help-prop-up-japans-currency-85794909?mod=article_inline)来为购买日元融资，而不出售美国国债。

自20世纪70年代以来，民主党和共和党领导的财政部都力求使债券发行“规律且可预测”，而不是择时发行。债券销售在季度再融资时详细公布。2024年启动的回购旨在改善交易清淡债券的流动性，同样也在同一时间安排。

贝森特也曾支持“规律且可预测”，但在上周三宣布回购时却偏离了这一原则，距离上一次季度再融资仅过去两周。

此次回购让投资者措手不及，从而实现了其短期目标：收益率下降。但这种进展可能无法持续。

如果发行和回购不再规律或可预测，债券可能会变得更加波动。加拿大皇家银行资本市场美国利率策略主管布莱克·格温表示，投资者可能会要求更高的收益率以“应对这种额外的不可预测性”。

通过从债券转向国库券，财政部面临更大的展期风险——即当这些债务需要再融资时，利率可能已经上升的风险。

然而，这可能是未来某位财政部长需要面对的问题。

## The Treasury Market’s Coveted Status as a Safe Haven Is Fading

## **The Treasury Market’s Coveted Status as a Safe Haven Is Fading**

### Two new studies find signs investors aren’t as willing to accept low yields because of Treasurys’ safety

By

Aug. 20, 2026 5:30 am ET

Treasurys are used to price, hedge and collateralize countless transactions in unrelated markets. Jeenah Moon/Reuters

This year’s upward march in bond yields has many drivers, from sticky inflation to heavy AI-linked corporate borrowing.

Lurking in the background, though, is a more troubling possibility. Treasurys, long the world’s preferred “safe” asset, are looking less safe. Relative to other securities, their yields are no longer quite so low, and in moments of stress, they don’t behave like a haven.

The main reason for this is that since the pandemic, the U.S. has flooded the market with additional debt, [reaching $40 trillion](https://www.wsj.com/economy/u-s-debt-just-topped-40-trillion-how-we-got-here-c1c6c9d2?mod=article_inline), including intragovernmental debt, this week. There is no sign of the flood abating.

The U.S. [Treasury’s surprise announcement Wednesday ](https://www.wsj.com/finance/investing/u-s-to-buy-back-more-longer-term-bonds-39524410?mod=article_inline)that it would boost buybacks of less-liquid longer-term debt simply treats the symptoms rather than the underlying cause. The move, while reducing yields in the short run, might yet raise them in the long run by eroding the U.S.’s reputation for stability and predictability.

Treasury debt has long enjoyed a unique status globally. It is backed by the unparalleled economic and financial strength of the United States, denominated in the world’s preferred reserve currency, and overseen by strong and stable laws and institutions, in particular the [Federal Reserve](https://www.wsj.com/topics/subject/federal-reserve) and the Treasury itself.

Treasurys don’t just fund the U.S. government. They are where investors and governments like to park extra cash, for example, to defend their currencies. They are used to price, hedge and collateralize countless transactions in unrelated markets.

This status brought “a safety premium,” conferring lower everyday borrowing costs for the U.S. and virtually unlimited financing capacity.

These benefits became especially apparent in the wake of the global financial crisis. U.S. borrowing soared, yet yields fell. The reason: Private borrowing collapsed, and securities once thought safe, such as triple-A rated mortgages, turned out not to be safe. The Fed and other central banks began buying their own governments’ debt.

This led to what Ricardo Caballero, an economist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, dubbed the “safe-asset shortage”: As investors’ demand for supersafe assets collided with a finite supply, yields plummeted.

Those conditions ended with the pandemic when inflation and interest rates rose and deficits shot up. In a recent study, Caballero finds that instead of investors accepting lower yields because Treasurys are so desirable, they are demanding higher yields; the safety premium has become an absorption premium.

One way Caballero infers this is by comparing Treasury yields to swaps, derivatives used to manage interest exposure that serve as a sort of benchmark corporate borrowing rate. Historically, Treasurys yielded less than swap rates. But recently, they have yielded more. He sees this as reflecting the greater strain dealers now bear to trade, warehouse and distribute debt.

Another clue comes from the term premium, the extra return investors demand to hold long-term bonds instead of less-volatile Treasury bills. That term premium has steadily marched higher.

Combining these two, Caballero infers that shift from a safety premium to an absorption premium explains about 0.75 percentage points of the roughly 2.5 percentage point rise in yields since 2015.

A separate [study](https://www.economicstrategygroup.org/publication/americas-risky-debt-what-markets-see-that-policymakers-dont/) by Hanno Lustig at Stanford University comes to similar conclusions. Historically, Treasurys yielded less than AAA-corporate bonds, even after adjusting for default risk, but since 2022, that advantage has disappeared. Treasury yields were also comparable to or lower than other sovereign bonds when hedged into dollars, but have been generally higher since the pandemic.

The study, prepared for the Aspen Economic Strategy Group (of which I am a member), listed signs that investors no longer flee to the safety of Treasurys during stress periods as they once did. When stocks go down, yields now tend to go up—for example, following President Trump’s “Liberation Day” tariffs last year. News of larger deficits tends to put upward pressure on yields, Lustig found. All of this, he argues, shows Treasurys are seen as less safe.

There could be other explanations for these developments, for example, related to market functioning or inflation.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent Julia Demaree Nikhinson/Associated Press

These dynamics cannot be attributed solely to Trump, since they were under way before he took office. But he also hasn’t reversed them. Early on, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Trump’s priority [was lower bond yields,](https://www.wsj.com/economy/10-year-treasury-yields-bonds-trump-explained-d988bd4e?mod=article_inline) which influence mortgage rates. This, he said, would be achieved by lowering inflation with increased energy production, AI-driven productivity, and a lower budget deficit, which was $1.8 trillion in fiscal 2024.

Instead, the Iran war has driven up energy prices and inflation. Meanwhile, the Congressional Budget Office projects the deficit will reach $2.1 trillion this year. As a share of gross domestic, that is double the 3% target Bessent once envisioned. At the same time, corporations will issue a record $1.9 trillion in investment-grade debt this year, much of it to finance the AI build-out, according to Barclays. All of this has pushed bond yields higher instead of lower.

So rather than address the fundamentals behind higher yields, Bessent has turned to fiddling with the bond market itself. Rather than expand bond auctions, he is financing the deficit with more Treasury bills and hinted that auctions of some bonds might actually shrink. He intervened to boost the Japanese yen to keep Japanese interest rates from rising (and spilling into the U.S.) while suggesting Japan [borrow from the Fed](https://www.wsj.com/economy/central-banking/why-bessent-is-leaning-on-the-fed-to-help-prop-up-japans-currency-85794909?mod=article_inline) to finance yen buying without selling Treasurys.

Since the 1970s, Democratic and Republican Treasury Departments alike, rather than time the market, sought to make debt issuance “regular and predictable.” Bond sales were detailed at quarterly refundings. Buybacks, launched in 2024 to improve liquidity in little-traded issues, were similarly scheduled at the same time.

Bessent has also endorsed “regular and predictable,” yet departed from it in announcing the buyback Wednesday just two weeks after the last quarterly refunding.

The buyback caught investors offside and thus achieved its short-term goal: yields fell. But such progress might not be sustained.

If issuance and buybacks are no longer regular or predictable, bonds might become more volatile. Investors might demand a higher yield to “account for this extra unpredictability,” said Blake Gwinn, head of U.S. rates strategy at RBC Capital Markets.

By shifting from bonds to bills, Treasury faces more rollover risk—the chance that interest rates will have risen when that debt must be refinanced.

That, however, might be a problem for a future Treasury secretary.
