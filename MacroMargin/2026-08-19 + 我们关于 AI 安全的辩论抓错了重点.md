### 中文译文

## 我们关于 AI 安全的辩论抓错了重点

中国对开源人工智能的拥抱让硅谷面临激烈竞争。这也成为北京的一大影响力来源，吸引了国际开发者，并成为习近平主席倡导的与全球南方合作以及对下一场科技革命发挥影响力的主张的基石。

但随着开放模型[能力不断增强](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/how-far-behind-the-frontier-are-leading-open-weight-models-on-cyber)，北京正在努力保持透明度的好处，而不让这些优势变为累赘。

各国政府不能掉以轻心。一次严重的人工智能安全故障很容易跨越国界。这意味着从杭州到旧金山的主要实验室都有充分动力，在可能拖慢全球人工智能发展的灾难发生*之前*抢先应对这些风险——正如日本福岛核灾难促使德国退出核电一样。

一个常见的说法（主要来自大型闭源模型公司内部）是，开放系统本质上更不安全。这种想法在纸面上说得通：一旦模型权重公开，开发者就失去了对其使用方式的控制。然而，[许多备受瞩目的人工智能滥用事件](https://florianbrand.com/posts/open-model-safety)却发生在护栏失灵的闭源模型中。这可能是因为闭源模型性能仍然更优，或者是因为恶意行为者发现它们比获取部署开源替代品所需的硬件和专业知识更容易使用。无论哪种情况，都意味着人工智能安全不能简化为仅仅围绕开放与封闭的辩论。

这种二元划分还掩盖了重要的细微差别，尤其是在最直接的威胁之一：网络安全方面。开放系统可用于强化防御，抵御人工智能驱动的攻击者。在一次测试中，一个失控的 OpenAI 代理[入侵了 Hugging Face](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/openai-says-its-ai-used-for-unprecedented-hugging-face-breach)，该平台使用了总部位于北京的智谱的开源模型作为其防御响应的一部分。Hugging Face 联合创始人克莱芒·德朗格（Clement Delangue）在那次事件后[表示](https://x.com/ClementDelangue/status/2083908468285620415)，人工智能将“让世界更安全，而不是更不安全，就像大多数重大技术一样。”他提出的方案是，用最好的工具武装防御者，**尤其是开放模型**，同时实施更有意义的惩罚措施，以威慑人工智能辅助的网络攻击，并强制要求披露事件和共享细节。这是一个很有前景的起点。

智谱周五发布了 GLM-5.3，称这是其在网络安全任务方面能力最强的模型。尽管智谱是开源 AI 的主要公开倡导者，但该公司计划在经过严格的安全评估后分阶段开放更广泛的访问权限。这是否意味着中国企业终于认为安全风险太大，不适合开放开发？很可能不是。在承认明显的双重用途风险的同时，该公司[将 GLM-5.3 定位](https://x.com/Zai_org/status/2088280509474320693)为“开源盾牌”，称该模型可以帮助防御者更早发现弱点、验证风险并加快修复。这是明智的预防措施。但仅靠一家公司无法解决这个问题。

禁止开放模型也不是那么简单。除了不可行且会损害美国开发者之外，这并不能[消除威胁](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17413)，反而可能将滥用行为推向更隐蔽的地方，同时限制防御工具的广泛分发。更不用说将权力集中在少数几家强大的实验室手中了。

一个常见的误解是，开放模型意味着任何人都可以自行运行一个强大的人工智能系统。但部署其中能力最强的模型需要大量的计算能力，这促使许多用户转向云服务提供商和托管服务商。政策制定者应与这些服务商合作，监控可疑活动并标记潜在的滥用行为。

华盛顿[据报道](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/china-s-open-weight-models-to-be-spared-us-tests-us-firms-told)将开放模型排除在特朗普政府关于发布前审查先进人工智能的框架之外。这是一个错误。开放系统可能更难控制，但这使得测试和理解风险更加重要，而非更不重要。滥用监管的困难不是对能力视而不见的理由。

网络安全的警笛声最为刺耳。即使立法者无法就更广泛的人工智能监管达成一致，他们现在也必须专注于加强防御。政策制定者应将其视为关键基础设施，而不是让每家公司或机构自行应对。这意味着共享情报、标准化最佳实践，以及类似政府规划自然灾害那样的集中化准备。

与此同时，工业界和政府需要为技术保障和更广泛的人工智能安全研究提供更多资金和支持。一些[研究](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06601)表明，从训练集中移除危险信息（例如围绕生物威胁的信息）可能有助于防止开放模型被滥用（尽管尚不清楚这些方法在多大程度上能应对其他危害，例如网络风险）。行业的竞争性似乎已将安全变成了事后考虑。这是不可持续的。

在当今相互不信任的环境中，美中全面合作不太可能。但一些对话是必要的。正如卡内基国际和平基金会的马特·希恩所[写](https://carnegieendowment.org/emissary/2026/07/ai-safety-parallel-us-china)，如果没有测试或护栏的信息，两国领导人都将做出最坏的假设。他指出，下个月特朗普与习近平的峰会可能是一个好的开端，前提是人工智能安全差距被视为共同威胁，而不是其他事情的讨价还价筹码。

如果我们把这些风险简化为开放与封闭实验室之间的竞争辩论——或者将不作为视为全球竞赛的代价——那么所有各方都将蒙受损失。

### English Original

## We’re Having the Wrong Debate About AI Safety

China’s embrace of open-source artificial intelligence is giving Silicon Valley a run for its money. It’s also become a major source of clout for Beijing, luring international developers and emerging as a cornerstone of President Xi Jinping’s pitch for collaboration with the Global South and influence over the next tech revolution.

But as open models [grow more capable](https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/how-far-behind-the-frontier-are-leading-open-weight-models-on-cyber), Beijing is scrambling to preserve the benefits of transparency without letting those same advantages become liabilities.

Governments can’t afford to look away. A serious AI safety failure could easily spill across borders. That means major labs from Hangzhou to San Francisco have every incentive to get ahead of these risks *before* a catastrophe occurs that could slow AI development globally — the same way the disaster in Fukushima, Japan, pushed Germany’s retreat from nuclear power.

A persistent refrain, largely from people inside big closed-model companies, is that open systems are inherently less safe. The idea makes sense on paper: Once model weights are published, developers lose control over how they are used. Yet [many of the high-profile abuses](https://florianbrand.com/posts/open-model-safety) of AI have occurred with closed models with broken guardrails. That may be because they still perform better, or because bad actors find them easier to use versus acquiring the hardware and know-how needed to deploy open alternatives. Either way, it means that AI safety can’t be flattened to a debate solely around open versus closed.

That binary also obscures important nuances, especially around one of the most immediate threats: cybersecurity. Open systems can be used for hardening defenses against AI-powered attackers. When a rogue OpenAI agent [hacked Hugging Face](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-21/openai-says-its-ai-used-for-unprecedented-hugging-face-breach) during testing, the platform used an open model from Beijing-based Zhipu as part of its defensive response. Clement Delangue, Hugging Face’s co-founder, [argued](https://x.com/ClementDelangue/status/2083908468285620415) after the scare that AI will “make the world safer, not less safe, just as most major technologies have.” His prescription was to equip defenders with the best tools,** **“especially open models,” while imposing more meaningful penalties to disincentivize AI-assisted cyberattacks, as well as mandatory incident disclosure and detail sharing. That’s a promising way to start.

Zhipu on Friday unveiled GLM-5.3, which the company said is its most capable model for cybersecurity tasks. Despite being a major public champion of open AI, it plans to allow broader access in stages, following rigorous safety evaluations. Does that mean Chinese firms are finally deciding safety risks are too great for open development? Probably not. While acknowledging clear dual-use risks, the [company is framing](https://x.com/Zai_org/status/2088280509474320693) GLM-5.3 as an “open shield,” saying the model can help defenders identify weaknesses earlier, validate risks and accelerate remediation. It’s taking sensible precautions. But one company alone cannot solve the problem.

It’s not as simple as banning access to open models, either. In addition to being unfeasible and hurting American developers, this doesn’t [eliminate the threat](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.17413), and has the potential to drive misuse further underground while limiting broader distribution of defensive tools. Not to mention concentrating power in the hands of a few powerful labs.

A common misconception is that open models mean anyone can instantly run a powerful AI system on their own. But deploying the most capable of them requires substantial computing power, pushing many users toward cloud and hosting providers. Policymakers should work with these services to monitor for suspicious activity and flag potential abuses.

Washington is [reportedly](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-05/china-s-open-weight-models-to-be-spared-us-tests-us-firms-told) excluding open models from the Trump administration’s framework for reviewing advanced AI before release. That is a mistake. Open systems may be harder to control, but that makes testing and understanding risks more important, not less. Difficulty policing abuse is not a reason to turn a blind eye to capabilities.

The cybersecurity sirens are blaring the loudest. Even if lawmakers can’t agree on broader regulation for AI, they must focus now on hardening defenses. Policymakers should treat this as critical infrastructure, rather than leaving every company or agency to fend for itself. That means shared intelligence, standardizing best practices, and centralized preparedness akin to the way governments plan for natural disasters.

At the same time, much more funding and support from industry and government is needed for research on technical safeguards and AI safety more broadly. Some [studies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06601) suggest that removing hazardous information, such as around biological threats, from training sets may help prevent abuse of open models (though it’s unclear how broadly such methods will work to address other hazards, such as cyber risks). The competitive nature of the industry has seemingly turned safety into an afterthought. This isn’t sustainable.

Full US-China cooperation is unlikely in today’s environment of mutual distrust. But some dialogue is necessary. As Matt Sheehan of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace [writes](https://carnegieendowment.org/emissary/2026/07/ai-safety-parallel-us-china), without information on testing or guardrails, leaders in both countries will assume the worst. Next month’s summit between Trump and Xi could be a good place to start, he notes, provided AI safety gaps are treated as a mutual threat, not bargaining leverage for something else.

If we flatten these risks into a debate about competition between open and closed labs — or excuse inaction as the price of a global race — all sides stand to lose.
