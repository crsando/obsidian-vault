## 唐纳德·特朗普对加拿大的欺凌无休无止

## 唐纳德·特朗普对加拿大的欺凌无休无止

### 加拿大人开始反对马克·卡尼的务实回应

戈迪·豪国际大桥是一座长2.5公里的警示碑，警示讨好唐纳德·特朗普的危险。加拿大为这座横跨底特律河通往美国的[闪亮新桥](https://www.economist.com/the-world-in-brief/2026/07/27/828e8900-bb22-495d-86a3-1db938827ac7)支付了全部64亿加元（46亿美元）的费用。随后，美国总统威胁要阻止其开通达数月之久，直到美国获得过路费收入的一部分才作罢。这座桥（如图）终于在7月底通车。加拿大人对马克·卡尼关于这次勒索的含糊解释感到难堪，但更糟的还在后面。特朗普先生以宣布对从北方邻国进口到美国的一系列商品征收50%的新关税来奖励加拿大总理的顺从。

欧洲和世界其他地区应引以为戒。特朗普先生有先以武器攻击加拿大、随后又将武器对准其他人的先例，这些武器包括[关税](https://www.economist.com/united-states/2026/07/22/the-tariffs-that-just-wont-die)和领土威胁。这一次，如果新关税在8月19日生效，加拿大将成为首个根据《斯穆特-霍利关税法》第338条被征收关税的国家。该法于1930年通过，允许总统对被认为的贸易歧视作出回应。与2月被美国最高法院否决的紧急关税不同，该法案下的关税无需国会批准。华盛顿贸易咨询公司Rideau Potomac Strategy Group的负责人埃里克·米勒表示：“这看起来很像唐纳德·特朗普贸易政策的未来，因为它给了他灵活性。”

特朗普先生正利用这一自由对价值200亿美元的加拿大出口商品征收关税，从冰球棒到蜂蜜。这些关税将叠加在已经阻碍加拿大汽车、钢铁、铝、木材和铜行业的旧关税之上。关键在于，与2025年实施的紧急关税不同，新关税也适用于《美墨加协定》（USMCA）这一北美自由贸易协定所涵盖的商品。

卡尼先生似乎认为他有谈判空间。他最好是对的。他在2025年大选中意外获胜，此前他承诺要对抗特朗普先生。卡尼先生承诺达成一项比USMCA“更好的协议”。自那以来，让步一个接一个：加拿大放弃了对主要是美国社交媒体巨头的数字服务税，然后取消了大部分报复性关税，以及对Netflix等流媒体服务商征收的另一种税。特朗普先生的回应是漠不关心。“我们不需要他们，”特朗普先生于7月28日在与USMCA另外两个缔约方墨西哥和加拿大谈判前表示。“协议对他们很重要。对我们不重要。”

这种欺凌似乎正在国内伤害卡尼先生，尤其是戈迪·豪大桥的羞辱。加拿大在2012年同意支付大桥的建设费用，条件是在大桥收回成本之前，加拿大将获得所有过路费收入。在特朗普先生要求并达成新协议后，卡尼先生一再声称加拿大的成本仍将优先得到支付。美国商务部长霍华德·卢特尼克在推特上得意洋洋的帖子表明事实并非如此。事实上，在桥梁运营的头15年，美国政府现在将获得净收入的一半，扣除运营成本后但在偿还债务之前，这一让步将使加拿大损失约3亿加元。根据民调机构Angus Reid于7月26日公布的研究，对卡尼先生能与特朗普先生达成好协议表示有信心的加拿大人比例在4月至7月间下降了8个百分点。

同一项民调显示，加拿大人仍然对特朗普的关税感到愤怒；几乎三分之二的人希望卡尼先生以自己的关税进行反击。但这只会加剧加拿大的痛苦。“你必须意识到，在这里反击欺凌者也会重创加拿大人民的要害，”密歇根大学的贾斯汀·沃尔弗斯说。卡尼先生已派遣谈判代表返回华盛顿，指示其加强旨在延长USMCA的讨论。特朗普政府在7月初拒绝这样做，但协议仍可能随时达成。卡尼先生已排除在任何报复行动中打出加拿大最强大的牌——能源出口。

1930年，加拿大没有表现出卡尼那样的克制。它在《斯姆特-霍利关税法》成为法律之前就先发制人地进行了报复，对美国进口商品加征了自己的关税。许多其他国家也纷纷报复。四年之内，世界贸易的名义价值下降了三分之二，大萧条也到了最低点。

因此，尽管令人不悦，加拿大人仍应希望卡尼先生（Mr Carney）的决心能够坚持下去。而其他所有人也都应希望，当特朗普先生（Mr Trump）将他的新关税指向他们时，其他世界领导人也能效仿。

## Donald Trump’s bullying of Canada is endless

## Donald Trump’s bullying of Canada is endless

### Canadians are starting to turn against Mark Carney’s pragmatic response

The Gordie Howe International Bridge is a 2.5km-long monument to the perils of appeasing Donald Trump. Canada footed the entire C$6.4bn ($4.6bn) bill for the [gleaming new span](https://www.economist.com/the-world-in-brief/2026/07/27/828e8900-bb22-495d-86a3-1db938827ac7) over the Detroit river to the United States. America’s president then threatened for months to block its opening, relenting only when the United States got a cut of the toll revenues. The bridge (pictured) finally saw traffic at the end of July. Canadians cringed at Mark Carney’s evasive explanation for the shakedown, but worse was to come. Mr Trump rewarded the Canadian prime minister’s compliance by announcing new tariffs of 50% on a range of goods imported into the United States from its northern neighbour.

Europe and the rest of the world should take heed. Mr Trump has form in attacking Canada first with weapons which he subsequently turns on others, both [tariffs](https://www.economist.com/united-states/2026/07/22/the-tariffs-that-just-wont-die) and threats to territory. This time, if the new tariffs come into force on August 19th, Canada will be the first country ever to be subjected to tariffs under Section 338 of the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, a law passed in 1930 that allows the president to respond to perceived trade discrimination. Tariffs under that act, unlike the emergency duties struck down by the US Supreme Court in February, need no congressional approval. “This looks a lot like the future of Donald Trump’s trade policy because it gives him flexibility,” says Eric Miller, head of the Rideau Potomac Strategy Group, a trade consultancy in Washington.

Mr Trump is using that freedom to place tariffs on $20bn-worth of Canadian exports, from hockey sticks to honey. These would pile up next to older duties that have hobbled the Canadian car, steel, aluminium, wood and copper industries. Crucially, and unlike the emergency tariffs imposed in 2025, the new tariffs also apply to goods covered by the USMCA, the North American free-trade pact.

Mr Carney seems to think he has room to negotiate. He had better be right. His surprise election win in 2025 came after he promised to stand up to Mr Trump. Mr Carney committed to delivering “an even better deal” than the USMCA. One concession has followed another since then: Canada abandoned its digital-services tax on mostly American social-media giants, then dropped most of its retaliatory tariffs and a different tax on streamers such as Netflix. Mr Trump’s response has been indifference. “We don’t need them,” Mr Trump said on July 28th ahead of negotiations with the two other parties to USMCA, Mexico and Canada. “The deal is important for them. It’s not important for us.”

This bullying appears to be hurting Mr Carney domestically, particularly the Gordie Howe mortification. Canada agreed to pay for the construction of the bridge in 2012 on the condition that it would take all tolling revenue until its costs were recovered. After Mr Trump demanded and received a new deal, Mr Carney claimed repeatedly that Canada’s costs would still be paid first. A crowing tweet from Howard Lutnick, the US commerce secretary, made it clear that was not true. In fact, for the first 15 years of the bridge’s operation the US government will now take half of net revenues, after operating costs but before debt repayment, a concession that will cost Canada some C$300m. The share of Canadians who say they feel confident that Mr Carney can land a good deal with Mr Trump fell by eight percentage points between April and July, according to research published on July 26th by Angus Reid, a pollster.

That same poll shows that Canadians remain outraged by Mr Trump’s tariffs; almost two-thirds want Mr Carney to strike back with tariffs of his own. But that would only compound Canadian misery. “You’ve got to realise that punching the bully back here is also going to punch the Canadian people in the guts,” says Justin Wolfers of the University of Michigan. Mr Carney has sent negotiators back to Washington with instructions to intensify discussions aimed at extending USMCA. The Trump administration declined to do this in early July, but agreement could still come at any time. Mr Carney has ruled out playing Canada’s strongest card, energy exports, in any retaliation.

In 1930 Canada did not show Carney levels of forbearance. It retaliated against Smoot-Hawley pre-emptively, launching tariffs of its own on American imports before the bill had even become law. Many other countries retaliated too. Within four years the nominal value of world trade had fallen by two-thirds and the Depression had hit its nadir.

And so, unpleasant as it is, Canadians should hope that Mr Carney’s resolve holds. And everyone else should hope that other world leaders follow suit when Mr Trump turns his new tariffs upon them.
