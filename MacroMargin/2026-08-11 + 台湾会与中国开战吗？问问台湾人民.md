### 中文译文

## 台湾会与中国开战吗？问问台湾人民

### 五位台湾人谈北京阴影下的未来

台北一座纪念前领导人蒋介石的纪念馆外的广场。

| 摄影：Sean Marc Lee 为《华尔街日报》拍摄

Aug. 10, 2026 2:41 pm ET

[台湾](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/taiwan)，这座位于美中超级大国竞争中心的民主岛屿，其人民正面临关于未来的关键抉择。

这个问题迫在眉睫：台湾是应该与中国走得更近，还是站起来反对北京对这座岛屿的征服企图？

对一些人来说，答案取决于他们只认同自己是台湾人还是也认同自己是中国人。另一些人则是出于维持自身生活方式的愿望。而所有人都是中国行动的目标——中国以军事威胁和其他手段，试图说服该岛的2300万人民向北京屈服。

《华尔街日报》采访了数位年龄、背景和所在地各不相同的台湾民众，了解他们的立场——包括他们是否信任中国人、美国人，还是只信任自己。以下就是他们的回答。

#### **训练有素，随时待命**

![Cony Hsieh wearing a "Fight for Taiwan" shirt in an army supply store.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-8102-bc66-d0ea16566735-02716eaf54cf6e71fbcf68c03efa2976.jpg)

Cony Hsieh在台湾高雄市的一家军需品商店。

Cony Hsieh已准备好与中国作战。

Hsieh在台湾的主要军官培训学校获得学位，并在军中服役六年，还是一名受过训练的预备役成员。

这在台湾并不寻常，因为台湾征兵面临挑战，义务兵役仅限于男性，且仅为一年。现年33岁、自认性别流动的Hsieh自愿服役。

“如果中国真的发动攻击，且[军队]需要我回到前线尽一份力并抵抗，我心里毫无疑虑，我会这么做，”Hsieh说。

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-81ce-bfc7-e071a6c553f6-32924caa646c3a047cb5228787e6fcfa.jpg)

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-8130-91bc-e00043f56493-f4afdaa25087e20cf244700b23fe8ecc.jpg)

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-816c-9e94-e73cef4d4c88-5981e86ebe00061d75410ac1930f5c96.jpg)

除了从事性别研究研究员的工作外，Hsieh还主持一个关于加强台湾防务的播客。

“我同时推动全民防卫与民防，讨论我们如何反制中国的渗透与虚假信息，捍卫民主，并在全台建立社会韧性，”谢（Hsieh）说。

谢认为发展自卫能力是必要的，他怀疑特朗普总统是否会派遣美军来保卫台湾，抵御中国的入侵。

与迫在眉睫的战争相比，谢更担心的是，2028年台湾总统选举可能使主张加强国防的执政党民主进步党下台，并将总统职位交给主张与北京改善关系的反对党国民党（KMT）。

“那基本上就是把台湾拱手送给中国，”谢说。

#### **在独裁统治下成长**

![Dov Lin at the Jingmei White Terror Memorial Park.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-812e-a33c-cfd53a36fe1a-3899c2ab75a08c893a5a520f772db83b.jpg)

多夫·林（Dov Lin）参观了台湾被称为“白色恐怖”的军事独裁统治时期受害者的纪念碑。

多夫·林和许多其他人一样，决心维护台湾的民主。

这不仅仅是因为这位54岁的老人害怕共产党接管政权——尽管他说他会为阻止这种情况而战。更是因为他在毛泽东的军队将国民党政府赶出大陆后，亲历了国民党施加的军事独裁下的镇压。

在学生时代，20世纪70年代和80年代戒严时期，林先生和他那一代人被教导要认同自己是中国人。据他理解，台湾是一个大中国的一部分，其政治中心恰好位于距离大陆100英里的一座岛上。

然后，在1980年代末，军事统治开始让位于民主。

“社会开放了，大家开始在公开场合谈论台湾意识，团结在台湾身份认同之下——就这样，它确立了，”林先生说。

林的叔公在被称为“白色恐怖”的国民党镇压时期被监禁。几十年来，这段遭遇是家庭禁忌，林先生直到晚年才得知此事。

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-81a6-8ebd-c753c6d0ca5d-f16257117683596baca3f22f7f537773.jpg)

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-8103-8b0d-d265f057df3d-d57c51a02a0cedfabb37a4f061c0b554.jpg)

林先生在纪念碑的前政治犯名单上找到了他叔公的名字。

“我认为自己有点像台湾大中华民族主义的遗物，”他说。“我们这一代差不多是最后一批了。”

林先生在台北经营一家小型IT服务公司。他说他不反对与大陆的商务和文化交流，但他补充说，他不会冒险去访问。

他认为台湾应该维持现状——保持民主和事实上的独立，不做出任何可能引发中国大陆入侵的改变。

如果中国真的入侵，林先生表示他会“全力以赴”保卫这座岛屿。

### “如果这是我们称之为家的土地，我们为什么要选择逃跑或投降？”

#### **‘我不能称自己为中国人’**

![Artist Hsu Mei-chih peers from behind a translucent fabric embroidered with abstract blue designs.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-8109-9e0f-cb2e08ebbfe3-3ce94f05bf92fd308755213bc0f1745c.jpg)

艺术家许美智在她位于家中的工作室里。

一个艺术节上的顿悟帮助27岁的艺术家许美智（其母亲来自中国）确定了自己作为台湾人的身份。

这件事发生在台湾的绿岛，她父亲的出生地，也是国民党关押并处决数千名政治犯的臭名昭著的地点。

该艺术节致力于用艺术探索白色恐怖。许美智开始更好地理解台湾走向民主的斗争，并深受感动。

“我不想忘记那段历史，”她说，“我爱这片土地，我想为之贡献，这就是为什么我把自己视为台湾人。”

许（Hsu）认为中国和台湾应当保持区分。作为一名艺术家，她探索了支离破碎的家庭历史所带来的经历。

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-81e7-9c60-c9e8960f15b8-fd5a6777c8acb5b1e01f4f3e1d7cfcd3.jpg)

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-811b-b27b-d43fd21b006a-829b27c91453b793fb98ca9860af19fb.jpg)

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-815a-98a2-fd8cd3de3cc1-9158dbd2307fb6f12a39888064c28a4e.jpg)

许的作品探索了她家庭复杂的历史，以及这段历史如何与她自身身份相关联。

她的母亲难以理解她为何拒绝承认中国人的身份。

“我不能称自己是中国人，因为我对那个地方不太了解，”许说。“我不会说他们那些方言，但[我会说台湾话](https://www.wsj.com/world/asia/chinese-pressure-fuels-an-unlikely-language-revival-in-taiwan-11640178003?mod=article_inline)。”

尽管如此，她仍希望能够去探望住在港口城市青岛的外祖父母，那是她母亲的故乡。

“我们是家人，”她曾这样告诉她的中国亲戚，“但这并不意味着我们来自同一个国家。”

#### **创建“中华共同体”**

![Ben Chen at the Hakka Cultural District in Hsinchu, Taiwan.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-817b-bcfc-c68721ca0bc9-df22ca930411d360a2ab38b40bbdb26d.jpg)

本·陈（Ben Chen）参观了位于台湾北部新竹的一个以客家文化为主题的园区。

63岁的陈本（Ben Chen）深情地回忆起1980年代末在阿拉斯加与中国大陆同学的相遇。陈本来自台湾——他的父亲曾参加中国内战——他说自己起初害怕他们，视他们为“共匪”。

在了解他们之后，他发现自己和他们一起分享饺子。

“尽管我们有意识形态分歧，以及多年分离积累起来的猜疑……我们说着同样的语言，有着相同的风俗，”陈本说。“到了晚上，大家就一起开心地喝酒开玩笑。”

带着这段经历，水土保持工程师陈本对当今的紧张局势提出了一个解决方案：建立“中华联邦”，让中国大陆、台湾、香港和澳门生活在同一个屋檐下。台湾将拥有自己的国旗、军队和外交地位，包括与其它成员一起在联合国拥有席位。

联邦制可能不太现实，但像台湾的一些人一样，陈本认为同时认同多个身份是可能的。

部分原因在于，他来自台湾南部的母亲是客家人，客家人早在17世纪就开始从大陆迁徙而来。

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-812a-baff-d9fbc30bc25c-fd957438d8e41599656e6f478b6d1c1b.jpg)

![图片](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-81b2-bc15-e31aaa7cb905-5d2261a28a26f7545a5b06757af0845d.jpg)

陈本在参观保存了数百年客家历史的文化区时，手持一张他在中国的祖籍村庄地图。

“在台湾长大，我是台湾人。但我也中国人——我父亲来自中国大陆，我们双方有着相同的语言和文化遗产。”

陈还认为，当华盛顿在1979年正式放弃承认台湾时，美国就失去了对台湾事务发言权的道德立场。

此外，陈表示，战争不是一个选项。他说，既然中国是一个现代化的全球超级大国，如果台湾将中国视为敌人，那将注定灭亡。

#### **以谦逊求和平**

![Eric Li crouching with an ornate decorative tile pattern in Daliang Ecological Park, Tainan, Taiwan.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-813a-b00d-c4137b5c03de-d8375bd5da85ebdf8feb250e44a70705.jpg)

Eric Li说，他这一代人倾向于维持台湾的现状。

25岁的Eric Li表示，如果战争爆发，他不认识任何会冲上战场的人。相反，他说，“每个人都说他们会躲起来，躲起来，躲起来。”

这并不意味着他在台湾民主时代出生的这一代人看不到保护自己生活方式的需要。

“现在的年轻人更倾向于维持现状，因为没有人想要战争，”他说，“每个人都只想和平地生活，快乐地工作。”

李在台北一所大学学习政治学，但为了避免冲突，他不与朋友们谈论政治，鉴于台湾政坛的激烈对立。

但当被追问时，李显然有自己的看法。他说他珍视台湾的选举和开放的经济体制，这些自由在大陆是不存在的。

![Eric Li stands at The Spring, an urban lagoon in Tainan, Taiwan.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b981cc90b148190ad39ffdee0e88a92/3b981cc9-0b14-81ec-b49f-ceb1d92b7770-05749dcd6f2c52cf07a4248188718570.jpg)

李在他的家乡台南参观了一个由旧商场改造的公共空间。

他还支持以更友好的方式处理与北京的关系。李说，他认为台湾应该学习中国的成功经验，而不是专注于威胁。

李曾多次前往大陆，他对中国的基础设施以及在无人机和机器人领域的技术进步印象深刻，在这些领域，台湾更多地是追随者而非创新者。

他还认为中国太强大，不容忽视。维持和平可能需要对北京做出一些象征性让步，例如台湾在奥运会上以“中华台北”的名义参赛，而不是坚持使用其官方名称“中华民国”。

“对我来说，享有一些好处而吃点小亏完全没问题，”他说，“这又不是真的损失什么。”

## Will Taiwan Fight China? Just Ask Its People

## **Will Taiwan Fight China? Just Ask Its People**

### Five Taiwanese discuss their future under the shadow of Beijing

The plaza outside a Taipei memorial to former leader Chiang Kai-shek.

By

| Photography by Sean Marc Lee for WSJ

Aug. 10, 2026 2:41 pm ET

The people of [Taiwan](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/taiwan), the island democracy at the center of the U.S.-China superpower rivalry, are facing a critical calculation about their future.

The question looms: Should Taiwan get closer to China or stand up against Beijing’s push to conquer the island?

The answer for some is shaped by whether they see themselves as Taiwanese only or Chinese as well. Some are driven by a wish to preserve their way of life. All are targets of a campaign by China, which uses military threats and other means to convince the island’s 23 million people to yield to Beijing.

The Wall Street Journal asked a handful of Taiwanese citizens of varied ages, backgrounds and locations in Taiwan where they stand—including whether they trust the Chinese, the Americans or themselves alone. Here is what they said.

---

#### **Trained and ready**

Cony Hsieh at an army supply store in the Taiwanese city of Kaohsiung.

Cony Hsieh is ready to fight China.

Hsieh earned a degree at Taiwan’s primary officer-training school, served in the military for six years and is a trained member of the reserves.

That is unusual in Taiwan, where recruitment is a challenge and mandatory service is limited to one year, for men only. Hsieh, who is 33 years old and identifies as gender-fluid, volunteered for service.

“If China actually attacks and [the army] needs me back on the front line to do my part and resist, there is zero doubt in my mind I’d do it,” said Hsieh.

In addition to a job as a researcher in gender studies, Hsieh hosts a podcast on building up Taiwan’s defenses.

“I promote both national and civil defense, discussing how we can counter Chinese infiltration and disinformation, safeguard democracy and build social resilience across Taiwan,” said Hsieh.

Hsieh sees developing self-defense as a necessity, doubtful that President Trump would send U.S. forces to defend Taiwan against a Chinese invasion.

Hsieh is less worried about imminent war than about the possibility that the 2028 presidential election in Taiwan could knock out the ruling Democratic Progressive Party, which supports stronger defense, and deliver the presidency to the opposition Kuomintang, or KMT, which advocates better relations with Beijing.

“That would basically be handing Taiwan to China on a silver platter,” Hsieh said.

---

#### **Coming of age in a dictatorship**

Dov Lin visits a memorial to victims of Taiwan’s era of military dictatorship known as the White Terror.

Dov Lin, like many others, is determined to preserve Taiwan’s democracy.

That isn’t just because the 54-year-old fears a Communist takeover—though he says he would fight to prevent one. It is because he experienced repression under the military dictatorship imposed by the Kuomintang after Mao Zedong’s army drove the KMT government from the mainland.

During his school years, under martial law in the 1970s and ’80s, Lin and others of his generation were taught to identify themselves as Chinese. As he understood it, Taiwan was part of a greater China whose political center happened to be an island 100 miles off the mainland.

Then, in the late 1980s, military rule began to give way to democracy.

“Society opened up and everyone started speaking out in public about Taiwanese consciousness, rallying behind a Taiwanese identity—and boom, it was established,” Lin said.

Lin’s great uncle was imprisoned during the regime of Kuomintang repression known as the White Terror. For decades, the ordeal was a family taboo, something Lin only learned about later in his life.

Lin found his great uncle’s name on the memorial’s list of former political prisoners.

“I see myself as a bit of a relic of Greater Chinese nationalism in Taiwan,” he said. “Our generation is pretty much the last of us.”

Lin runs a small IT service company in Taipei. He said he doesn’t oppose business and cultural exchanges with the mainland, though he adds that he wouldn’t risk visiting.

He believes Taiwan should maintain its current status—preserving democracy and de facto independence without making any changes that would trigger a Chinese invasion.

If China does invade, Lin says he would “fight tooth and nail” to defend the island.

### “If this is the land we call home, why should we choose to run or surrender?”

---

#### **‘I can’t call myself Chinese’**

Artist Hsu Mei-chih in her home studio.

An epiphany at an arts festival helped 27-year-old artist Hsu Mei-chih, whose mother is from China, define her identity as Taiwanese.

It happened on Taiwan’s Green Island, her father’s birthplace and the notorious site where the Kuomintang held and executed thousands of political prisoners.

The festival was dedicated to using art to explore the White Terror. Hsu began to better understand Taiwan’s struggle toward democracy, and she was deeply moved.

“I don’t want to forget that history,” she said. “I love this land and I want to contribute to it, which is why I see myself as Taiwanese.”

Hsu believes China and Taiwan should remain distinct. As an artist, she explores the experience of a fragmented family history.

Hsu’s work explores her family’s complicated history and how it relates to her own identity.

Her mother struggles to understand why she rejected a Chinese identity.

“I can’t call myself Chinese because I don’t know that place that well,” Hsu said. “I don’t speak their dialects, but [I speak Taiwanese](https://www.wsj.com/world/asia/chinese-pressure-fuels-an-unlikely-language-revival-in-taiwan-11640178003?mod=article_inline).”

Still, she wants to be able to visit her grandparents in the port city of Qingdao, her mother’s hometown.

“We are family,” she once told her Chinese relatives, “but that doesn’t mean we are from the same country.”

---

#### **Creating a ‘Chinese Commonwealth’**

Ben Chen visits a cultural district dedicated to the Hakka people, in Hsinchu, northern Taiwan.

Ben Chen, 63, fondly recalls an encounter with mainland-Chinese classmates in Alaska in the late 1980s. Coming from Taiwan—with a father who fought in the Chinese Civil War—Chen said he initially feared them and saw them as “Communist bandits.”

After getting to know them, he found himself sharing dumplings.

“Even with our ideological differences and the suspicion built up over years of separation…we speak the same language, share the same customs,” Chen said. “By evening, everyone would just be happily drinking and joking together.”

With that experience in mind, Chen, a soil-and-water conservation engineer, has a solution to today’s tensions: the establishment of a “Chinese Commonwealth” in which China, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau live under the same umbrella. Taiwan would have its own flag, military and diplomatic standing, including a seat at the United Nations alongside the others.

A commonwealth may be unlikely, but Chen, like some in Taiwan, believes it is possible to embrace more than one identity.

That is in part because his mother, from southern Taiwan, is Hakka, a people that began migrating from the mainland as early as the 17th century.

Chen holds a map of his ancestral village in China during his visit to the cultural district, which preserves centuries of Hakka history.

“Growing up in Taiwan, I am Taiwanese. But I am also Chinese—my father is from China, and we share the same language and cultural heritage on both sides.”

Chen also believes the U.S. lost the moral standing to have a say in the island’s affairs when Washington dropped formal recognition of Taiwan in 1979.

What’s more, Chen says, war isn’t an option. Now that China is a modern, global superpower, he says, Taiwan would be doomed if it treated China as an enemy.

#### **Peace through humility**

Eric Li says his generation favors maintaining the status quo in Taiwan.

Eric Li, 25, said he doesn’t know anyone who would rush into battle if war breaks out. Instead, he said, “Everyone says they’d hide, hide, hide.”

That doesn’t mean his generation, born in Taiwan’s democratic era, doesn’t see a need to preserve their way of life.

“Young people these days prefer the status quo because nobody wants a war,” he said. “Everyone just wants to live in peace and work happily.”

Li studied political science at a Taipei university but doesn’t talk politics with his friends, to avoid conflict given the rancor of Taiwan’s political scene.

But when pressed, Li clearly has his views. He said he values Taiwan’s elections and open economic system, freedoms that aren’t available on the mainland.

Li visits a former mall turned public space in his hometown, Tainan.

He also supports a friendlier approach to relations with Beijing. Li said he believes Taiwan should learn from China’s successes rather than focusing on the threats.

Having made several trips to the mainland, Li was particularly impressed by China’s infrastructure and its technological advancements in drones and robotics, areas where Taiwan has been a follower more than an innovator.

He also sees China as too powerful to ignore. Keeping the peace may require making some symbolic concessions to Beijing, as when Taiwan competes at the Olympics under the banner of “Chinese Taipei” rather than insisting on using its official name, “Republic of China.”

“Getting the perks while taking a small loss is totally fine by me,” he said. “It’s not like you’re actually losing out on anything.”
