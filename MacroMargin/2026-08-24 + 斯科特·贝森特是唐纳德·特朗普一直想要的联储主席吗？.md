### 中文译文

## 斯科特·贝森特是唐纳德·特朗普一直想要的联储主席吗？

### 或许吧。但他压低债券收益率的努力很可能失败

![photograph: kenny holston/the new york times/redux/eyevine](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c681cc90b148171bc8ffff3e92cc8bb/3c681cc9-0b14-81fe-adfa-e59809469d47-df6b00bcf10358ad5cd45ad21f91182e.jpg)

摄影：Kenny Holston/《纽约时报》/Redux/eyevine

斯科特·贝森特常说他想成为美国的“首席债券推销员”。对这位财政部长来说，债券收益率下降将是成功的标志。不过，当一位会计遇到一个通过购买自己的产品来美化数据的推销员时，可能会皱起眉头。8月19日，贝森特先生公布了财政部回购数百亿美元长期政府债务的计划。收益率一度下降，但随后再次上升（见图表1）。这个花招不仅可能失败，还有可能损害品牌吗？

今年，全球政府债券收益率飙升（意味着价格下跌）。美国十年期国债收益率上升了0.6个百分点；德国国债上升了0.4个百分点；日本政府债券上升了0.8个百分点。这源于几股力量。在疫情后的飙升之后，高通胀尚未被完全击败，而此时美国和以色列与伊朗开战，推高了油价。为大规模建设人工智能数据中心而进行的私人借贷也使得包括政府在内的所有人的资本成本更高。

![chart: the economist](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c681cc90b148171bc8ffff3e92cc8bb/3c681cc9-0b14-818d-9df3-d336ac2468cf-a8e0cea3e988c92e0545ec479be07b15.png)

图表：《经济学人》

最令人担忧的是，债券市场开始正视富裕世界债务狂欢的后果。美国的预算赤字占GDP的6%，是除经济衰退和战争时期以外最宽的；8月19日，财政部表示联邦债务已超过40万亿美元（约为GDP的130%）。无党派的国会预算办公室预计赤字和债务只会继续增长。贝森特先生自己提出的到2028年将赤字降至GDP的3%的目标看起来很不现实。其他大型经济体，尤其是法国和日本，状况也不佳。更糟糕的是，美国赤字中现在有一半以上用于支付过去借款的利息（见图表2）。这可能导致收益率上升、赤字扩大、收益率进一步上升的恶性循环，如此循环往复。

这不仅让贝森特先生难堪，对特朗普政府来说也是个问题。距离中期选举还有十周，“可负担性”是当下的关键词。然而，对许多选民来说，两个最突出的成本——汽油价格和30年期抵押贷款利率——都在朝着错误的方向发展。前者正在推动收益率上升；后者则是其后果。

![chart: the economist](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c681cc90b148171bc8ffff3e92cc8bb/3c681cc9-0b14-81cc-9c11-e1f484c12639-3acb36b12b29d0912a759ed6de56d8e7.png)

图表：《经济学人》

通过回购，贝森特试图施加影响。通常情况下，财政部认为自己的职责是在危机期间维持债券市场有序和流动性，而不是在相对平静的时期搅动收益率。贝森特的言论听起来很像他曾经的对冲基金交易员风格，他持有不同观点。“我们认为收益率并未反映基本面，”他在宣布该计划后的电视采访中表示。

美联储——美国宏观经济政策中的另一个权力中心——有时确实会尝试通过量化宽松（QE：通过创造银行准备金购买债券）或2011年的“扭转操作”（卖出短期国债并买入长期国债，这与贝森特的计划在央行层面形成镜像）等项目来影响债券收益率。但其目标始终是短期经济目标，如对抗衰退或通胀，而非维持政府财政运转。具有讽刺意味的是，新任美联储主席凯文·沃什是在贝森特主导的流程中被选中的，他却否定了量化宽松，并表示政策制定者不应在市场中留下沉重足迹。

贝森特的声明立即产生的影响有限。收益率已回到他宣布前的水平。回购不会改变美国政府债务的总量，因为其资金将来自发行短期债券。与美国借贷规模相比，涉及金额较小。更重要的是该计划向市场传递的信号：如果收益率持续上升，财政部愿意进行抵制。但由经济基本面变化驱动的市场走势往往压倒最坚定的政府。贝森特在1992年曾帮助乔治·索罗斯的对冲基金打破英国汇率挂钩，他应该比大多数人更清楚这一点。

另一个讽刺之处在于，贝森特批评其前任珍妮特·耶伦将财政部政治化并干预美联储工作。在耶伦任内，财政部提高了短期债务发行比例。这与贝森特的回购类似，将借款从长期转向短期债务。耶伦领导下的财政部称其为技术性债务管理；贝森特则呼应批评，称其为2024年大选前刺激经济的“激进财政部发行”。

上任后，贝森特悄悄维持了同样的发行模式。现在他更进了一步，高调宣示意图。美国最终可能需要发行更多短期债务，因为一些长期债券的大买家（如固定收益养老金计划）的重要性正在下降。但这不是贝森特正在做的事情。

![chart: the economist](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c681cc90b148171bc8ffff3e92cc8bb/3c681cc9-0b14-81eb-8c9d-d558989031a4-4e11dd388ac8cc84cefbd9617c519bf7.png)

图表：《经济学人》

回购只是本届政府抵制债券收益率上升的最新努力。7月，贝森特与日本联合干预以提振日元，通过出售欧元为干预融资，从而最小化其对美国国债的影响。据报道正在讨论的与阿联酋开设美元互换额度，将确保阿联酋（其主权财富基金是美国国债的大持有者）在危机时无需出售这些债券。此外，过去一年中，政府赞助企业房利美和房地美增加了对抵押贷款支持证券的购买，显然是在其好斗的老板比尔·普尔特和特朗普总统的敦促下，试图压低抵押贷款利率（见图表3）。（在米莱总统紧张的选举竞争中支持阿根廷比索，也表明贝森特愿意将美国金融火力用于政治目的。）

贝森特的行动长期来看可能影响不大，但很可能足以将收益率压低一点，至少持续到中期选举。这已经是近期历史上对国债市场更为大胆的政治化操作。不幸的是，市场有其他泄压阀。他的回购声明后，美元下跌，黄金飙升：两者都表明投资者对美国资产的怀疑加剧。（包括副总统J.D.万斯在内的一些MAGA人士会欢迎美元走弱，以降低出口价格并促进制造业。但直接影响将是进口更贵导致通胀上升。贝森特不属于弱美元阵营，因此这不太可能成为计划的一部分。）

![chart: the economist](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c681cc90b148171bc8ffff3e92cc8bb/3c681cc9-0b14-81ad-ac8a-c769e32526eb-35205c221795afd9310e4e27f6fb3d6d.png)

图表：《经济学人》

### **斯科特对阵凯文**

归根结底，收益率下降加上美元走弱等于经济刺激，类似于降息。无论政治盘算如何，美国经济都不需要这样的刺激。市场预期，美联储在9月下一次会议上甚至可能加息。沃什先生坚称，他在让通胀回到美联储2%目标的问题上“不会动摇”，而通胀已超过目标五年。他在上任头几个月对自己的想法刻意含糊其辞，这本身就可能推高了收益率。

财政部放松与美联储收紧可能会在未来几个月引发一场奇特的货币政策拉锯战。特朗普先生长期以来一直抱怨高利率。贝森特先生可能盘算过，试图通过喊话压低收益率可以让老板看到他在努力，即使市场不买账。但他走得越远，给沃什先生施加的压力就越大。

贝森特先生的干预可能反映了他宏观交易员的过往、特朗普先生对经济学的奇特看法以及中期选举政治。但这些不仅仅是特朗普式的异动。随着各国债务状况恶化，干预市场的诱惑越来越大——看看日本对日元及其国债市场的不断干预就知道了。除非美国政客出人意料地展示削减赤字的勇气，或者人工智能带来巨大的生产力红利，否则美国将需要发行更多的国债来为其不断增长的债务融资。如果投资者不再将债券市场视为一个稳定、非政治化的场所，那么发债成本将变得更高。每一次干预，贝森特先生及其继任的推销员们都会发现他们的货越来越难卖。■

### English Original

## Is Scott Bessent the Fed chair Donald Trump always wanted?

### Perhaps. But his effort to talk bond yields lower is likely to fail

Photograph: Kenny Holston/The New York Times/Redux/eyevine

Scott Bessent often says he wants to be America’s “top bond salesman”. For the treasury secretary, falling bond yields would be a sign of success. An accountant might raise an eyebrow, though, on encountering a salesman who juiced his figures by purchasing his own wares. On August 19th Mr Bessent set out plans for the Treasury to buy back tens of billions of dollars’ worth of long-dated government debt. Yields briefly declined, but then rose again (see chart 1). Might the wheeze not only fail, but also risk spoiling the brand?

This year government-bond yields around the world have surged (meaning prices have fallen). They are up by 0.6 percentage points on American ten-year Treasuries; by 0.4 points on German bunds; and by 0.8 points on Japanese government bonds. This is due to a handful of forces. High inflation had still not been fully beaten back after the post-pandemic surge when America and Israel went to war with Iran, pushing up oil prices. Private borrowing to fund the vast build-out of artificial-intelligence data centres has also made capital costlier for everyone, including governments.

Chart: The Economist

Most worryingly, bond markets are beginning to reckon with the consequences of the rich world’s debt binge. America’s budget deficit is 6% of GDP, the widest ever outside recession and wartime; on August 19th the Treasury said federal debt had passed $40trn (around 130% of GDP). The Congressional Budget Office, a nonpartisan scorekeeper, expects both deficits and debt only to grow. Mr Bessent’s own target of reducing deficits to 3% of GDP by 2028 looks fanciful. Other big economies, notably France and Japan, are also in poor shape. Worse, more than half of America’s deficit now consists of interest payments on past borrowing (see chart 2). That risks a vicious circle of rising yields, bigger deficits, still higher yields and so on.

This is not just an embarrassment for Mr Bessent. It is also a problem for the Trump administration. The midterm elections are ten weeks away and “affordability” is the word of the moment. Yet the two most salient costs for many voters—petrol prices and the 30-year mortgage rate—are both moving in the wrong direction. The first is helping to cause the rise in yields; the second is its consequence.

Chart: The Economist

With his buy-backs, Mr Bessent is trying to put his thumb on the scale. Usually, the Treasury sees its role as keeping the bond market orderly and liquid during crises, not jostling yields around in what should be quieter times. Mr Bessent, sounding rather like the hedge-fund trader he once was, has taken a different view. “We believe that the yields don’t reflect the underlying fundamentals,” he said in a television interview after announcing his scheme.

The Federal Reserve—the other centre of power in American macroeconomic policy—does sometimes try to move bond yields, through programmes like quantitative easing (QE: buying bonds by creating bank reserves) or “Operation Twist” in 2011 (selling short-term Treasuries and buying long-term ones, a central-banking mirror of Mr Bessent’s scheme). But its goal has always been a short-term economic one, such as fighting recession or inflation, not keeping the government’s finances afloat. Ironically, Kevin Warsh, the Fed’s new chair, who was picked in a process run by Mr Bessent, has disavowed QE and says policymakers should not leave a heavy footprint in markets.

Mr Bessent’s announcement had only a limited immediate effect. Yields are already back to where they were before he made it. The buy-backs will not alter the total amount of American government debt, since they will be funded by issuing shorter-term bonds. And the amounts involved are small compared with the scale of America's borrowing. More important is the signal the scheme sends to markets: that the Treasury is willing to resist if yields keep rising. But market moves driven by changes in economic fundamentals have a habit of overwhelming even the most determined governments. Mr Bessent, who in 1992 helped to break Britain’s currency peg while working for George Soros’s hedge fund, should know that better than most.

Another irony is that Mr Bessent criticised his predecessor, Janet Yellen, for politicising the Treasury and interfering with the work of the Fed. Under Ms Yellen, the Treasury nudged up the share of government debt issued at shorter maturities. That, like Mr Bessent’s buy-backs, shifted borrowing from long- to short-term debt. Ms Yellen’s Treasury called it technocratic debt management; Mr Bessent echoed criticism of this as “activist Treasury issuance” to juice the economy before the 2024 election.

After taking charge, Mr Bessent quietly maintained the same issuance pattern. Now he has gone further with his loud declaration of intent. America may eventually need to issue more short-term debt, since some big buyers of long-dated bonds, like defined-benefit pension schemes, are receding in importance. But that is not what Mr Bessent is up to.

Chart: The Economist

The buybacks are only the administration’s latest effort to resist rising bond yields. In July Mr Bessent structured his joint intervention with Japan to boost the yen in a way that minimised its impact on Treasuries, funding it through selling euros. Opening a dollar swap line with the United Arab Emirates, an idea reportedly under discussion, would ensure that the UAE, whose sovereign-wealth fund is a big holder of Treasuries, would not need to sell them in a crunch. In addition, over the past year Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the government-sponsored enterprises that package up mortgages, have increased their purchases of mortgage-backed securities in an apparent effort to push down mortgage rates, urged by Bill Pulte, their pugnacious boss, and President Trump (see chart 3). (Support for Argentina’s peso during a tight election fight for Javier Milei, the president, also illustrated Mr Bessent’s willingness to use American financial firepower for political purposes.)

Mr Bessent’s actions may not amount to much over the long term, but they may well be enough to squeeze yields down a little, at least until the midterms. That is already a far more brazen politicisation of the Treasury market than anything in recent history. Unfortunately for Mr Bessent, markets have other release valves. The dollar tumbled after his buy-back announcement (see chart 4), while gold surged: both signal investors’ increased scepticism about American assets. (Some MAGA types, including J.D. Vance, the vice-president, would welcome a weaker dollar, in order to make exports cheaper and boost manufacturing. But the immediate impact would be higher inflation, via dearer imports. Mr Bessent is not in the weak-dollar camp, so this is unlikely to be part of the plan.)

Chart: The Economist

### **Scott against Kevin**

Ultimately, lower yields plus a weaker dollar equals economic stimulus, akin to an interest-rate cut. That is not what America’s economy needs, whatever the political calculations. Markets expect, if anything, the Fed to raise rates at its next meeting in September. Mr Warsh insists he “will not waver” in returning inflation to the Fed’s 2% target, which it has overshot for five years. His determination to be vague about his thinking in his first few months in the job may itself have pushed yields up a bit.

Easing by the Treasury and tightening by the Fed could lead to a curious monetary-policy tug-of-war over the next few months. Mr Trump has long groaned about high interest rates. Mr Bessent may have calculated that trying to jawbone yields down could show the boss he is trying, even if markets rebuff his efforts. But the further he goes, the more pressure he piles on Mr Warsh.

Mr Bessent’s interventions may reflect his macro-trader past, Mr Trump’s quirky views on economics, and midterm politics. But they are more than mere Trumpian aberrations. Messing with markets becomes more tempting as countries’ debt situation worsens—just look at Japan’s constant meddling in the yen and its own government-bond market. Barring an unexpected display of deficit-cutting courage by its politicians, or a vast productivity windfall from AI, America will need to issue ever more Treasuries to fund its growing debt. That will become pricier if investors no longer see the bond market as a stable, apolitical place. With each fiddle, Mr Bessent and his successor salesmen may find their wares increasingly hard to hawk. ■
