### 中文译文

## 台湾为无人机制造商安排70亿美元以应对中国

![A soldier in camouflage releasing a drone during a military exercise in Taiwan.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3ca81cc90b148186bbfcc1c44ba3911d/3ca81cc9-0b14-81a4-8f68-d7dd7f4faac4-fe6f2310ff59ecca645a8751a32718ca.jpg)

台湾已启动全岛范围的行动以加强无人机生产。彭博社

台北——[台湾](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/taiwan)立法院周四批准了一项计划，将在未来六年内投入超过70亿美元用于无人机，投资于一项旨在威慑中国攻击的战略，并支持一个旨在成为世界领导者的新兴本土产业。

通过押注购置包括空中和[水下无人机](https://www.wsj.com/world/asia/taiwan-looks-to-new-sea-drone-tech-to-repel-china-c1615d42?mod=article_inline)在内的完整无人系统编队，台湾希望建立防御能力，旨在[让其强大得多的对手在入侵前三思](https://www.wsj.com/world/asia/taiwans-new-strategy-make-china-fear-the-pain-of-an-invasion-dfe28815?mod=article_inline)。

台湾军方规划人员表示，他们正在借鉴乌克兰和伊朗的经验，以应对来自中国的威胁。中国宣称这个自治的民主岛屿是其领土的一部分，并且不排除以武力攻取的可能。

但中国在无人机生产方面[拥有巨大的领先优势](https://www.wsj.com/politics/national-security/the-u-s-wants-to-break-chinas-drone-dominance-heres-where-it-will-struggle-39e69e18?mod=article_inline)。台湾官员表示，这种先发优势和失衡增加了台湾发展军事无人机供应链的紧迫性，这条供应链[可以摆脱对中国组件的依赖而蓬勃发展](https://www.wsj.com/world/taiwan-drone-stockpiling-efforts-bcf70ef5?mod=article_inline)。

“迟做总比不做好”，副总统萧美琴表示，她指出台湾如何仔细研究了乌克兰无人机部队如何从“血泪战场”中建立起非对称作战能力。

台湾已启动全岛范围的行动以加强无人机生产。作为势头的一个迹象，根据政府资助的民主、社会与新兴技术研究所（DSET）的数据，今年头两个月台湾出口了超过85,000架无人机，而2024年全年为3,473架。

分析师表示，在等待更可靠的国内需求的同时，出口[无人机](https://www.wsj.com/topics/subject/drones)帮助台湾本土产业实现了增长。根据DSET去年发布的一份报告，该产业的总价值从2023年的8700万美元扩大到2024年的1.55亿美元。

据台北“国防安全研究院”（DSET）专注于无人机的政策分析师凯西·方（Cathy Fang）称，新的资助计划可能为无人机制造带来更可靠、更大的需求，从而提振台湾本土产业并建立威慑。

方表示：“无人机至关重要，因为它们直接支持台湾建设更分布式、更具生存能力和非对称的防御态势。”

她说：“如果台湾能够将六年年度预算框架转化为及时、可预测且规模足够的采购订单，这笔支出将加强威慑，以及台湾本土无人系统工业基础的韧性和竞争力。”

该计划侧重于购买台湾制造的无人机，但不排除外国采购。

获批的支出计划不如执政党所寻求的那样有力，执政党寻求约60亿美元的特别预算。

由反对党控制的立法机构批准了更高的数额，但要求将其分散在更长时期内，且附加更多条件。这还推迟了实施，并将资金纳入年度预算，而年度预算需要每年审批。

方表示：“最终通过的版本反映了折衷。主要的不确定性在于实施。”

台湾国防部对政府提出的预算未能通过表示遗憾。该提案专门拨款用于未来五年购买三类无人平台：海岸监视无人机、海岸攻击无人机和小型单向攻击无人艇。

反对党批准的法案仍须由台湾行政院长卓荣泰（Cho Jung-tai，执政的民主进步党成员）签署才能生效。

投票结束后，卓荣泰未表示是否会签署该法案。

“我关心的是国家安全，”卓荣泰说。“我们将从国家需求和国家安全的角度仔细评估此事。”

## Taiwan Lines Up $7 Billion Boost for Drone Builders to Counter China

Taiwan has launched an islandwide effort to bolster drone production. Bloomberg

TAIPEI—[Taiwan](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/taiwan)’s legislature approved a plan on Thursday to spend over $7 billion on drones over the next six years, investing in a strategy to deter an attack by China and supporting an emerging domestic industry that aims to be a world leader.

By placing bets on acquiring a full fleet of uncrewed systems, including aerial and [undersea drones](https://www.wsj.com/world/asia/taiwan-looks-to-new-sea-drone-tech-to-repel-china-c1615d42?mod=article_inline), Taiwan hopes to build defensive capabilities intended [to make its far more powerful adversary think twice](https://www.wsj.com/world/asia/taiwans-new-strategy-make-china-fear-the-pain-of-an-invasion-dfe28815?mod=article_inline) about invading.

Taiwanese military planners said they are taking a page from Ukraine and Iran to counter the threat from China, which claims the self-ruled democracy as part of its territory and hasn’t ruled out taking it by force.

But China [has a formidable lead in drone production](https://www.wsj.com/politics/national-security/the-u-s-wants-to-break-chinas-drone-dominance-heres-where-it-will-struggle-39e69e18?mod=article_inline). The head start and imbalance add to the urgency for Taiwan to develop a military-drone supply chain that [can flourish without relying on Chinese components](https://www.wsj.com/world/taiwan-drone-stockpiling-efforts-bcf70ef5?mod=article_inline), Taiwan officials say.

“Better late than never,” said Vice President Hsiao Bi-khim, noting how closely Taiwan had studied how Ukrainian drone units built asymmetric warfare capabilities from the “battlefield of blood and tears.”

Taiwan has launched an islandwide effort to bolster drone production. In one indication of momentum, it exported more than 85,000 drones in the first two months of this year, compared with 3,473 in all of 2024, according to the government-funded Research Institute for Democracy, Society and Emerging Technology, or DSET.

Making [drones](https://www.wsj.com/topics/subject/drones) for export has helped Taiwan’s homegrown sector grow as it waits for more reliable domestic demand, analysts say. The sector’s total value expanded to $155 million in 2024 from $87 million in 2023, according to a DSET report published last year.

The new funding plan could potentially bring a more reliable, larger demand for drone production, giving a boost to Taiwan’s domestic industry and building deterrence, according to Cathy Fang, a policy analyst focusing on drones at DSET in Taipei.

“Drones are critical because they directly support Taiwan’s effort to build a more distributed, survivable and asymmetric defense posture,” said Fang.

“If Taiwan can convert the six-year annual-budget framework into timely, predictable, and sufficiently large procurement orders, the spending can strengthen both deterrence and the resilience and competitiveness of Taiwan’s domestic uncrewed-systems industrial base,” she said.

The plan is focused on buying Taiwan-made drones, but doesn’t rule out foreign acquisition.

The approved spending plan wasn’t as robust as that sought by the ruling party, which sought a special budget of around $6 billion.

The opposition-controlled legislature approved a higher figure but is requiring that it be spread over a longer period, with more conditions. It also delays implementation and includes the funds in annual budgets, which require annual approval.

“The final passed version reflects a compromise,” said Fang. “The main uncertainty is implementation.”

Taiwan’s Ministry of National Defense expressed regret that the government’s proposed budget had failed to pass. That proposal allocated funding specifically for the purchase of three types of uncrewed platforms over the next five years: coastal surveillance drones, coastal attack drones and small one-way attack drone boats.

The opposition-approved bill must still be signed by Taiwan Premier Cho Jung-tai, a member of the ruling Democratic Progressive Party, to take effect.

Following the vote, Cho didn’t say whether he would sign it.

“What I care about is national security,” Cho said. “We will carefully evaluate this from the perspective of national needs and national security.”
