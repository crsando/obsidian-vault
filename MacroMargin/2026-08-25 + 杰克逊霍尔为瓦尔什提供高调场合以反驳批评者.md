### 中文译文

## 杰克逊霍尔为瓦尔什提供高调场合以反驳批评者

凯文·瓦尔什作为美联储主席的首次重要讲话，意外地成为对他精简沟通风格的考验。

瓦尔什面临的挑战是，既要反驳关于他对经济观点不够坦诚的批评，又不能在坚持不向投资者透露未来政策动向线索的决心上让步。

美联储在怀俄明州杰克逊霍尔的年度会议——瓦尔什将于周五发表讲话——是他实现这一平衡的机会。

上个月一场表现不稳的新闻发布会引发债券市场的斥责后，来自华尔街的压力巨大。此后，经济学家和分析师一直毫不留情地批评瓦尔什，认为他在限制美联储沟通方面走得太远。

上周，财政部长斯科特·贝森特宣布了出人意料的美国国债回购，以压低长期收益率，这使瓦尔什的任务变得更加棘手。

“很明显，瓦尔什想要少说而不是多说，”北方信托资产管理公司联席首席投资官安维蒂·巴胡古纳表示，“但关于你为何处于当前境地、你今天看到了什么，多一些透明度和沟通，是市场有理由提出的问题。”

这并不意味着这位新任主席可能会道歉。瓦尔什的捍卫者表示，市场对他7月新闻发布会的反应过度了。通胀预期——如果对美联储的信心下降，通胀预期本应上升——仅温和变动，并仍锚定在与央行2%目标一致的水平。他们说，债券收益率是由政府和公司借贷激增等因素推动的。

芝加哥大学经济学教授、2006年至2009年担任美联储理事的兰德尔·克罗兹纳（Randall Kroszner）认为，沃什只是处于沟通改革的开端，该改革旨在约束有关政策走向的明确指引。

克罗兹纳表示：“市场有时会出错。我在美联储任职期间，市场犯过很多错误。”“采用新方法时，总会遇到一些初期问题。”

沃什的美联储同事，包括旧金山联储行长玛丽·戴利（Mary Daly）和圣路易斯联储主席阿尔贝托·穆萨勒姆（Alberto Musalem），也反驳了央行信誉受损的指控。

戴利在彭博电视的[采访](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/daly-says-bond-market-showing-trust-in-fed-s-policy-positioning)中表示：“我不认为我们的信誉面临风险。”

### **发布会上的摇摆**

本不必如此充满争议。

沃什于5月上任后，因反复强调在他的任期内美联储将让通胀回到2%目标而赢得赞誉。在7月向立法者作证时，他表示央行对通胀零容忍。

这缓解了人们对沃什的担忧，即如果条件需要，这位由唐纳德·特朗普总统任命的人会抵制加息。特朗普曾猛烈抨击沃什的前任，并承诺任命一位将降低利率的新主席。

他在美联储7月政策会议后首次出现重大动摇，当时政策制定者投票维持基准联邦基金利率不变。尽管有三位官员持异议，但该决定符合预期，且已被债券市场消化。正是沃什在会后新闻发布会上引发了投资者和经济学家的广泛批评。

美联储观察人士称，这场新闻发布会存在三个关键问题。首先是没有解释为何维持政策不变；其次是沃什不愿将加息视为未来可能需要采取的明确政策工具；第三是他在谈及美联储2%通胀目标时随口一说，让一些人认为这一目标可能在明年1月被调整。

最终结果是债券市场遭遇多年来最严重的抛售，沃什的沟通风格也招致大量批评。沃什讲话后，30年期美国国债收益率飙升至2007年以来的最高水平。

“沃什即使被直接问到，也无法或不愿解释7月份为何未采取政策行动。这令人费解，”美联储前高级政策顾问罗伯特·泰特洛表示。“他本可以轻易做到，而不必陷入前瞻性指引。”

近几周，这轮抛售仍在持续。当然，这并非全因美联储所致。投资者对美国债务不断攀升日益担忧，随着华盛顿借入更多资金，它正与投入大量资金发展人工智能的科技公司争夺资金。此外，欧洲和日本主权债券收益率上升表明，投资者的担忧已超出美国通胀范围。

“眼下多种因素交织在一起，或多或少都在推高长期收益率，”先锋集团高级经济学家亚当·希克林表示。

贝森特计划回购较长期债券以压低收益率，这给美联储带来了另一个潜在复杂因素，尤其是如果计划中的购债通过加大短期债务销售来融资的话。这将侵入美联储传统上主导的国债曲线领域。

对沃什来说，一个令人宽慰的迹象是，自7月政策决定以来的数据大致表明经济活动正在放缓，这可能会缓解美联储加息的压力。7月零售销售创下一年多来最大降幅，核心通胀温和。与此同时，雇主7月意外裁员，前两个月的招聘数据也被下修。

尽管如此，许多人同意沃什需要回应他的批评者。

杜克大学经济学教授艾伦·米德表示，美联储主席需要在削减前瞻指引的愿望与不模糊透明度之间取得平衡。米德在美联储理事会任职数十年，曾为美联储官员提供建议。

“沃什并没有为自己赢得多少好感，”她说，“他把自己逼入了绝境。”

### English Original

## Jackson Hole Offers Warsh High Profile Slot to Rebut His Critics

Kevin Warsh’s first major speech as chairman of the Federal Reserve has become an unexpected trial of his slimmed-down communications style.

The challenge facing Warsh is to counter criticism that he hasn’t been forthright with his views on the economy, without capitulating on his determination not to spoon feed investors with clues on future policy moves.

The central bank’s annual gathering in Jackson Hole, Wyoming, where Warsh will speak Friday, is his opportunity to strike that balance.

The pressure from Wall Street is intense after a shaky press conference last month triggered a bond market rebuke. Economists and analysts have since been relentless in calling out Warsh for, in their view, going too far in a quest to limit Fed communications.

Warsh’s task was made even trickier last week when Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced surprise buybacks of US debt in an effort to lower long-term yields.

The Jackson Lake Lodge, site of the Kansas City Federal Reserve’s Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium in Moran, Wyoming. Photographer: Natalie Behring/Bloomberg

“Clearly Warsh wants to say less than more,” said Northern Trust Asset Management’s co-chief investment officer, Anwiti Bahuguna. “But some transparency and some communication on why you are where you are, what do you see today, is a reasonable question for markets to ask.”

That doesn’t mean the new chairman is likely to offer any apologies. Warsh’s defenders say the market reaction to his July press conference was overdone. Inflation expectations — which should climb if confidence in the Fed drops — moved only modestly and remain anchored at a level consistent with the central bank’s 2% target. Bond yields, they say, are being driven by surging government and corporate borrowing, among other factors.

Randall Kroszner, an economics professor at the University of Chicago and a Fed governor from 2006 to 2009, argued that Warsh is only at the start of a communications overhaul that’s aimed at reining in explicit guidance on where policy is headed.

“Markets can sometimes get things wrong. I was at the Fed when the markets got a lot of things wrong,” Kroszner said. “There’s always going to be kind of a teething problem when there’s a new approach.”

Warsh’s Fed colleagues including San Francisco Fed chief Mary Daly and St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem have also pushed back against charges that the central bank’s credibility has been damaged.

“I don’t see our credibility at risk,” Daly said in an [interview](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-20/daly-says-bond-market-showing-trust-in-fed-s-policy-positioning) on Bloomberg Television.

### **Press Conference Wobble**

It needn’t have been so contentious.

After taking office in May, Warsh won praise for hammering home a message that under his watch the Fed would return inflation to its 2% target. During testimony to lawmakers in July he said the central bank had no tolerance for inflation.

This eased worries that Warsh, who was appointed by President Donald Trump, would resist raising rates if conditions called for that. Trump had savaged Warsh’s predecessor and promised to name a new chair who would lower rates.

His first major wobble came right after the Fed’s July policy meeting when policymakers voted to leave the benchmark federal funds rate unchanged. While three officials dissented, the decision was expected and priced into bond markets. It was Warsh’s post-meeting press conference that triggered a broad rebuke from investors and economists.

Fed watchers say there were three pivotal aspects of the press conference. The first was the absence of an explanation as to why policy was held steady, and the second was Warsh’s unwillingness to present rate hikes as an obvious policy tool that might yet be needed. The third was a throwaway remark on the Fed’s 2% inflation target that led some to think it might be altered come January.

The end result was the bond market’s worst selloff in years and a torrent of criticism over Warsh’s communication style. The yield on 30-year Treasuries surged to its highest since 2007 after Warsh spoke.

“Warsh couldn’t, or wouldn’t, even explain the non-policy action in July, even when asked directly. That’s baffling,” said Robert Tetlow, a former senior policy adviser at the Fed. “It would have been easy for him to do without slipping into forward guidance.”

That selloff has continued in recent weeks. To be sure, it’s not all due to the Fed. Investors have grown increasingly concerned by rising US debt, and as Washington borrows more it’s competing for funds with technology companies pouring money into artificial intelligence. Moreover, rising yields for sovereign debt in Europe and Japan show investors’ concerns are wider than US inflation.

“There’s just a confluence of factors right now that are all more or less contributing to higher long-term yields,” said Adam Schickling, a senior economist at Vanguard Group Inc.

Bessent’s plan to buy back longer dated bonds in an effort to hold down yields is another potential complication for the Fed, especially if the planned purchases are financed through larger sales of shorter-dated debt. That would trespass into the sector of the Treasury curve the Fed traditionally dominates.

In a hint of relief for Warsh, data since the July policy decision has broadly pointed to a slowing in economic activity that may ease pressure on the Fed to raise interest rates. Retail sales fell in July by the most in more than a year while core inflation was subdued. At the same time employers unexpectedly cut jobs in July and hiring in the prior two months was revised lower.

Still, many agree Warsh will need to address his critics.

The Fed chairman will need to strike a balance between his desire to trim forward guidance without clouding transparency, said Ellen Meade, an economics professor at Duke University who advised Fed officials during a decades-long career at the board.

“Warsh is not doing himself a lot of favors,” she said. “He has painted himself into a box.”
