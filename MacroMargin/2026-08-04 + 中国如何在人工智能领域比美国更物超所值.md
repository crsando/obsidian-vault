> 📌 **核心摘要**
>
> 中国在人工智能领域的投资远低于美国，却以更高效的方式取得了接近的模型性能，这种反差源于成本优势、技术制约与务实战略。
> • 美国科技巨头今年数据中心资本支出或超7400亿美元，中国预计不到其十分之一。
> • 中国模型性能接近美国前沿水平，如K3达到Anthropic Fable 5的95%且成本低70%。
> • 美国制裁限制中国获取最先进芯片，迫使中国采用国产替代和更高效的训练技术。
> • 中国AI需求有限、企业IT预算节俭，且政策更注重技术扩散而非追求超级智能。
> • 中国投资者长期警惕AI过度支出，而美国投资者近期开始担忧数据中心过剩。

### 中文译文

## 中国如何在人工智能领域比美国更物超所值

### 投资远逊于美国，模型却不落下风

![Photograph: Alamy 图片：alamy](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b281cc90b14812f858ed9e334118661/3b281cc9-0b14-8117-b4a8-e3cac11043e1-033da3949c8d6b7215ceb72df73ffe27.jpg)

企业应在人工智能上投入多少资金？美国科技巨头认为答案是：大量投入。据彭博行业研究预计，今年它们在数据中心上的资本支出可能超过 7400 亿美元。过去同意这一观点的投资者开始动摇。7 月 26 日，有消息称英伟达正洽谈为一座规模超 5000 亿美元的数据中心出资 2500 亿美元，该中心将装满其芯片，并由领先模型制造商 OpenAI 运营，英伟达股价随即大跌。数日前，谷歌母公司 Alphabet 表示今年将把人工智能支出提高到 2050 亿美元，令投资者大为震惊。日本、韩国和台湾的人工智能类股票也大幅下挫。

企业应在人工智能上投入多少资金？美国科技巨头认为答案是：大量投入。据研究公司彭博行业研究估算，今年它们在数据中心上的资本支出可能超过 7400 亿美元。此前认同这一观点的投资者如今开始动摇。7 月 26 日有消息称，英伟达正洽谈为一座规模超 5000 亿美元的数据中心承销 2500 亿美元——该中心将满载其芯片，并由领先模型制造商 OpenAI 运营——其股价随即下跌。数日前，谷歌母公司 Alphabet 宣布今年将把人工智能支出增至 2050 亿美元，令投资者大为震惊。日本、韩国及台湾地区的人工智能相关股票也大幅下挫。

美国在 AI 上的挥霍与中国人的节俭相比，显得尤为浪费。据预测，到 2026 年，中国科技巨头在数据中心上的投入将不到美国同行的十分之一（见图表）。但正因为这种节俭，它们的模型在性能上只是稍逊一筹。总部位于北京的初创公司月之暗面（Moonshot AI）上月推出高级模型 K3，在广泛使用的基准测试中，其聪明程度达到另一家美国顶尖实验室 Anthropic 的前沿模型 Fable 5 的 95%，使用成本还低 70%。8 月 3 日，中国科技巨头阿里巴巴发布了一款模型，据报道在某些指标上位居世界前列。

![Chart: The Economist](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b281cc90b14812f858ed9e334118661/3b281cc9-0b14-81c1-9e27-e4842d7cc02c-9f383139539b9152e2e32d3e5595ef79.png)

中国的地价（用于数据中心）、部分设备（用于向其中填充 AI 服务器）和劳动力（从建筑工人到 AI 奇才）都低于美国。中国公司还被指利用昂贵的美国模型的输出来训练自己的模型。这种被称为“蒸馏”的过程，让中国 AI 开发者得以在某种程度上搭美国支出的便车。部分中国 AI 支出也可能未纳入总资本支出数字。中国 AI 公司（尤其是小公司）原本可能用于购买英伟达最先进芯片的部分资金，或许被转而用于从性能较弱的芯片中压榨出性能。DeepSeek 在 2025 年初发布了一款出奇强大且高效的模型，让美国 AI 公司和投资者惊出一身冷汗，它很可能正在投入可观的资金来发展降低算力需求的技术。

然而，仅靠这些因素无法解释，为什么中国能够以远低于美国的投入，取得看似相当的 AI 成果。那到底是什么原因呢？

对中国投资的一种制约并非资金短缺，而是缺乏可投资的项目。美国对华技术出口限制意味着，中国公司无法购买最好也最贵的人工智能芯片——这些芯片由美国公司英伟达设计，由与美国关系密切的台湾代工制造商（或晶圆厂）台积电（TSMC）生产。非正式地，北京监管机构一直不鼓励使用中国公司目前仍可购买的二流英伟达芯片，有时还阻止其进口，目的是让中国的 AI 基础设施免受制裁影响，并推广国产替代品。

以阿里巴巴为例。去年，该公司表示将在 2026 至 2028 年间向 AI 投资 530 亿美元。与亚马逊或 Alphabet 相比，这笔计划支出简直是九牛一毛。即便如此，这笔支出可能仍难以实现。阿里巴巴拥有中国最先进的芯片设计项目之一，过去曾将制造外包给台积电。美国的制裁迫使其寻找国内供应商。

中国唯一能够生产性能相当强大芯片的供应商是华为和中芯国际。华为是一家自2019年以来被美国列入黑名单的科技巨头，而中芯国际是一家国有控股的代工厂，负责制造华为的设计。然而，美国的限制还涉及精密的芯片制造设备，迫使华为和中芯国际想出昂贵的变通办法，这限制了这两家能够生产出的足够强劲芯片的数量。此外，中芯国际无法通过增加资本支出来抵消这种低效率，因为同样的制裁也限制了它能够获得的较不先进的芯片制造设备。

美国的制裁并非限制中国人工智能资本支出的唯一因素。中国对人工智能服务的需求也是如此。中国企业在IT预算方面的吝啬是出了名的，其总额合计不到美国公司在软件上支出的十分之一，尽管中国的GDP是美国的的三分之二（按购买力平价调整后则高出三分之一）。这种精打细算很可能会降低人工智能公司投资的回报，从而削弱它们一开始的投资意愿。

此外，中国共产党的AI雄心更多是将技术扩散到整个经济中，而不是创造出越来越聪明、需要越来越多数据中心中越来越强大半导体的系统。据称，数十家美国公司正在尝试开发人工通用智能，这种智能在大多数脑力任务上可能超越人类。相比之下，上海人工智能协会党委书记许琦（Xu Qi）指出，中国已知在研发AGI的公司不到十家。

另一个控制AI支出的信号来自熟悉所有这些制约因素的投资者。长期以来，中国投资者对AI过度支出的警惕，与美国投资者现在显然正变得警惕的程度不相上下。然而，美国科技巨头直到最近还因激进的AI支出计划而获得更高的股价，而中国的科技巨头则更可能因AI挥霍而受到惩罚。

投资者现在可能开始认为美国的数据中心太多了。但中国尽管有着令人钦佩的克制，却可能没有足够的数据中心来充分利用其人工智能明星公司的创新，或让这些明星公司变现。字节跳动是全球顶尖视频制作AI的创造者（也是TikTok的所有者），正遭受严重的计算瓶颈，一些视频片段需要十个小时才能处理完。来自智谱AI（另一家北京实验室）和阿里巴巴云计算部门的某些服务，必须严格计量，并在几分钟内售罄。K3有一长串抱有期望的用户在排队等待。实用AI的普及对算力的需求，远不如对超级智能的追求那样迫切。但过于节制仍可能阻碍增长。■

### English Original

## How China gets better bang for its buck than America in AI

### Its investment lags far behind America’s. Its models do not

其投资远不及美国，但模型表现却不逊色

HOW MUCH money should companies lavish on artificial intelligence? American tech giants think the answer is: a lot. Their capital spending on data centres may surpass $740bn this year, according to Bloomberg Intelligence, a research firm. Investors, who used to agree, are having second thoughts. When on July 26th it emerged that Nvidia was in talks to underwrite $250bn of a gargantuan $500bn-plus data centre, stuffed full of its chips and run by OpenAI, a leading model-maker, its share price tumbled. Days earlier investors blanched when Alphabet, Google’s parent company, said it would boost its AI spending to $205bn this year. AI stocks in Japan, South Korea and Taiwan have plummeted, too.

企业应在人工智能上投入多少资金？美国科技巨头认为答案是：大量投入。据研究公司彭博行业研究估算，今年它们在数据中心上的资本支出可能超过 7400 亿美元。此前认同这一观点的投资者如今开始动摇。7 月 26 日有消息称，英伟达正洽谈为一座规模超 5000 亿美元的数据中心承销 2500 亿美元——该中心将满载其芯片，并由领先模型制造商 OpenAI 运营——其股价随即下跌。数日前，谷歌母公司 Alphabet 宣布今年将把人工智能支出增至 2050 亿美元，令投资者大为震惊。日本、韩国及台湾地区的人工智能相关股票也大幅下挫。

The American AI splurge looks especially profligate compared with Chinese parsimony. In 2026 Chinese tech titans are forecast to invest less than a tenth as much in data centres as their American counterparts (see chart). Their models appear only fractionally less powerful owing to this frugality. K3, an advanced model launched last month by Moonshot AI, a Beijing-based startup, is 95% as clever (on widely used benchmarks) as Fable 5, a frontier model from Anthropic, another top American lab. It is also 70% cheaper to use. On August 3rd Alibaba, a Chinese tech giant, released a model which reportedly scored among the world’s best by some measures.

The cost of land (for data centres), some equipment (to fill these with AI servers) and labour (from construction workers to AI whizz-kids) is lower in China than in America. Chinese firms are also accused of training their models using the outputs of expensive American ones. Such “distillation”, as the process is known, lets China’s AI developers free-ride on some American spending. Some Chinese AI spending may also not be captured in the headline capital-expenditure figures. Part of the sums that Chinese AI firms, especially smaller ones, might have spent on Nvidia’s best chips may instead have gone towards squeezing results from inferior ones. DeepSeek, which gave American AI firms and investors a jump scare by launching a surprisingly powerful and efficient model in early 2025, is probably investing a fair bit in its techniques that reduce demand for computing power.

Yet such factors alone cannot explain why China can get away with investing so much less than America for what appear to be comparable AI results. So what does?

One check on Chinese investment has not been a shortage of capital but a dearth of things on which to spend it. American restrictions on tech exports to China mean that Chinese firms cannot buy the best—and priciest—AI chips, which are designed by Nvidia, an American firm, and manufactured by TSMC, a Taiwanese contract manufacturer (or foundry) with close links to America. Unofficially, regulators in Beijing have discouraged the use of second-rate Nvidia chips, which Chinese firms can still buy for now, and at times blocked their import, in order to sanction-proof China’s AI infrastructure and promote homemade alternatives.

Consider Alibaba. Last year it said that it would invest $53bn in AI between 2026 and 2028. The planned spending is chump change relative to that of Amazon or Alphabet. Even so, it may be out of reach. Alibaba, which boasts one of China’s most advanced chip-design programmes, used to outsource manufacturing to TSMC. American sanctions have forced it to find domestic suppliers.

The only Chinese purveyors capable of producing comparably powerful chips are Huawei, a technology behemoth blacklisted by America since 2019, and SMIC, a state-controlled foundry, which manufactures Huawei’s designs. However, American restrictions extend to sophisticated chipmaking gear, forcing Huawei and SMIC to come up with costly workarounds that limit how many sufficiently powerful chips the duo can churn out. Moreover, SMIC cannot offset this inefficiency by ramping up capital spending because the same sanctions also constrain how much less sophisticated chipmaking kit it can get its hands on.

American sanctions are not the only thing that limits Chinese AI capital spending. So does Chinese demand for AI services. Chinese businesses are notoriously stingy with their IT budgets, which are collectively less than one-tenth what American firms spend on software despite Chinese GDP being two-thirds of America’s (and a third bigger when adjusted for purchasing power). This penny-pinching is likely to reduce returns to AI firms’ investment—and thus their willingness to invest in the first place.

Moreover, the Communist Party’s AI ambitions are more about diffusing the technology through the economy than creating ever cleverer systems that require ever more powerful semiconductors in ever more data centres. Dozens of American firms are thought to be trying to develop artificial general intelligence, which could outdo humans in most intellectual tasks. In China, by contrast, fewer than ten companies are known to be working on AGI, notes Xu Qi, the head of the Communist Party committee at the Shanghai AI Association.

Another signal to keep AI spending in check comes from investors familiar with all these constraints. Chinese ones have long been as wary of overspending on AI as those in America are apparently becoming. Whereas America’s tech giants had until recently been rewarded with higher share prices for aggressive AI-spending plans, Chinese ones were likelier to be punished for AI profligacy.

Investors may now be entertaining the idea that America has too many data centres. But China, for all its admirable restraint, may not have enough to take full advantage of its AI stars’ innovations—or for those stars to cash in. ByteDance, creator of the world’s top-ranked video-making AI (and owner of TikTok), suffers from intense computing bottlenecks that mean some clips take ten hours to process. Certain services from Zhipu AI, another Beijing lab, and Alibaba’s cloud-computing unit have to be strictly metered and sell out in minutes. K3 has a long waiting list of hopeful users. Diffusion of practical AI is less hungry for computing power than the quest for superintelligence. An overly restrictive diet can still stunt growth. ■
