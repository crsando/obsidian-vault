### 中文译文

## 人民币不仅仅是全球失衡的一种症状

### 将其仅仅视为更深层次扭曲的副产品，会让中国过于轻易地脱身，阿南塔·纳格斯瓦兰和P.S.斯里尼瓦斯认为

![illustration: sam kerr](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b881cc90b1481cf80f4ed68befede70/3b881cc9-0b14-8137-bcb6-d073d5b52a9c-c2a82af85f4df46056e82a9aa90201f4.jpg)

2026年8月10日

在最近为*《经济学人》*撰写的一篇[文章](https://www.economist.com/by-invitation/2026/07/28/dont-blame-global-imbalances-on-the-undervalued-yuan)中，Gita Gopinath、Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas和Hélène Rey认为，中国的持续盈余和美国的持续赤字不应归咎于人民币被低估。他们说，汇率是国内储蓄和投资选择的结果，而不是纠正这些失衡的独立杠杆。这个论点在某种程度上是成立的：从长期看，汇率确实反映国内基本面。

但该论点走得太远，把人民币仅仅视为更深层扭曲的反映，好像货币独立于维持这些扭曲的政策体系之外。该文章的政策结论——汇率不是独立的杠杆——依赖于一个假设，即储蓄和投资驱动货币，而非相反或双向关系。诚然，一个国家相对于投资储蓄过多，往往会出现经常账户盈余，且常常伴随实际汇率走弱。然而，这并不使汇率在分析上在每个案例中都处于次要地位。

在短期内，汇率本身也是一个政策杠杆。如果不是这样，国际货币基金组织就不会长期以来将货币贬值作为其结构调整条件的一部分——这相当于默认汇率独立于其背后的政策而具有重要意义。在中国，那些抑制消费的国家主导型机构也阻止货币自由调整。汇率并不在失衡之外；它正是失衡产生的渠道之一。

溢出效应是真实的，而且在数据中越来越明显。中国的商品贸易顺差在2025年达到创纪录的1.2万亿美元，比上年增长五分之一，即使对美出口大幅下降；对其他国家出口的激增远远弥补了这一缺口。一个极具竞争力的货币使中国商品在国外保持廉价，迫使竞争对手相继压低本币汇率，从而把调整问题出口出去，而不是解决它：中国近来最大的出口是它的通缩。出口价格仍比其贸易伙伴的出口价格上涨得更慢，即便国内生产者价格最近有所回升。

这正是作者偏好的替代方案比看起来更弱的地方。2024年，家庭消费占中国GDP的比重约为40%，与二十年前几乎持平，尽管温家宝曾将中国“不平衡”的增长模式列为政策重点。二十年来，表露的意图几乎没有改变这个数字。汇率水平每天可见。改革承诺却可以无限期模糊。

作者们拒绝中国明天就能升值并彻底解决问题的幻想，这是正确的。单独来看，大幅升值可能会加剧通缩压力并削弱国内需求——尽管汇率变动对国内价格的传导往往比最坏情况下的假设要小。

国际货币基金组织（IMF）自己的2026年《对外部门报告》将人民币低估幅度的中值定为21.3%，一些市场估计甚至更高。假设人民币升值至IMF的中值水平，需求将从国内商品转向进口，部分国内产能将变得过剩并被淘汰——从再平衡的角度看，这两点都是受欢迎的。此外，人民币走强还有其他诸多好处：减少为压低汇率而进行的成本高昂的冲销干预，降低进口能源和大宗商品的价格（这些价格会传导至国内），以及减少因低估而日益招致的贸易摩擦——这对中国自身有利，而非对其贸易伙伴的恩惠。真正的问题是，持续升值压力与金融自由化和国内改革相结合，能否改变二十年来温和劝诫未能改变的激励机制。

中国的经常账户盈余在过去二十年中波动很大。但这种波动反映的是过剩储蓄的去向，而非潜在失衡是否得到修复：2007年至2018年间盈余的大幅收窄恰逢国内投资热潮，而非家庭消费上升。根深蒂固的部分在于储蓄-投资缺口本身——其根源在于地方政府财政、国有银行资产负债表以及可贸易部门的政治分量，而非经常账户表现，后者比生产它的结构更容易变动。这就是为什么与在选举周期和独立中央银行框架内展开的美国财政赤字相比，中国的再平衡更慢且代价更高。将两者视为对称，会让更根深蒂固的失衡逃避审视。

美国也必须调整自身的财政政策，以缩小吸引全球资本的赤字。发展中国家受到两方面的挤压：中国的过剩产能挤占了它们自身的制造业，而美国的赤字则将投资从它们身边引走。赤字国家至少维持了对其他国家所售商品的需求。

人民币的实际升值，加上美国真正的财政调整，将比任何一方的单独改革更能再平衡全球需求，并缓解以邻为壑的贸易措施压力。它还有助于重振许多发展中经济体的增长，稳定其社会，从而改善一个冲突更少的世界的前景。人民币是塑造全球失衡的政策架构的一部分，而非与之无关的装饰性结果。

当然，想一想世界上谁能独自促成人民币升值是毫无意义的。只有中国能做到。迄今为止，中国一直回避这个问题。在最近一篇关于中国“K型政策体制”的深刻文章中，麻省理工学院斯隆管理学院的黄亚生指出，在中国，解决国内需求是一个政治挑战，因为这要求国家让渡权力，而供给侧回应则无需如此。出口、贸易表现以及因此对竞争性货币的需求，都属于供给侧阵营。什么能说服中国为再平衡全球需求做出贡献，这个问题仍然悬而未决。■

### English Original

## The yuan is more than a symptom of global imbalances

### Treating it as a mere by-product of deeper distortions lets China off too easily, argue Anantha Nageswaran and P.S. Srinivas

Aug 10th 2026

In a recent [essay](https://www.economist.com/by-invitation/2026/07/28/dont-blame-global-imbalances-on-the-undervalued-yuan) for *The Economist*, Gita Gopinath, Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas and Hélène Rey argue that China’s persistent surpluses and America’s persistent deficits should not be blamed on an undervalued yuan. The exchange rate, they say, is an outcome of domestic savings and investment choices, not an independent lever for correcting them. The argument is sound as far as it goes: exchange rates do reflect domestic fundamentals over the long run.

But it goes too far in treating the yuan as merely a reflection of deeper distortions, as if the currency were external to the policy regime that sustains them. The essay’s policy conclusion—that the exchange rate is not an independent lever—depends on assuming savings and investment drive the currency, rather than the other way round or a two-way relationship. Yes, a country that saves too much relative to investment will tend to run a current-account surplus, often alongside a weaker real exchange rate. That, however, does not make the exchange rate analytically secondary in every case.

In the short run, the exchange rate is also a policy lever in its own right. If it were not, the imf would not have long made currency devaluation a standard part of its structural-adjustment conditionality—a tacit admission that exchange rates matter independently of the policies behind them. In China the same state-led institutions that depress consumption also prevent the currency from adjusting freely. The exchange rate is not outside the imbalance; it is one of the channels through which the imbalance is generated.

The spillovers are real and increasingly visible in the data. China’s goods-trade surplus hit a record $1.2trn in 2025, up a fifth on the year before, even as exports to America fell sharply; the shortfall was more than made good by a surge in shipments elsewhere. A hyper-competitive currency that keeps Chinese goods cheap abroad forces competitors to hold down their own exchange rates in turn, exporting the adjustment problem rather than solving it: China’s biggest export of late has been its deflation. Export prices are still rising more slowly than its trading partners’, even as domestic producer prices have recently ticked up.

This is where the authors’ preferred alternative is weaker than it looks. Household consumption’s share of Chinese gdp stood at roughly 40% in 2024—almost exactly where it stood two decades earlier, even as Wen Jiabao flagged China’s “unbalanced” growth model as a policy priority. Two decades of stated intent have barely moved the number at all. A currency level is visible every day. A reform pledge can be obscured indefinitely.

The authors are right to reject the fantasy that China could revalue tomorrow and solve the problem outright. A sharp appreciation, taken in isolation, could intensify deflationary pressure and weaken domestic demand—though the pass-through from currency moves to domestic prices tends to be smaller than the worst-case framing assumes.

The imf’s own 2026 External Sector Report puts the yuan’s undervaluation at a midpoint of 21.3%, with some market estimates running higher still. Suppose it closed at the imf’s midpoint. It would shift demand from domestic goods to imports and render some domestic capacity redundant, taking it out of circulation—both of which are welcome from a rebalancing standpoint. Among many other reasons, a firmer yuan would ease the costly sterilised intervention needed to hold the currency down, lower the cost of imported energy and commodities that feed into domestic prices, and reduce the trade friction its undervaluation increasingly invites abroad—gains to China itself, not favours to its trading partners. The real question is whether sustained appreciation pressure, paired with financial liberalisation and domestic reform, can shift incentives that two decades of polite exhortation have not.

China’s current-account surplus has swung considerably over the past two decades. But that volatility reflects where the excess savings went, not whether the underlying imbalance was fixed: a sharp narrowing of the surplus in 2007-18 coincided with a domestic investment boom, not a rise in household consumption. The entrenched piece is the savings-investment gap itself—rooted in local-government finance, state-bank balance-sheets and the political weight of the tradable sector, not its current-account expression, which moves far more easily than the structure producing it. That is what makes it slower and costlier to rebalance than an American fiscal deficit that unfolds within an electoral cycle and an independent central bank. Treating the two sides as symmetrical lets the more entrenched imbalance escape scrutiny.

America must also adjust its own fiscal policy to narrow the deficit that attracts global capital. Developing countries are squeezed from both directions: China’s excess capacity crowds out their own manufacturers, while American deficits draw investment away from them instead. A deficit country at least sustains demand for what others sell it.

A real appreciation of the yuan, alongside genuine fiscal adjustment in America, would do more to rebalance global demand and ease pressure towards beggar-thy-neighbour trade measures than either side’s reforms alone. It would also help reinvigorate growth in many developing economies and stabilise their societies, improving the prospects for a less conflict-prone world. The yuan is part of the policy architecture that shapes global imbalances, not a decorative outcome that sits outside it.

Of course, it is pointless to wonder who in the world can engineer an appreciation of the yuan all on their own. Only China can do it. So far, it has avoided the question. In an insightful recent post on China’s “k-shaped policy regime”, Yasheng Huang of mit Sloan suggests that addressing domestic demand is a political challenge in China because it requires the state to cede power, whereas a supply-side response does not. Exports, trade performance and hence the need for a competitive currency fall in the supply-side camp. The question of what will persuade China to contribute to rebalancing global demand remains open. ■
