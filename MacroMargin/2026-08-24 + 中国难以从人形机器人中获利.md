### 中文译文

## 中国难以从人形机器人中获利

### 领先对手并培训数千人，但盈利遥遥无期

![photograph: xinhua/shutterstock](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c681cc90b1481c1a26ef6f3bde6d6ec/3c681cc9-0b14-8149-8080-caa27ce92eae-d11e48e3e160984fa0e38bb555d06bcd.jpg)

图片：新华社/Shutterstock

中国的人形机器人正在飞速超越竞争对手——毫不夸张。8月17日，其中一台名为“超人”的机器人以每秒12.66米的速度奔跑，打破了尤塞恩·博尔特此前保持的纪录，随后撞上了墙。这一胜利进一步加剧了人们对“超人”制造商宇树科技的关注，该公司两天后在上海上市，股价暴涨460%。

随着人形机器人比赛在北京举行，未来几天预计还会有更令人印象深刻的表现。然而，“超人”奔跑壮举的不光彩结局表明，这项技术还有很长的路要走。预计中国企业今年将销售5万台人形机器人，是去年数字的三倍多。它们已在很大程度上掌握了机器背后的硬件。然而，软件则是另一回事。

为了创建能让机器人无缝执行大量有用任务的基础模型，现在必须收集海量数据。驱动当前一代人形机器人的模型拥有数十亿个“参数”；而要复制人体的功能，它们将需要数千亿个参数。

这将使它们的规模与驱动聊天机器人的大型语言模型相当。不同之处在于，聊天机器人可以在互联网上大量的数字文本上进行训练。而要收集教会机器人识别玻璃杯、拿起并倒入咖啡所需的数据则困难得多。它必须理解从空间位置到玻璃杯的脆性以及内容物的粘度等一切信息。这些信息必须从现实世界的经验中挖掘，或者从这些经验的详细重现中获取。

目前，人形机器人获取的是混杂的数据，通常来自低质量的模拟。从现实世界收集的数据要有用得多，也因此备受追捧。其中最好的形式是“真实机器”数据，这类数据由机器人的行为产生，无论是由远程控制还是自行执行任务时产生的。另一个重要来源是记录人类完成任务的过程。这种被称为“第一人称视角”的数据，是通过给人配备头戴设备和触觉传感器手套来收集的，用于记录人的动作。

中国正迅速成为获取真实机器数据竞赛的领跑者，这要归功于湖北人形机器人创新中心等训练中心。在中国中部城市武汉的这座庞大设施中，多达100台机器人每天花数小时观察和模仿人类。在中心数十个工位之一，一名年轻男子站在柜台后，戴着数字传感器手套的手做圆周运动，仿佛在往咖啡上倒热水。他身旁站着一台机器人，模仿他的动作，但真的在用壶往咖啡漏斗里倒水。机器人手腕和手指的移动方式像人一样令人毛骨悚然。离这个临时咖啡馆几步远的地方是一个看起来像药房的区域，那里有一对人和机器人在整理货架。旁边是便利店，然后是工厂流水线，接着是工作生活中各种其他场景。（此前外国记者不被允许参观这些中心。）

据咨询公司Interact Analysis的数据，武汉的这个中心是中国为获取人类动作秘密而专门建造的53个设施之一。大多数是在过去两年建成的。另有34个正在建设或规划中。上海正在建设的一个设施将为1000台人形机器人提供空间。沿海的浙江省一个小城市已经有六个训练中心。

训练中心需要大量空间和许多人来运营，还需要一批尖端人形机器人，每台造价可达10万元人民币（合1.5万美元）。汇丰银行的分析师Corey Chan估计，这种训练每小时成本为500至700元人民币。在咖啡馆工位，机器人每倒八小时咖啡，只产生三小时有用数据。其余数据被浪费，主要是由于工程师不希望机器人学习的错误。据估计，要开发一款能执行各种日常任务的机器人，需要1亿小时的训练数据。

对于像宇树科技这样的中国企业来说，幸运的是政府承担了这些中心的大部分费用。大约五分之四的训练中心有政府支持。许多是私人企业和地方政府之间的合作，地方政府购买人形机器人，并将产生的数据卖回给企业。估计各不相同，但在2026年上半年，中国制造的人形机器人中可能有多达70%进入了这些训练中心，总计约1.3万台。通过建设训练中心，地方官员可以支撑其所在地区制造的机器人的需求。

与此同时，中国的机器人制造商正在寻找从该国庞大的制造业劳动力以及许多失业年轻人那里收集自我中心数据的方法。一个为这一目的制造可穿戴设备的小型产业已经兴起。一些人希望年轻人可以在家中穿戴这些装备作为兼职工作。电子商务公司京东今年早些时候表示，它已启动一个项目，将支付其10万名员工和另外50万人来追踪他们自己的动作。据报道，在另一个沿海省份江苏的一个设施中，该公司支付报酬让人们模拟杂货店和其他环境慢慢执行任务。未来两年，它打算收集1000万小时捕捉真实世界人类场景的数据。

由于人类肢体和机器人肢体在现实世界中的运动方式不同，人类产生的自我中心数据远不如真实的机器数据有用。而且即使是有用的部分也必须进行标注，需要人工手动标记每个动作，以便机器人知道该动作适用于哪个肢体或动作。尽管如此，中国通过雇佣大批人来标注图片，在图像识别技术上获得了早期优势。一位在机器人公司工作的工程师表示，中国可能会再次这样做。

### **如何训练你的机器人**

中国的人形机器人产业不同于该国大多数蓬勃发展的制造业。未来几年，它将生产并销售数十万台昂贵但大多无用的机器，希望未来能制造出更便宜且有用的机器。美国竞争对手一直依赖成本更低的训练方法。据说美国最大的人形机器人生产商特斯拉正在自己的仓库中训练机器人，这节省了建造独立训练设施的费用。多家公司一直在使用人类运动的视频来训练其人形机器人。而且，由于如今美国几乎没有工厂工人可以分发装备用于自我中心数据收集，据报道，一些公司正在支付比中国工资更低的贫穷国家的工人，让他们在工作中佩戴头盔和触觉手套。

仍然，与计算机视觉一样，中国似乎几乎肯定会在数据收集方面保持领先。但代价是什么？鉴于技术的现状以及改进所需的时间，人形机器人的商业市场在本十年内不太可能出现。银行摩根士丹利估计，到2030年，中国每年可能生产约45万个。其中许多仍将愚笨——并继续撞墙。

## China will struggle to make money from humanoid robots

## China will struggle to make money from humanoid robots

### It is ahead of rivals and training up thousands, but profits are a long way off

Photograph: Xinhua/Shutterstock

China’s humanoid robots are sprinting ahead of their rivals—quite literally. On August 17th one of their number, called Superman, clocked up a running speed of 12.66 metres per second, beating the previous record set by Usain Bolt, before crashing into a wall. The triumph only added to the buzz over Unitree, Superman’s maker, which debuted its shares in Shanghai two days later. Their price leapt by 460%.

More impressive displays are expected over the coming days as the Humanoid Robot Games take place in Beijing. Yet as the ignominious end of Superman’s running feat demonstrates, the technology still has a long way to go. Chinese companies are expected to sell 50,000 humanoids this year, more than triple last year’s figure. They have largely mastered the hardware behind the machines. The software, however, is another matter.

In order to create the foundation models that will allow robots to seamlessly carry out a multitude of useful tasks, enormous amounts of data must now be collected. The models powering the current generation of humanoids possess a few billion "parameters"; to replicate the functioning of the human body, they will need hundreds of billions.

That would put them at a similar size to the large language models powering chatbots. The difference is that chatbots can be trained on the reams of digital text available on the internet. Gathering the data necessary to teach a robot to identify a glass, pick it up and fill it with coffee is much harder. It must understand everything from its spatial position to the brittleness of the glass and the viscosity of its contents. That information must be mined from experiences in the real world, or detailed recreations of those experiences.

Humanoids are currently fed a hotchpotch of data, often from low-quality simulations. Data collected from the real world are far more useful—and now highly sought after. The best form of these is "real machine" data, which are generated by the actions of a robot, either when it is controlled remotely or when it is carrying out tasks by itself. Another important source comes from recording humans as they complete tasks. Known as "egocentric" data, this information is gathered by equipping a human with a headset and haptic-sensor gloves that record a person’s movements.

China is quickly becoming the frontrunner in the quest for real machine data, thanks to training centres such as the Hubei Humanoid Innovation Centre. At the sprawling facility in the central Chinese city of Wuhan, up to 100 robots spend hours each day observing and mimicking humans. At one of the dozens of work stations in the centre, a young man behind a counter is moving his hand, gloved in digital sensors, in a circular motion as if he were pouring hot water over coffee. Next to him stands a robot that is mimicking his motion but spouting real water from a kettle over a funnel of coffee. The manner in which the bot’s wrist and fingers move are creepily human. A few steps from the makeshift café is what looks like a pharmacy, where another human-robot duo are stocking shelves. Next is a convenience store, then a factory line followed by various other scenes from working life. (Until now foreign journalists have not been allowed to visit these centres.)

The centre in Wuhan is one of 53 facilities in China built especially for gleaning the secrets of human movement, according to data from Interact Analysis, a consulting firm. Most were created in the past two years. Another 34 are under construction or planned. One being constructed in Shanghai will have space for 1,000 humanoids. A small city in coastal Zhejiang province already has six training centres.

Training centres require a large amount of space and many people to run them, as well as a legion of cutting-edge humanoids, which can run to 100,000 ($15,000) yuan apiece. This form of training costs 500-700 yuan per hour, reckons Corey Chan of HSBC, a bank. For every eight hours of coffee-pouring, the droid at the café station produces just three hours of useful data. The rest is spoiled, mostly by errors that engineers do not want the robots to learn. According to one estimate, 100m training hours will be required to develop a robot that can perform a wide variety of daily tasks.

Fortunately for Chinese businesses like Unitree, the government has been picking up much of the tab for the centres. Around four-fifths are state-backed. Many are collaborations between private enterprises and local governments, which buy humanoids and sell the data produced back to the companies. Estimates vary but in the first half of 2026 up to 70% of the humanoids made in China may have gone to these training centres, adding up to around 13,000 machines. By building one, local officials can shore up demand for robots that are being manufactured in their area.

At the same time Chinese robot-makers are finding ways of collecting egocentric data from the country’s large manufacturing workforce—as well as its many unemployed youngsters. A cottage industry has emerged of vendors making wearable gear for that purpose. Some hope young people will wear the kit while at home as a part-time job. JD.com, an e-commerce company, said earlier this year that it has launched a project to pay 100,000 of its staff and 500,000 others to track their own movements. At a facility in Jiangsu, another coastal province, which has reportedly received state funding, the company pays people to slowly carry out tasks in fake grocery stores and other environments. Over the next two years it intends to gather 10m hours of data capturing real-world human scenarios.

Egocentric data generated by humans are far less useful than real machine data, owing to the differences in how human limbs and robotic ones move in the real world. And even the bits that are useful must be labelled, requiring a human to tag each movement manually so that a bot knows to which limb or action it applies. Still, China gained an early edge in image-recognition technology by employing large groups of people to tag pictures. It could do the same again, says an engineer who works for a robotics company.

### **How to train your droid**

China’s humanoid industry is unlike most other manufacturing businesses that have thrived in the country. Over the next few years it is set to produce and sell hundreds of thousands of costly but mostly useless machines in the hope that it can make cheaper and useful ones in the future. American competitors have been relying on less expensive training methods. Tesla, America’s biggest producer of humanoids, is said to be training robots in its own warehouses, which saves constructing standalone training facilities. Various companies have been using videos of human movement to train their humanoids. And since these days America has few factory workers who can be handed gear for egocentric collection, a number of firms are reportedly paying workers in poor countries, where wages are lower than in China, to don headsets and haptic gloves as they work.

Still, as with computer vision, China seems almost certain to maintain the lead in data collection. But at what cost? Given the current state of the technology, and the time that will be required to improve it, a commercial market for humanoids is unlikely to emerge this decade. Morgan Stanley, a bank, reckons that China could be producing about 450,000 of them a year by 2030. Many will remain dim-witted—and continue charging into walls.
