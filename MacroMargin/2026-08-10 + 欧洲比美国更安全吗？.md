> 📌 **核心摘要**
>
> 通过比较20个富裕城市调整后犯罪数据，研究发现欧洲并非全面比美国安全，芝加哥谋杀率最高，但其他犯罪类别差异不明显，且两地总体趋势向好。
> • 数据集由UCL犯罪学家Matt Ashby整理，统一城市边界与犯罪定义，覆盖20个人口≥250万城市。
> • 2024年芝加哥谋杀率22/10万，为数据中最高，是洛杉矶（次高）的2.7倍，欧洲十城均值仅1.7/10万。
> • 持刀抢劫在两地发生率相近，伦敦巴黎最严重（>300/10万）；车辆盗窃芝加哥洛杉矶多，纽约优于多数欧洲城市；入室盗窃欧洲更常见。
> • 官方数据存在报告偏差，如英格兰威尔士强奸统计翻倍但调查显示持平，反映报告率上升。
> • 趋势向好：伦敦纽约谋杀率历史最低，巴黎抢劫十年降半，城市总体上比上一代更安全。

### 中文译文

## 欧洲比美国更安全吗？

**一组新颖的数据集提供了一些令人惊讶的答案**

欧洲城市充斥着持刀的无礼青年，他们会为了抢你的手机而刺你。美国到处都是持枪的歹徒和无法无天的ICE特工。这类陈词滥调在社交媒体上很常见——大西洋两岸都认为对方无法无天。

真相是什么？比较各国的犯罪统计数据是出了名的棘手。个人报案倾向因地而异，定义也很少能完全对齐。犯罪率还对人口分母敏感：如果一个城市包含大片腹地（例如纽约的斯塔滕岛），可能让其他地区（例如布朗克斯）看起来比实际更安全。

由伦敦大学学院犯罪学家Matt Ashby及其同事整理的一组新颖数据集试图克服这些问题。首先，他们选取了20个人口至少250万的富裕世界城市，这些城市均发布年度犯罪统计数据。接着，他们调整了边界，使所有城市的人口密度相近（例如，巴黎包括整个法兰西岛大区，而不仅仅是巴黎市）。最后，他们统一了定义，使犯罪类别与英国大致可比。研究结果比网络键盘侠预想的更为细致。

谋杀，或者更准确地说，杀人罪（包括过失杀人），并不是衡量街头安全的完美指标：你更可能被认识的人杀害。但它是跨国可比性最强的统计数字，因为尸体就是尸体。基于此，芝加哥是数据中迄今为止最致命的城市。2024年，该市发生587起杀人案，相当于每10万居民22起，是次高城市洛杉矶的2.7倍。十个欧洲城市的平均比率为每10万人1.7起。

然而，在其他犯罪类别上，分歧并不那么明显。涉及使用武力的个人抢劫在两个地区发生的频率大致相当，尽管伦敦和巴黎的情况尤其糟糕，每年每10万人中超过300起。车辆盗窃在芝加哥和洛杉矶很常见，但纽约的表现优于大多数欧洲城市。与此同时，入室盗窃在欧洲更为常见。一方面，欧洲慷慨的社会安全网可能会减少人们偷窃的动机。另一方面，美国强劲的警务力量——以及高得多的监禁率——可能有助于拉平差距。

阿什比先生表示，这些统计数据是让数据具有可比性的“最不坏的尝试”。他和同事开发这些数据，是为了让政策制定者能够就国家间的趋势提出“详细的问题”。报告差异仍然是一个大问题。英格兰和威尔士的官方强奸数据在过去十年中翻了一番。但保密调查显示，案件数量保持稳定：增长似乎是由于更多人报警和更完善的犯罪报告机制。同样，阿什比先生说，伦敦记录在案的商业抢劫案可能有所增加，因为警方让此类犯罪的报告变得更加容易。

尽管国家间的比较很困难，但大西洋两岸的趋势都提供了乐观的理由。伦敦和纽约的谋杀率处于有记录以来的最低水平。巴黎的个人抢劫案可能看起来很高，但过去十年几乎减少了一半。总体趋势是，欧洲和美国的城市都比上一代人时更安全、更友好、更有趣。这是每个人都应该能够庆祝的事情。

### English Original

## Is Europe safer than America?

**A novel dataset provides some surprising answers**

European cities are awash with knife-wielding rude boys who will stab you just to snatch your phone. America is full of gun-toting gangsters and rogue ICE agents. Such clichés are common on social media—each side of the Atlantic Ocean thinks the other is lawless.

What is the truth? Comparing crime statistics across countries is notoriously tricky. Individuals’ propensity to report crime varies from place to place, and definitions rarely align neatly. Crime rates are also sensitive to population denominators: if a city includes a large hinterland (eg, Staten Island in New York) that can make other parts (eg, the Bronx) appear safer than they are.

A novel dataset curated by Matt Ashby, a criminologist at University College London, along with colleagues, attempts to overcome these problems. First, they selected 20 rich-world cities with a population of at least 2.5m, all of which publish annual crime statistics. Next, they adjusted boundaries so all had similar population densities (for example, Paris includes the whole Île-de-France region rather than just the city of Paris). Last, they harmonised definitions so categories of crime were broadly comparable to Britain’s. The findings are more nuanced than a keyboard warrior might expect.

Murder or, more precisely, homicide (which includes manslaughter) is an imperfect measure of street safety: you are most likely to be killed by someone you know. But it is the statistic that travels best across borders, since a dead body is a dead body. On this basis, Chicago is by far the most deadly city in the data. In 2024 it had 587 homicides, a rate of 22 per 100,000 residents, or 2.7 times that of the next-worst offender, Los Angeles. The average rate among ten European cities is 1.7 per 100,000 people.

There is less of a clear divide in other categories of crime, however. Personal robberies with a use of force happen about as often in both regions, even if London and Paris do especially badly with a rate of more than 300 per 100,000 people each year. Vehicle thefts are common in Chicago and Los Angeles, but New York does better than most European cities. Meanwhile, domestic burglaries are more common in Europe. On the one hand, a generous social-safety net in Europe may reduce the incentive for people to steal. On the other, America’s robust policing—and far higher incarceration rates—could help even things out.

Mr Ashby says the statistics are the “least-worst attempt” to make data comparable. He and his colleagues developed them so that policymakers could ask “detailed questions” about trends between countries. Reporting differences remain a big problem. Official figures for rape in England and Wales have doubled in the past ten years. But confidential surveys suggest cases have held steady: the increase appears to be owing to more people telling the police and better crime reporting. Similarly, Mr Ashby says recorded business robberies in London may have risen because the police have made it easier to report such crimes.

Although comparisons across countries are hard, trends on both sides of the pond provide reasons for optimism. Murder rates in London and New York are lower than at any time since record-keeping began. Personal robbery in Paris may look high, but it has almost halved over the past decade. The broad trend is that cities in both Europe and America are safer, friendlier and more fun than a generation ago. That is something everyone should be able to celebrate.
