### 中文译文

## 当遵守美国法律意味着违反中国法律

数十年以来，美国一直试图将其意志强加于在世界各地开展业务的公司——借助一只漫长而强大的域外手臂。事实上，这正是其针对伊朗的新经济战的基础，[本周启动](https://www.economist.com/leaders/2026/08/27/americas-new-sanctions-are-unlikely-to-topple-irans-regime)。然而如今，国际公司还必须担心另一只长臂。这只手臂属于中国。

在习近平主席领导下，世界第二大经济体正迅速向最大的经济体学习。其目标之一是反击美国的压力。但中国也在集结武器以扩展自身影响力。正如将他人排除在全球金融体系之外的威胁是美国的超级力量，中国也有自己的超级力量：其世界领先的供应链。公司面临一个新的现实：可能被夹在两把锤子之间。

本十年内制定并于四月加强的法律赋予中国政府权力，[惩罚遵守外国制裁的公司](https://www.economist.com/china/2026/08/27/the-long-arm-of-the-chinese-state)。如果公司无法同时遵守美国和中国的法律，它们将陷入困境。美国两家大型银行摩根大通和花旗集团在中国法院被起诉，索赔金额达数千万美元。它们被指控遵从美国财政部的命令，避免与被列入黑名单的中国实体开展业务。其他国家的公司也被卷入。六月，中国最高法院援引新法律裁定一家新加坡公司败诉，该公司拒绝为一家受制裁的香港机构运输电子产品。

其他因素也可能引发麻烦。四月，中国反对人工智能初创公司Manus被美国最强大的公司之一Meta收购。尽管该公司数月前已迁至新加坡试图逃避中国监管，中国仍下令撤销这笔20亿美元的交易。中国没有法律权威命令该交易解除。尽管Meta的Facebook在中国境内被禁止，但中国公司在该平台上花费数百亿美元投放广告。尽管如此，Meta还是服从了。禁止Manus的中国创始人离开祖国，似乎有助于让相关方面认真对待。

中国针对企业的武器库正在壮大。当局正在制定规则，旨在让他们对中国制造的人工智能技术的流动拥有更多控制权。9月15日，新的权力将生效，对人员实施出入境禁令。该党意图使此类立法具有广泛范围，既震慑批评者，也将意志强加于海外公司。

对于一家典型的跨国公司——在纽约设有银行、在深圳拥有供应链——这真是一条棘手的道路。中国在全球制造业中的主导地位赋予其巨大的影响力。违反中国规则的公司除了面临民事处罚和罚款外，还可能被没收资产，并被列入与中国合作伙伴合作的黑名单。

中国过去在利用莫须有的罪名拘留员工（无论是中国籍还是外籍）方面很少有所顾忌。两大经济体之间的法律角力在8月24日进一步升级，当时美国财政部长斯科特·贝森特以帮助伊朗出售石油和采购两用技术为由，对数十名中国个人和企业实施制裁。中国警告称可能进行报复。

跨国公司必须在一个它们被夹在中间的世界中应对。遵守美国法律意味着违反中国法律，反之亦然。美国的措施透明，可在独立法院审查，并受法律约束。而在中国，法律模糊且宽泛，法院听命于共产党。高管们面临胁迫的风险——以及消失在拘留中的风险。

企业该如何准备？许多企业已将中国业务剥离为独立的法人实体，或彻底关停。它们可以让最有价值的数据远离中国，限制敏感技术的转让，并确保中国以外的运营不依赖于在中国的管理人员或资产。供应链应具备足够的韧性，以防止任何一个国家导致业务停摆。

### **法外之徒的长臂**

然而，面对一个无视这些防火墙的政府，企业防火墙的作用有限。企业将发现自己踮着脚尖穿过雷区，决定该遵守谁的法律，并屈从于威胁造成最大伤害的国家。商业世界继续分裂为相互竞争的阵营。■

### English Original

## When obeying an American law means breaking a Chinese one

For decades America has sought to impose its will on companies doing business around the world—using a long, powerful, extraterritorial arm. Indeed, that is the basis of its new economic war against Iran, [launched this week](https://www.economist.com/leaders/2026/08/27/americas-new-sanctions-are-unlikely-to-topple-irans-regime). These days, however, international companies also have to worry about another long arm. This one belongs to China.

Under President Xi Jinping, the world’s second-biggest economy is fast learning from the biggest. One of its goals is to hit back against American pressure. But China is also mustering its weapons in order to extend its own influence. Just as the threat of excluding others from the global financial system is America’s superpower, so China has its own: its world-beating supply chains. Companies face a new reality, of potentially being caught between two hammers.

Laws written this decade and beefed up in April give the Chinese state the power to [punish firms for complying with foreign sanctions](https://www.economist.com/china/2026/08/27/the-long-arm-of-the-chinese-state). If companies cannot obey both America and China at the same time, they will be in a bind. Two big American banks, JPMorgan Chase and Citigroup, are being sued in Chinese courts for tens of millions of dollars. They are accused of following orders from America’s Treasury Department to avoid business with blacklisted Chinese entities. Other countries’ firms are caught up, too. In June China’s top court cited the new laws in ruling against a Singaporean firm for refusing to transport electronics for a Hong Kong outfit under sanctions.

Other factors can also cause trouble. In April China objected to an AI startup, Manus, being taken over by Meta, one of America’s most powerful companies. It ordered the $2bn deal to be unwound even though the firm had moved to Singapore months earlier to try to escape Chinese regulation. China had no legal authority to order the deal dissolved. Though Meta’s Facebook is banned inside China, Chinese firms spend tens of billions of dollars advertising on the platform. Still, Meta complied. Barring Manus’s Chinese founders from leaving their native country seems to have helped focus minds.

The Chinese armoury against corporations is growing. Authorities are working on rules designed to give them more control over the flows of Chinese-made AI technology. On September 15th new powers will come into effect that impose entry and exit bans on people. The party intends such legislation to have broad scope, intimidating its critics as well as imposing its will on firms abroad.

For a typical multinational, which banks in New York and has supply chains in Shenzhen, this is a tricky path to tread. China’s dominant role in global manufacturing gives it immense clout. As well as facing civil penalties and fines, firms that fall foul of China’s rules can have assets seized and be blacklisted from working with Chinese partners.

China has in the past shown few scruples about using trumped-up charges to detain staff, whether Chinese or foreign. The legal tussles between the two biggest economic powers ratcheted higher on August 24th, when Scott Bessent, America’s treasury secretary, imposed sanctions on dozens of Chinese individuals and businesses for helping Iran sell its oil and procure dual-use technologies. China warned that it could retaliate.

Multinationals have to cope in a world where they are caught in the middle. Obeying American laws will mean breaking Chinese ones, and vice versa. America’s measures are transparent, reviewable in independent courts and constrained by law. In China the laws are vague and sweeping and the courts do the Communist Party’s bidding. Executives are at risk of coercion—and of disappearing into custody.

How can firms prepare? Many have hived off their Chinese operations into separate legal entities, or shut them down entirely. They can keep their most valuable data clear of China, restrict transfers of sensitive technology and ensure that operations outside China do not depend on executives or assets in the country. Supply chains should be resilient enough to prevent any single country from bringing business to a halt.

### **The long arm of the lawless**

Yet corporate firewalls are of limited use against a government that ignores them. Businesses will find themselves tip-toeing through minefields, deciding whose law to obey and deferring to whichever country threatens the most harm. The fracturing of the world of commerce into rival blocs continues. ■
