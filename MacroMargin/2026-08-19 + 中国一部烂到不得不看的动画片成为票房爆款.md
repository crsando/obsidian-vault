### 中文译文

## 中国一部烂到不得不看的动画片成为票房爆款

一部关于小牛的低成本动画片在中国成为出人意料的票房热门，将网上的嘲笑转化为数百万美元的电影票销售，并引发了在精美AI生成娱乐时代对粗糙动画价值的争论。

*《牛来》*，一部由自学成才的电影制作人及其母亲制作的86分钟电影，上映头10天仅收入7000元人民币（1038美元）。据票务平台猫眼数据，截至周二，该片在中国单日票房排名第五，累计票房超过2000万元人民币，预计总票房有望达到1.35亿元人民币。

这部卡通的恶名帮助传播了口碑，并为观众做好了心理准备，许多人预期这将是一部制作拙劣的奇观。周二下午，在北京闹市区一家爆满的影院里，观众们原本做好了心理准备，但最终真的度过了一段愉快时光。

“我在网上看到帖子，来这儿就是为了笑一笑，”一名只透露姓氏为白的16岁高中生说道。“它确实非常欢乐。故事逻辑也完全说得通！”

这部电影的片名译为“过来，牛”，讲述一只名叫牛来的新生小牛，学习友谊、信任、责任和牺牲的故事，通常他的母亲陪伴在身边。

这段旅程最终原来是一场梦。在圆满的结局中，牛来的母亲把小牛介绍给了他的父亲。

当观众对着屏幕拍照或简要议论剧情时，无人抱怨。动画画面摇晃，对白缓慢，但观众大多是年轻人，气氛欢快，所有人都坚持看到了最后。

据当地媒体报道，《牛来了》不可思议的票房致富之路始于五年前，当时34岁、受过景观建筑师训练的辛雨萌与在国企工作的母亲一起开始创作这部作品。

由于缺乏任何相关资质或经验，两人自己边学边掌握了所需的新技能，从动画技术、编剧到配音。母亲孙丽芳不仅编写了剧本，还创作了片尾曲，其忧伤的曲调和歌词触动了观众的心弦。

这段幕后故事更让中国公众对这部动画心生好感。一些人在网上发帖指出，动画的手工质感在人工智能时代展现出一种罕见的真诚。

但在一些观众在社交媒体上分享那些怪异的牛形象后，它才真正开始流行起来。

有时，最初的回应充满轻蔑，近乎愤慨，质疑为什么如此粗糙的产品竟能获得国家主管部门的院线上映许可。一些人甚至怀疑这部电影是为了洗钱或获取政府补贴而拍的。

最严厉的评论之一是：“这不仅是劣币驱逐良币，而是冥币”——即烧给祖先的纸钱——“驱逐假钞。”

后来，潮流突然逆转，最初的嘲讽让位于好奇，网上对乡土角色的表情包化也起到了推波助澜的作用。许多人只是想看一眼这部电影到底有多烂。

它也在更多小众受众中引起了共鸣。

例如，一些中国选股者抓住了名为“宝拉”的豹子角色，其名字是“爆炸性上涨”的谐音，或者将牛卡通视为牛市的先兆。其他人从小牛呼喊妈妈的画面中找到了隐喻，认为这代表了向央行求助。

事实上，这部电影出人意料地成功已经开始波及股市，投资者纷纷涌入名称中包含“牛”字的公司。河北金牛化工和罗牛山的股价本周一度分别上涨21%和26%。北京拓新信息科技（Beijing Topnew Info&Tech）的中文名听起来像“通牛”，周三一度上涨5%，随后逆转，跌幅一度达9.6%。

更多粉丝创作的内容以表情包的形式出现，迅速刷屏社交媒体。由于这是一部低成本作品，没有官方海报，一些影院[紧急制作](https://x.com/zengying1107/status/2089369328286376150?s=46)手绘海报来吸引观众。

虽然《牛来》无法与中国喜剧《中东往事》和克里斯托弗·诺兰的《奥德赛》等高票房电影的商业成功相媲美，但其低制作成本仍可能使其成为全球第二大电影市场有史以来最赚钱的电影之一。

在中国类似Instagram的社交媒体平台小红书上，一位用户甚至将其与马塞尔·杜尚的作品相提并论。杜尚是概念艺术的先驱，他曾将一个小便池放在底座上并称之为雕塑，引发争议。

“*《牛来》*无疑是当代动画产业在美学上的背叛，”该人士写道。“它的粗糙不是妥协——它标志着主流审美的马塞尔·杜尚（Marcel Duchamp）时刻。”

### English Original

## Chinese Cartoon So Bad You Have to See It Becomes Box Office Hit

A low-budget cartoon about a calf has become an unlikely box-office sensation in China, turning online ridicule into millions of dollars in ticket sales and sparking a debate over the merits of crude animation in the age of polished AI-generated entertainment.

*Niu Lai*, an 86-minute production made by a self-taught filmmaker and his mother, earned just 7,000 yuan ($1,038) during its first 10 days in theaters. By Tuesday, the film ranked fifth at China’s daily box office with cumulative sales exceeding 20 million yuan, according to ticketing platform Maoyan, putting it on track to reach 135 million yuan in total revenue.

The notoriety of the cartoon helped spread the word and prepared viewers for what many expected to be a poorly made spectacle. At a packed theater hall in downtown Beijing on Tuesday afternoon, moviegoers braced themselves but ended up having a genuinely good time.

“I saw the online posts and came for the laughter,” said a 16-year-old high-school student who gave only his surname, Bai. “It is indeed very cheerful. The logic of the story also makes perfect sense!”

The film, whose title translates as “Come Here, Cow,” follows a newborn calf named Niu Lai as he learns about friendship, trust, responsibility and sacrifice, usually with his mother at his side.

The journey ultimately turns out to be a dream. In the happy ending, Niu Lai’s mother introduces the calf to his father.

Nobody complained when viewers photographed the screen or briefly chatted about the plot. The animation was shaky and the dialogue slow, but the audience of mostly young people was full of good cheer and everyone stuck around to the end.

*Niu Lai*’s improbable path to box-office riches began five years ago, according to local media reports, when Xin Yumeng, 34, a landscape architect by training, started to work on the project together with his mother, who’s an employee at a state-owned company.

Lacking any relevant qualifications or experience, the two picked up the new skills they needed on their own, from animation techniques and playwriting to voice acting. The mother, Sun Lifang, not only wrote the screenplay but also the ending theme song, whose melancholic tune and lyrics have touched a nerve among audiences.

That backstory did more to endear the cartoon to the Chinese public. Some noted in online posts that the handmade feel of the animation offered a rare display of sincerity in the AI age.

But it really began to catch on after some viewers shared the grotesque cow images on social media.

At times, the initial response was full of scorn bordering on indignation over why a product so crudely made was cleared for theatrical release by the national authorities. Some even wondered if the film was made to launder money or get government subsidies.

“This is not just bad money driving out the good,” read one of the harshest comments. “It’s ghost money” — or bills burned as an offering to ancestors — “driving out fake notes.”

Then suddenly the tide began to turn, as early ridicule gave way to curiosity, helped along by the memeification online of the rustic characters. Many just wanted a peek at how bad the film really is.

It’s also struck a chord with more niche audiences.

Some Chinese stock pickers, for example, latched onto a leopard character named Bao La, whose name is a homonym for “explosive rally,” or took the cow cartoon as a harbinger of a bull market. Others found a metaphor in the image of the calf crying out for his mother and concluded it represented an appeal for help from the central bank.

And indeed, the film’s unlikely success has started to spill into the stock market, with investors piling into companies whose names contain the Chinese character for cow. Shares of [Hebei Jinniu Chemical](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/600722%20CH%20Equity) and [Luoniushan](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/000735%20CH%20Equity) have surged as much as 21% and 26% this week. Beijing Topnew Info&Tech, whose Chinese name sounds like “Tong Niu,” rose 5% before reversing course and falling as much as 9.6% Wednesday.

More fan-created content has materialized in the form of emojis that quickly flooded the social media. Given it was a low budget operation and no official posters were available, some cinemas [rushed out](https://x.com/zengying1107/status/2089369328286376150?s=46) hand-drawn posters to attract viewers.

While *Niu Lai* is no match for the commercial success of such top-grossing movies as the Chinese comedy *Once Upon a Time in the Middle East *and* *Christopher Nolan’s *The Odyssey*, its low production costs could still make it one of the most profitable films ever in the world’s second-largest film market.

On the Chinese Instagram-like social media platform Xiaohongshu, one user even compared it with the oeuvre of Marcel Duchamp, a pioneer of conceptual art who scandalously put a urinal on a pedestal and called it a sculpture.

“*Niu Lai* is no doubt an aesthetic betrayal of the contemporary animation industry,” the person wrote. “Its coarseness is not a compromise — it marks a Marcel Duchamp moment for mainstream taste.”
