### 中文译文

## 新世界秩序？人人为己

![© Ann Kiernan](https://images.ft.com/v3/image/raw/ftcms%3Ae2e9718c-859f-4351-b325-380a968ca869?source=next-article&fit=scale-down&quality=highest&width=1440&dpr=1)

既然美国在倡导民主方面已经挂冠，那么对暴君们来说就是春天。

科菲·安南有一个最喜欢的驳斥，用来回应那些试图辩称独裁者可能是弱小或混乱国家的正确答案的人。他会说：“那是尊尼获加在说话……”，回忆起他在加纳成长时，每当有人做出离奇断言，他和朋友们就会这样回应。他认为独裁政权“建立在沙土之上”；培育国家的唯一途径是良政；你必须一直努力。

十五年前，我曾在一次坦桑尼亚之行中听到这位已故的联合国秘书长温和地发表这句“尊尼获加”式的反驳。当时，冷战后关于希望的目的论已经走到尽头。然而，一股充满渴望的民主之风吹遍了发展中世界的许多地区。

安南如今会多么沮丧。当然，威权主义在中国和俄罗斯等大国中已经抬头了十多年，在“中等强国”中也是如此，从土耳其和海湾国家到印度，以及更近期且相当迅速地，印度尼西亚也是如此。

但既然美国已经决定在倡导民主方面挂冠，那么暴君们就迎来了更广阔的春天。从津巴布韦到缅甸，暴虐的威权政体似乎被怂恿，几乎可以为所欲为，甚至不怕受到言辞上的谴责。

至于坦桑尼亚，当局刚刚暂停了一家报纸的发行，因为它胆敢报道政府内部可能出现的紧张。十年前，这样的行为会招致西方捐助者的压力。对于不容异己的政客们来说，能够无所顾忌地压制媒体而不用害怕制裁，这该是何等畅快……

有些人可能忍不住反驳说，这并不新鲜：美国什么时候成为过美德的典范？毕竟，它一向自私自利、虚伪——一如多年来所有主导大国那样。

今年1月，加拿大总理马克·卡尼将美国领导的“基于规则的全球秩序”抨击为“美好的虚构”时，他的批评在全球南方尤其受到欢迎。那里的许多人长期以来一直怨恨西方的自相矛盾。控方证据A：冷战时期的扎伊尔独裁者蒙博托·塞塞·塞科。他是个窃贼统治之王，然而他和其他独裁者却被多位美国总统当作反苏的棋子加以娇惯。

然而，尽管为美国在世界舞台上的记录辩护并不流行，但这并非全貌。1945年后的几十年里，许多饱受围困的反对党和新闻机构依靠美国国务院及其机构的支持才得以维持。1990年代初，当美国政府资助的自由亚洲电台记者前往安哥拉——一个向世界开放的一党制国家——时，他们被誉为传奇人物，因为他们的广播产生了巨大影响。

但那是过去。去年，特朗普政府关闭了自由亚洲电台。民主推广已从美国议程上消失。上个月，津巴布韦总统埃默森·姆南加古瓦的盟友违宪强行通过新法律以延长其任期时，美国没有发出任何抗议之声。其他西方国家似乎也失去了勇气。英国和法国专注于津巴布韦的债务减免，却对权力攫取只字不提。

这一退缩以及更广泛的软实力撤资所节省的财政开支只是四舍五入的误差罢了。但对反对党来说，影响却是灾难性的。这也是短视的。北京正在填补软实力真空。

在津巴布韦，姆南加古瓦的支持者呼吁他作为事实上的终身总统继续留任。他刚刚提拔了其中10人担任参议员。据说他从特朗普过去随口提议他可能寻求第三个任期的话语中获得了勇气。就这样，暴政扎下了根。

有些人绝望地——且不准确地——谈论回归“丛林法则”。当英国帝国主义的编年史家鲁德亚德·吉卜林创造这个短语作为一首诗的标题时，那是一种赞美。他是在颂扬狼群协作的方式；他的丛林法则关乎社群，而非混乱。“因为群体的力量来自狼，”他写道，“而狼的力量来自群体。”

卡尼在谈到中等国家需要共同努力以保持多边主义存活时，隐晦地援引了这种“群体”精神作为灵感来源。有时这将会发生。诸边主义——在特定问题上组成的临时联盟——的理念正赢得越来越多拥护者，灵活的诸边主义（无论它意味着什么）也是如此。

但总体而言，全球时代精神是“各人自扫门前雪”——或者说，是的，就是通常理解的丛林法则。这是一个你死我活的时代，你可以无情地追求目标。联合国官员绝望地谈到，多达10个国家在为苏丹战争火上浇油，而所有这些国家都确信自己不会被追究责任。

支持美国不干预做法的人谈到这是姗姗来迟的回归现实主义。他们说世界已经变了。确实如此。但美国对传播启蒙理想的兴趣终结，代价高昂。

安南会知道如何回应那些试图为普遍存在的对民主价值的冷嘲热讽式的漠视辩护的人。他会平静但坚定地嘲笑这种想法，说：“那是约翰尼·沃克在说话。”他当然是对的。

### English Original

## The new world order? Every man for himself

Now that America has hung up its hat when it comes to championing democracy, it is springtime for tyrants

Kofi Annan had a favourite put-down for people who tried to argue that an autocrat could be the right answer for a weak or troubled state. “That’s Johnnie Walker talking . . . ” he would say, recalling how he and friends used to respond in Ghana when he was growing up and someone made an outlandish claim. Autocracies were “built on sand”, he believed; the only way to nurture a nation was via good governance; you just had to keep working at it.

I heard the late UN secretary-general deliver this gentle “Johnnie Walker” rejoinder on a tour of Tanzania 15 years ago. It was a time when the post-cold war teleology of hope had run its course. And yet an aspirant breeze of democracy was blowing across much of the developing world.

How dispirited Annan would be now. Authoritarianism has of course been on the rise in great powers, such as China and Russia, for over a decade, and in “middle powers”, from Turkey and the Gulf to India, and more recently and quite rapidly Indonesia too.

But now that America has decided to hang up its hat when it comes to championing democracy, there is a broader springtime for tyrants. From Zimbabwe to Myanmar, thuggish autocracies seem emboldened to do pretty much what they like without fear of even a rhetorical reproof.

As for Tanzania, the authorities have just suspended a newspaper for daring to report on possible tension in the government. A decade ago such an act would have led to pressure from western donors. How liberating it must be for intolerant politicians to be able to clamp down on the media with no fear of sanction . . .

Some will be tempted to riposte that this is nothing new: since when has America been a paragon of virtuous behaviour? It has, after all, always been self-interested and hypocritical — as have all dominant powers over the years.

When Mark Carney, Canada’s prime minister, lambasted the idea of the American-led “rules-based global order” as “pleasant fiction” in January, his critique found particular favour in the global south. Many there have long resented the west’s inconsistencies. Exhibit A for the prosecution: Zaire’s dictator Mobutu Sese Seko in the cold war. He was a king of kleptocracy, yet he and other despots were cosseted by multiple US presidents as anti-Soviet pawns.

And yet, unfashionable as it is to defend America’s record on the world stage, that’s not the whole picture. Over the post-1945 decades there were many countries in which beleaguered opposition parties and news outlets were kept going by the support of the US state department and its agencies. In the early 1990s, when correspondents of the US government-funded Voice of America travelled to Angola, a one-party state opening up to the world, they were hailed as legends, such had been the impact of their broadcasts.

Well that was then. Voice of America was closed by the Trump administration last year. Democracy promotion is off the US agenda. When last month allies of Zimbabwe’s president Emmerson Mnangagwa forced through a new law to extend his term, in breach of the constitution, there was not a chirp of protest from America. Other western countries too seem to have lost their spine. Britain and France are focusing on debt relief for Zimbabwe while saying nothing about the power grab.

The financial savings from this retreat and a broader disinvestment in soft power are a rounding error. But the impact on opposition parties is dire. It is also short-sighted. Beijing is filling the soft power vacuum.

In Zimbabwe, Mnangagwa’s backers are calling for him to stay on as a de facto president-for-life. He has just promoted 10 of them to be senators. He is said to have drawn heart from Trump’s offhand past suggestion that he might seek a third term. And so a tyranny takes root.

Some talk despairingly — and inaccurately — of a return to “the law of the jungle”. When Rudyard Kipling, the chronicler of British imperialism, coined the phrase as the title of a poem, it was a compliment. He was saluting the way wolves worked together; his law of the jungle was about community, not chaos. “For the strength of the pack is the wolf,” he wrote. “And the strength of the wolf is the pack.”

Carney implicitly invoked this “pack” spirit as a source of inspiration when he talked about middle powers needing to work together to keep multilateralism alive. Sometimes this will happen. The idea of plurilateralism — ad hoc coalitions on specific issues — is gathering fans, as is flexilateralism, whatever that may mean.

But overall the global zeitgeist is “every man for himself” — or what is, yes, commonly understood as the law of the jungle. This is a cut-throat age when you can ruthlessly pursue goals. UN officials talk despairingly of how as many as 10 countries are fuelling the war in Sudan, all feeling assured of impunity.

Supporters of America’s disengaged approach talk of a long-overdue return to realism. The world has changed, they say. That is true. But the end of US interest in spreading enlightenment ideals comes at a high price.

Annan would have known how to respond to anyone seeking to defend the prevailing cynical disregard of the merits of democracy. Quietly but firmly he would have ridiculed the notion, saying: “That’s Johnnie Walker talking.” And he would, of course, have been right.
