### 中文译文

## 亚洲储蓄者涌入股市，重塑本土市场

本土投资者正在扭转亚洲市场的力量平衡。几十年来，纽约和伦敦的投资者（主要是大型机构）推动了亚洲的交易量。但亚洲不断壮大的中产阶级正在将储蓄从黄金和房地产转向股票、债券和其他金融资产，成为亚洲资本的主要来源。可支配收入的增加也起到了助推作用。

在某些情况下，政府鼓励家庭承担更多风险。印度、日本和韩国正在推动改善公司治理、提高股东回报，以扩大股票投资的吸引力。家庭踊跃响应，通过共同基金、交易所交易基金以及个股将储蓄投入股市。“一种新兴的股票文化正在形成，”新加坡里昂证券首席股票策略师亚历山大·雷德曼表示。

这一转变影响深远，因为亚洲占全球上市公司数量的56%——股票总价值达43.5万亿美元，其中包括处于人工智能热潮核心的公司。本土投资者日益崛起意味着印度、台湾、越南等地的市场对海外交易者的任性举动不再那么敏感，这些海外交易者往往在伊朗战争等危机时期撤资。“外国资金流动可能非常反复无常，”雷德曼说。（资金流动指买入减去赎回。）

然而，这一变化也带来自身风险，引发了对估值过热和投机交易的担忧。国内投资者可能进一步将资本集中于本土龙头企业和热门行业，可能使市场更容易遭受突然逆转。“一旦情绪转变，每个人都可能试图挤过同一扇窄门，”盛宝银行首席投资策略师沙鲁·查纳纳表示。在当局收紧保证金贷款规则后，中国股市在1月份下挫。这一举措表明，对由人工智能引领的涨势感到不安，该涨势曾将基准指数推至多年高点，并推动成交额达到创纪录水平。最近，韩国投资者在7月份市场历史性上涨后遭受了巨额亏损。一些散户投资者通过使用杠杆交易所交易基金加剧了自身损失，政府此后已采取措施予以遏制。

本土投资转变的规模之大难以言喻。根据金融数据提供商EPFR的数据，2022年至2025年，位于新兴亚洲经济体的基金向本国股票市场注入了5570亿美元，是此前四年投资总额的七倍多。相比之下，外国基金投入的金额下降了81%，至260亿美元。

普通投资者的影响力只会越来越大。经济合作与发展组织预计，到2030年，全球中产阶级的65%将生活在亚洲。根据大华银行2025年的一份报告，亚洲在全球私人财富中的占比在25年间从6%攀升至21%。到2029年，亚洲可能占据这一财富总量的四分之一，价值达99万亿美元。“我1999年来到亚洲时，这里资本匮乏，”法国巴黎银行亚太区现货股票研究主管威廉·布拉顿表示。“本土资本池相对较小，如果你想做点什么，在很大程度上需要获取外国资本。”

再看其他惊人的数据：印度在过去六年中超越英国和法国，成为按股票市值计算的全球第五大股票市场。台湾的证券账户数量达1450万，约占总人口的62%。去年，国内投资者占印尼交易量的70%，而在越南，截至7月23日，外国投资者的比例降至约14%，创历史新低，HSC战略市场研究发现。

在上述每个市场以及其他一些市场中，基准股指在2025年均上涨，尽管外国投资者撤资。[摩根大通](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/JPM%20US%20EQUITY)驻新加坡的亚洲业务主管兼全球新兴市场股票策略联席主管拉吉夫·巴特拉（Rajiv Batra）表示，当亚洲市场开盘时，本地投资者的股票购买行为“在欧洲市场开始交易前定下了基调”。

海外抛售仍可能重创亚洲市场。印度和印尼股市今年表现艰难，原因是外国投资者因油价上涨、货币走弱以及国内缺乏人工智能相关股票而撤退。不过，巴特拉表示，如果没有本地买家，这些市场“早就损失惨重”。*— 参与报道：王欣（Cindy Wang）、普里马·维拉亚尼（Prima Wirayani）和阮乔江（Nguyen Kieu Giang）*

## Asia’s Savers Piled Into Stocks and Reshaped Their Home Markets

Homegrown investors are overturning the balance of power in Asia’s markets. For decades investors in New York and London, primarily big institutions, drove trading volumes in Asia. But the continent’s [rising ­middle class](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2021-09-02/more-than-1-billion-asians-will-join-global-middle-class-by-2030) is shifting its saving from gold and real estate toward stocks, bonds and other financial assets, becoming the primary source of capital in Asia. The increase in disposable incomes helps too.

In some cases, governments are encouraging households to take more risk. India, [Japan](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/features/2025-09-30/japan-activist-investor-yoshiaki-murakami-revives-push-for-higher-stock-prices) and South Korea are pushing to improve corporate governance and boost shareholder returns to broaden the appeal of stock investing. Families are taking the bait, funneling savings into equities through mutual and exchange-traded funds as well as individual stocks. “You have this budding equity culture,” says Alexander Redman, chief equity strategist at CLSA in Singapore.

The shift has broad implications because Asia accounts for 56% of the world’s listed companies—shares valued at $43.5 trillion, including those at the heart of the artificial intelligence boom. The growing prominence of domestic investors means markets in India, Taiwan, Vietnam and elsewhere have become less sensitive to the whims of overseas traders who like to yank their money in times of crisis, such as the Iran war. “Foreign flows can be very fickle,” Redman says. (Flows are purchases minus redemptions.)

Yet the change carries its own risks, raising concerns about overheating valuations and speculative trading. Domestic investors could further concentrate capital in local champions and popular sectors, potentially leaving markets more vulnerable to abrupt reversals. “Once sentiment turns, everyone may try to exit through the same narrow door,” says Charu Chanana, chief investment strategist at Saxo Markets. Chinese stocks slumped in January after authorities tightened rules on margin financing. The move signaled an unease over an AI-led rally that had sent benchmarks to multiyear highs and driven turnover to record levels. More recently, investors in South Korea took steep losses in July after a historic market rise. Some retail investors’ exacerbated their misfortune through the use of leveraged exchange-traded funds, which the government has since [moved to curb](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-07-29/south-korea-to-hold-emergency-market-meeting-after-kospi-turmoil).

It’s hard to overstate the magnitude of the shift to domestic investing. Funds based in emerging Asian economies poured $557 billion into their stock markets from 2022 to 2025, more than seven times the sum they invested during the previous four years, according to financial data ­provider EPFR. By comparison, the amount that foreign-based funds committed dropped 81%, to $26 billion.

Rank-and-file investors will only grow in clout. The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development projects 65% of the global middle class will live in Asia by 2030. The region’s share of global private wealth climbed from 6% to 21% in 25 years, according to a 2025 United Overseas Bank Ltd. report. By 2029, Asia could claim a quarter of that stash, worth $99 trillion. “When I came to Asia in 1999, it was ­capital-constrained,” says William Bratton, head of cash equity research for the Asia-Pacific region at [BNP Paribas](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/BNP%20FP%20Equity). “Domestic capital pools were relatively small, and if you wanted to do something, there was a substantial need to access foreign capital.”

Consider other striking data points: India in the past six years has overtaken the UK and France to become the world’s fifth-largest stock market by the value of its shares. At 14.5 million, the number of securities accounts in Taiwan roughly equals 62% of its population. Domestic investors commanded a 70% share of trading in Indonesia last year, while in Vietnam the proportion of foreign investors fell to about 14% as of July 23, an all-time low, HSC Strategic Market Research found.

In each of these markets, as well as some others, benchmark stock indexes rallied in 2025 even as foreign investors pulled their money. When Asian markets open, the stock purchases of local investors are “setting the tone before Europe comes online,” says Rajiv Batra, head of Asia and co-head of global emerging-­markets equity strategy at [JPMorgan Chase & Co.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/JPM%20US%20EQUITY) in Singapore.

Overseas selling can still hit Asia markets hard. Indian and Indonesian shares struggled this year as foreign investors pulled back because of higher oil prices, weaker currencies and few domestic AI-linked stocks. Still, without local buyers, Batra says, those markets “would have gone to the cleaners.” *—With Cindy Wang, Prima Wirayani and Nguyen Kieu Giang*
