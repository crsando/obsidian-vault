## 海湾国家应在霍尔木兹问题上与伊朗达成协议

## 海湾国家应在霍尔木兹问题上与伊朗达成协议

### 如何减轻伊朗敲诈造成的痛苦

![Photograph: Aramco](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b481cc90b14817eb9eec84215e57c1d/3b481cc9-0b14-814a-9416-d10de1813590-3c1358955037be52db766cc8c2b3327b.jpg)

海湾国家陷入了唐纳德·特朗普制造的混乱之中。经过五个多月的战争，霍尔木兹海峡仍然基本处于关闭状态。一种熟悉的模式已经出现：当美国与伊朗的谈判未能取得进展时，双方又回到交战状态；当炸弹无法打破僵局时，谈判又重新开始。近日，特朗普先生再次一方面鼓吹与伊朗在霍尔木兹海峡达成协议，另一方面威胁要“狠狠地”打击伊朗。他给了伊朗“最后一次机会”重新开放海峡。又一次。

然而，伊朗仍然坚持对这条水道的近乎完全控制。其愤怒的邻国似乎对它会软化立场——或被逼如此——不抱太大希望。即使德黑兰出现新政权，假设有的话，也可能不愿放弃其新获得的战利品。

相反，海湾国家正竞相让霍尔木兹海峡变得不再必要。沙特阿拉伯和阿联酋都在建设或扩建绕过海峡的石油管道；伊拉克计划将石油北运。美国和沙特投资者正在考虑在海峡外建一座大型炼油厂。这些变通办法会有所帮助。但是，正如我们的“[霍尔木兹依赖度仪表盘](https://www.economist.com/middle-east-and-africa/2026/08/03/how-long-can-iran-weaponise-hormuz)”所示，这种增强韧性的努力不幸有其局限性。

[管道项目经常延期](https://www.economist.com/business/2026/08/05/a-global-pipeline-investment-boom-is-under-way)。但即使所有计划都按时完成，到2030年，战前每日通过霍尔木兹海峡的1500万桶原油中仍有500万桶不得不经过该海峡。此外，管道可能遭到伊朗的打击。依赖管道可能使海湾供应商面临其他咽喉要道的风险，尤其是红海曼德海峡，胡塞武装——也门亲伊朗的叛军——正在那里向船只开火。与伊朗一样，他们以前曾向船只收费，之后可能再次这样做（尽管他们否认）。

别忘了除原油以外的所有大宗商品。没有霍尔木兹海峡，卡塔尔仍无法运输其液化天然气——占全球供应量的五分之一。海湾国家的炼油厂在很大程度上仍处于被切断状态。而这还只是出口方面，即外部世界最关心的部分。从海湾国家的角度看，从食品到金属，它们的许多关键进口商品都无法通过陆路低成本运送。

修建管道值得，但海湾国家还应该投入更多资金保卫管道，并加快其他替代方案的推进。所有这些解决方案都需要时间。眼下，海湾国家需要其船只平安通过霍尔木兹海峡。美国的炸弹未能实现这一点。一次无人机袭击就足以吓退大多数船只，并推高保险费。

唯一现实的出路是达成协议，尽管可能令人不快。伊朗希望与阿曼（一个更务实的政府）共同管理海峡。谈判达成协议可能会让海湾国家有机会表明他们的红线和要求。海湾国家可能别无选择，只能支付过境费。只要这些费用不违反制裁，船东可能会容忍。这意味着美国需要参与。

这样的解决方案可能脆弱。国际法规定海上贸易应安全自由。一项涉及中国的多边协议将更加持久，中国有保持海峡开放的利害关系，并能约束伊朗。但目前看来这不太可能。伊朗似乎也尚未准备好接受海峡现状的回归。尽管如此，如果一项协议，即使有限且不平衡，能降低敌对行动并为海湾国家争取时间建立替代方案和防御力量以削弱伊朗的影响力，那么它值得达成。伊朗看起来可能赢得这场战役，但它可能仍会输掉更长远的战争。■

## Gulf states should make a deal with Iran on Hormuz

## Gulf states should make a deal with Iran on Hormuz

### How to make Iranian blackmail less painful

The gulf states are caught in a mess of Donald Trump’s making. After more than five months of war, the Strait of Hormuz is still mostly closed. A familiar pattern has emerged. When talks between America and Iran fail to yield progress, the two sides return to fighting; when bombs fail to break the deadlock, negotiations resume. In recent days Mr Trump has once again both talked up a deal on Hormuz and threatened to hit Iran “really hard”. He gave Iran “one last chance” to reopen the strait. Once again.

Iran, however, still insists on near-total control of the waterway. Its exasperated neighbours seem to hold out little hope that it will soften its stance—or be forced to do so. Even a new regime in Tehran, supposing one sprung up, may be unwilling to surrender its new prize.

Instead, Gulf countries are racing to make the Strait of Hormuz obsolete. Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates are both building or expanding oil pipelines to bypass the strait; Iraq plans to divert its barrels north. American and Saudi investors are considering a giant refinery outside the strait. Such workarounds will help. But, as our “[Hormuz dependency dashboard](https://www.economist.com/middle-east-and-africa/2026/08/03/how-long-can-iran-weaponise-hormuz)” shows, this quest for resilience unfortunately has its limits.

[Pipeline projects are often delayed](https://www.economist.com/business/2026/08/05/a-global-pipeline-investment-boom-is-under-way). Yet even if all the plans were completed on time, by 2030, 5m of the 15m barrels a day (b/d) that crossed Hormuz before the war would still have to pass through it. Besides, pipelines can be struck by Iran. Relying on them risks exposing Gulf suppliers to other choke points, not least the Bab al-Mandab strait in the Red Sea where the Houthis, Yemen’s Iran-allied rebels, are firing at ships. Like Iran, they have charged ships fees before and may do so again (though they deny this).

Don’t forget all the commodities besides crude oil. Without Hormuz, Qatar still cannot ship its liquefied natural gas—a fifth of the world’s supply. Gulf refineries remain largely cut off. And that is only on the export side, the part of the ledger that most concerns the outside world. From the point of view of the Gulf countries, many of their critical imports, from food to metals, cannot be sent cheaply overland.

Pipelines are worth building, but Gulf countries should also spend more on defending them, and hasten work on other alternatives. All these fixes will take time. For now, the Gulf states need their ships to traverse Hormuz unharmed. America’s bombs have not been able to accomplish this. One drone strike is enough to deter most ships, and jack up insurance premiums.

The only realistic way out is a deal, however unpalatable. Iran wants to manage the strait jointly with Oman, a more pragmatic government. Negotiating an agreement might give Gulf countries a chance to register their red lines and demands. Gulf countries might have no choice but to pay transit fees. Shipowners would probably tolerate them so long as they did not fall foul of sanctions. That means America needs to be on board.

Such a settlement would probably be fragile. International law says that maritime trade should be safe and free. A multilateral deal involving China, which has an interest in keeping the strait open and could restrain Iran, would be more durable. But that looks unlikely for now. Nor does Iran yet seem ready to accept a return to the status quo in the strait. Still, if a deal, however limited and unbalanced, lowers hostilities and buys Gulf countries time to build workarounds and defences that reduce Iran’s leverage, it will be worth having. Iran looks likely to win this battle. But it may yet lose the longer war. ■
