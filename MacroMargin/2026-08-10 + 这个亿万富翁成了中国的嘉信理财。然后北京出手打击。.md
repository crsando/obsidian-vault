### 中文译文

## **这个亿万富翁成了中国的嘉信理财。然后北京出手打击。**

### 李华（Leaf Li）的富途证券已成为政府控制中国资金外流行动的主要目标

2026年8月9日晚上7:00（美国东部时间）

富途在香港的一家门店。Lam Yik/Bloomberg

- 中国当局对富途处以2.71亿美元罚款，原因是其向中国大陆客户提供无牌照的经纪服务和共同基金。
- 这一处罚是北京控制中国资金外流行动的一部分。
- 富途创始人李华（Leaf Li）于2012年创立公司，与中国科技创始人及内部人士建立了早期关系。
李华（Leaf Li）表示，他的灵感来自[嘉信理财](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/SCHW)。这位中国亿万富翁于2012年创办其经纪公司，目标很简单：让亚洲大众都能进行股票投资。

在十年内，他建立了一家业务遍及中国、[香港](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/hong-kong)、亚洲市场和美国的公司。[像他的美国偶像](https://www.wsj.com/articles/how-schwab-ate-wall-street-11556474103?mod=article_inline)一样，李从简单的交易转向通过共同基金和咨询服务管理客户财富。

如今，李的雄心与中国监管部门发生冲突。他的公司[富途控股](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/FUTU) [FUTU0.22%上涨；绿色向上三角](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/FUTU)，已成为国家打击中国资金流向海外的主要目标。

今年五月，监管机构对在纳斯达克上市的富途处以2.71亿美元罚款，原因是其未持牌照向中国大陆客户提供经纪服务和共同基金。该处罚还包括对李个人约18万美元的罚款。

这一消息导致富途股价单日下跌近30%，虽然后来收复了部分失地，目前市值达150亿美元。不过，富途股价下挫已使李的净资产缩水数亿美元，据福布斯估计，目前约为57亿美元。

![Leaf Li, with a group of people, attended Futu’s Nasdaq listing in 2019.](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b881cc90b14817d9190eaa5eb430c20/3b881cc9-0b14-810e-937a-c58908d30bb8-6eef99c9a249c586a333ac3cd039965d.jpg)

身着条纹领带的Leaf Li位于图片中央，出席了2019年富途在纳斯达克的上市仪式。纳斯达克

李，中文名李华，表示富途正在与中国监管部门合作。富途已告知投资者，截至3月31日，其客户资产中仅有17%来自中国大陆账户，占公司收入的五分之一。公司表示预计全球增长将加速。

对富途及其同行的监管行动，只是北京重塑[中国超级富豪版图](https://www.wsj.com/world/china/chinas-tycoons-made-fortunes-offshore-now-the-party-is-over-7f5289ad?mod=article_inline)的努力的一部分。

此前，一个金融漏洞允许中国科技精英在香港或其他地方上市，并在中国监管部门无法触及的离岸市场积累财富。新规实际上关闭了这一路径。七月底，中国确认将对离岸信托征税，离岸信托此前是富裕中国人将资产存放海外的一种热门工具。

中国在法律上禁止其公民未经政府批准在海外投资股票和房地产。但据中国官方媒体报道，过去十年中，个人通过香港网站或亲自到香港，开设了富途证券及其同行的交易账户。尽管香港是中国的一部分，但其拥有自由交易货币和1997年之前在英国治理下建立的金融体系。

据中国官方媒体报道，个人利用当局为旅游或商务出行批准的[每年5万美元额度](https://www.wsj.com/world/china/china-economy-capital-flight-2ba6391b?mod=article_inline)向账户注资。

中国证监会在5月表示，这种为海外投资注资的方式是不被允许的，并对富途证券及其他两家券商处以罚款。这些券商现在被禁止在中国提供服务。内地客户不能再存入资金或进行投资，只能卖出持仓。

#### **互联网财富**

年近五旬的李，是这一漏洞的早期受益者，该漏洞使其得以积累海外财富。

他是[腾讯](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/TCEHY)的第18号员工，腾讯现在是中国市值最高的上市公司。他在早期获得了公司的股票，当腾讯于2004年在香港上市时，他一夜暴富。当时，在香港赚取的财富可以轻易地转换为其他货币。

2012年，随着中国海外财富的激增推动香港成为服务超级富豪的财富管理枢纽，李创立了富途证券。

据总部位于湖南省的报纸《今日湖南》2023年的一篇人物报道，他发现传统券商买卖全球证券笨拙且不尽人意。因此，他投入了约500万美元自有资金来打造一个交易平台。

富途的目标是让投资成本更低，并让没有市场经验的人也能参与，这一目标让人想起嘉信理财。1975年，美国监管机构取消了固定交易佣金——这一沿袭数十年的制度曾使股票经纪人免受竞争冲击——嘉信理财由此创办了首批折扣券商之一。

李最初将产品面向中国内地居民和香港约700万人口推销，后来以Moomoo品牌扩展到美国、新加坡、日本、马来西亚和澳大利亚。他于2019年将富途在纳斯达克上市。

早期，李与中国科技公司创始人和内部人士建立了关系，他们成为富途一项业务的客户，该业务帮助中国公司上市并向投资者出售股票。2019年，富途帮助科技巨头[阿里巴巴](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/HK/XHKG/9988)在香港通过向其客户出售股票筹集了110亿美元，这是富途支持的众多上市项目之一。

李受益于与腾讯的关系，腾讯是富途的早期投资者。李在2020年的一次会议上表示，这家科技巨头的员工成为富途用户，他们兑现腾讯股票期权的收益，并用这些钱在富途平台进行交易。

他开始管理在海外上市的中国公司的员工持股计划，这一举措使富途接触到更多富裕客户群体。

富途在2021年于美国增发股票时表示：“我们主要服务新兴的中国富裕人群。”该公司称，其高收入客户的中位年龄为34岁，上一年人均交易额为100万美元。

随着更多中国公司在海外上市，富途设立了一个部门，帮助超高净值人士建立自己的投资办公室来管理资金。

富途在2021年的股票发售中表示，它“正在寻求一个巨大的机遇，以推动财富管理行业千载难逢的转型。”

#### **监管机构不满**

中国当局不喜欢这种说法。2022 年底，他们表示富途（Futu）在没有必要牌照的情况下在中国内地运营。该公司很快从中国内地的应用商店下架了其应用。然而，中国当局表示，富途继续允许内地人士为账户注资并进行交易。

在 5 月受到处罚之前，富途一直高歌猛进，全球用户超过 3000 万。今年第一季度，其总交易量创下约 5300 亿美元的纪录。这一数字大部分来自在美国和香港交易股票的客户，其中许多人寻求参与人工智能股票的繁荣。相比之下，受美国散户投资者青睐的 Robinhood 在第一季度公布股票交易量为 6380 亿美元。

近期的打击行动旨在将中国散户投资者引导至国内持牌平台，让人们可以在中国境内证券交易所进行交易。公民也被允许进行海外投资，但现在主要通过政府批准的方式，这些方式以人民币返还任何利润。

富途没有此类国内持牌平台，因此目前专注于内地以外的业务，包括香港（据称每两名成年人中就有一名是富途用户）和东南亚。

与嘉信理财一样，李希望将业务转向为客户提供共同基金等投资产品。虽然证券交易占富途收入的绝大部分，但其财富管理业务正在增长。“我们不断拓展金融服务的边界，”李在 5 月公布富途第一季度业绩时表示。

## This Billionaire Became China’s Charles Schwab. Then Beijing Cracked Down.

## **This Billionaire Became China’s Charles Schwab. Then Beijing Cracked Down.**

### Leaf Li’s Futu brokerage has become a central target in the government’s campaign to control the flow of Chinese money offshore

By

Aug. 9, 2026 7:00 pm ET

A Futu storefront in Hong Kong. Lam Yik/Bloomberg

- Chinese authorities fined Futu $271 million for offering unlicensed brokerage services and mutual funds to customers in mainland China.
- The penalty is part of a campaign by Beijing to control the flow of Chinese money offshore.
- Futu founder Leaf Li set up the company in 2012, building early relationships with Chinese tech founders and insiders.
Leaf Li said his inspiration came from [Charles Schwab](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/SCHW). The Chinese billionaire started his brokerage in 2012 with a simple goal: bring stock investing to the Asian masses.

Within a decade, he built a company that spanned China, [Hong Kong](https://www.wsj.com/topics/place/hong-kong), Asian markets and the U.S. [Like his American idol](https://www.wsj.com/articles/how-schwab-ate-wall-street-11556474103?mod=article_inline), Li pivoted from simple trading to managing his clients’ wealth through mutual funds and advisory services.

Now, Li’s ambition is colliding with Chinese authorities. His company, [Futu Holdings](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/FUTU) [FUTU ](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/FUTU)[0.22%](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/FUTU)[increase; green up pointing triangle](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/FUTU), has become a central target in the state’s crackdown on the flow of Chinese money offshore.

Authorities in May handed Nasdaq-listed Futu a $271 million penalty for offering brokerage services and mutual funds to customers in mainland China without a license. The action included a personal fine for Li of roughly $180,000.

The news sent Futu’s share price down nearly 30% in a day, although it has since recovered some of those losses and now has a market capitalization of $15 billion. Still, the fall in Futu’s share price has knocked hundreds of millions of dollars off Li’s net worth, now at roughly $5.7 billion, according to Forbes.

Leaf Li, pictured center in a striped tie, attended Futu's Nasdaq listing in 2019. Nasdaq

Li, whose Chinese name is Li Hua, says Futu is cooperating with Chinese authorities. Futu has told investors that as of March 31, only 17% of its client assets were from accounts based in mainland China, making up a fifth of the company’s revenue. The company says it expects growth to accelerate globally.

The regulatory action against Futu and its peers is just one element of an effort by Beijing to reshape the [landscape for China’s superwealthy](https://www.wsj.com/world/china/chinas-tycoons-made-fortunes-offshore-now-the-party-is-over-7f5289ad?mod=article_inline).

A financial loophole previously allowed China’s tech elite to list their companies in Hong Kong or elsewhere and mint fortunes offshore beyond the reach of Chinese authorities. New regulation has effectively closed that path. In late July, China affirmed that it would tax offshore trusts, previously a popular tool for wealthy Chinese to park assets overseas.

China technically bans its citizens from investing overseas in stocks and real estate without government approval. But individuals over the past decade set up trading accounts with Futu and its peers via websites in Hong Kong or in person in the city, according to Chinese state media. While Hong Kong is part of China, it maintains its own freely traded currency and financial system built under British control until 1997.

Individuals funded their accounts using an [annual quota of $50,000](https://www.wsj.com/world/china/china-economy-capital-flight-2ba6391b?mod=article_inline) that authorities permit for overseas transfers for tourism or business travel, Chinese state media have reported.

The Chinese securities commission in May said this method of funding overseas investments was off limits and fined Futu and two other brokers. The brokers are now banned from offering their services in China. Mainland customers can no longer deposit money or make investments, only sell their positions.

#### **Internet riches**

Li, who is in his late 40s, was an early beneficiary of the loophole that allowed the accumulation of overseas wealth.

He was employee number 18 at [Tencent](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/TCEHY), now China’s most valuable listed company. He received stock in the business at an early stage, and when Tencent listed its shares in Hong Kong in 2004, he became wealthy overnight. In those days, fortunes minted in the territory could be easily converted into other currencies.

Li set up Futu in 2012 as this explosion of Chinese wealth overseas fueled the rise of Hong Kong as a hub for money managers catering to the superrich.

He found the buying and selling of global securities clunky and unsatisfying with traditional brokerages, according to a 2023 profile in Hunan Today, a Chinese newspaper based in Li’s home province. So he invested roughly $5 million of his own cash to build a trading platform.

Futu aimed to make investing lower-cost and available to those without market experience, a goal reminiscent of Charles Schwab. When U.S. regulators in 1975 abolished fixed trading commissions—blowing up a decades-old system that had shielded stockbrokers from competition—Schwab started one of the first discount brokerages.

Li initially marketed his product to Chinese nationals and Hong Kong’s roughly seven million people, and later expanded under the brand Moomoo to the U.S., Singapore, Japan, Malaysia and Australia. He listed Futu on the Nasdaq in 2019.

Early on, Li built relationships with Chinese tech founders and insiders, who became customers for a business Futu started to help Chinese companies list and sell stock to investors. It helped tech giant [Alibaba](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/HK/XHKG/9988) raise $11 billion in 2019 in Hong Kong by selling shares to Futu’s customers, one of many Futu-backed listings.

Li benefited from his connection with Tencent, an early investor in Futu. The tech giant’s employees became Futu users, cashing out their gains from Tencent stock options and using the money to trade on the Futu platform, Li told a conference in 2020.

He began managing the stock ownership plans of Chinese firms listing overseas, a move that connected Futu to further pools of well-off customers.

“We primarily serve the emerging affluent Chinese population,” Futu said in 2021, when it sold additional shares in the U.S. It said its high-earning customers had a median age of 34 and had traded an average of $1 million the previous year.

As more Chinese companies listed overseas, Futu established an arm helping ultra-high-net-worth individuals set up their own investment offices to manage their money.

Futu in its 2021 share sale said it was “pursuing a massive opportunity to facilitate a once-in-a-generation shift in the wealth management industry.”

#### **Regulators frown**

Chinese authorities didn’t like the sound of that. At the end of 2022, they said Futu was operating in mainland of China without necessary licenses. The company removed its app from mainland Chinese app stores soon after. Yet Chinese authorities say Futu continued to allow mainlanders to fund accounts and trade.

Before the penalty in May, Futu was flying high, with more than 30 million users globally. Its total trading volume hit a record of roughly $530 billion in the first quarter of this year. Most of that figure came from clients trading shares in the U.S. and Hong Kong, many of them seeking access to the boom in artificial-intelligence stocks. By comparison, Robinhood—a favorite of U.S. individual investors—posted share-trading volumes of $638 billion in the first quarter.

The recent crackdown is meant to funnel Chinese individual investors into licensed platforms inside the country where people can trade on China’s domestic stock exchanges. Citizens are also permitted to invest overseas, but now mainly through government-approved methods that return any profits in yuan.

Futu doesn’t have one of those domestic licensed platforms, so it is now focusing on business outside the mainland, including Hong Kong—where it says one of every two adults is a Futu user—and Southeast Asia.

Like Schwab, Li wants to shift his business toward offering investment products such as mutual funds to customers. While trading securities makes up the biggest chunk of Futu’s revenue, its wealth-management division is growing. “We keep expanding the boundaries of financial services,” Li said in May, when announcing Futu’s first-quarter results.
