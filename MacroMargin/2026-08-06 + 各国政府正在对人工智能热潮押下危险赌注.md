### 中文译文

## 各国政府正在对人工智能热潮押下危险赌注

**若要明白原因，不妨看看债券市场**

在经历了今年的稳步攀升后，美国、法国、日本和英国的30年期国债收益率均接近2007-09年全球金融危机以来的最高水平。这一成绩对英国而言尤为引人注目，该国在2022年财政恐慌期间的高点早已被超越。对于那些曾想知道随着疫情后通胀回落，收益率是否会回到2010年代低点的人来说，市场似乎给出了决定性的答案：不会。甚至可以说，它们还可能进一步攀升。

罪魁祸首很明确。通胀尚未被完全征服，这阻止了央行降息。就连日本也在告别宽松货币政策（尽管还不足以提振日元，日本和美国都通过官方购买进行了干预）。发达世界巨大的预算赤字几乎没有显著收窄的迹象，这增加了政府最终迫使央行通过通胀淡化债务负担的可能性。伊朗战争和关税等地缘政治动荡正在推高投资者要求的风险补偿。新任美联储主席凯文·沃什（Kevin Warsh）在早期与市场的交锋中失手，也无济于事。

高债券收益率对于负债累累的政府来说是个问题。例如，美国债券收益率每上升一个百分点，十年内将使其额外支付相当于年度GDP 1.3%的利息。然而，现在更难想象政府会通过削减支出来抵消这种压力。在美国，每个政党似乎都希望债务危机落在对方头上。法国明年的总统选举可能成为左翼和右翼民粹主义政党之间的争斗。日本则奇怪地正在进行财政刺激。政府等待调整的时间越长，调整的幅度就越大。

各国政府看起来越来越像是在押注经济增长来支付账单——而今天这意味押注人工智能。问题在于，更快的增长会通过推高投资，通常会带来更高的利率和更高的债券收益率。如今，更高的市场利率先于更快的增长到来。数据中心投资的规模巨大，投行高盛（Goldman Sachs）估计今年将达到1万亿美元，这正在使资本变得更加稀缺，并助推收益率上升。然而，人工智能尚未显著提升生产率，即使在中国——可以说该技术的普及程度走在前列——也是如此。

这种提振可能还需要一段时间。电力和计算机花费了数十年时间才显著提高生产率增长。各经济体的表现也不会一样。如果人工智能为美国带来更多增长，并拉高全球利率，但在旧大陆却因僵化的劳动力市场和高昂的能源成本而受阻，欧洲的财政困境可能会恶化。那将意味着债务成本更高，却没有更多增长。

还有一个问题。如果人工智能带来的生产率提升大到足以改变任何一个国家的财政算盘，那么大规模就业扰乱可能即将到来，这意味着政府需要为失业工人提供更多支出。收入也可能从劳动力转向资本，而资本的税率较低。如果人工智能引发军备竞赛，各国还需要增加国防开支。即使由人工智能驱动的科学进步所激发的寿命延长这一令人兴奋的前景，也会增加国家在养老金上的支出。智库布鲁金斯学会（Brookings Institution）的经济学家认为，这些因素加上更高的利率，可能会使人工智能带来的增长对美国赤字的积极影响减少一半以上。

上一次包括美国和英国在内的许多富裕国家实现预算盈余是在世纪之交。这需要快速的生产率增长，当时来自计算机和早期互联网。但赤字也必须削减。如果没有财政审慎，债券就是对人工智能的一场投机，看起来就像押注摇摇欲坠的科技股一样冒险。

### English Original

## Governments are making a dangerous bet on the AI boom

**To see why, look to the bond markets**

After a steady upward climb this year, 30-year bonds in America, France, Japan and Britain are all near their highest since the global financial crisis of 2007-09. That feat is particularly impressive for Britain, where the highs during a fiscal panic in 2022 have been long surpassed. For anyone who was wondering if yields would return to the lows of the 2010s as inflation fell after the pandemic, markets appear to have supplied a decisive answer: they will not. If anything, they are likely to climb higher still.

The culprits are clear. Inflation has not quite been beaten, deterring central banks from cutting interest rates. Even Japan is leaving behind its loose-money policies (though not enough to strengthen the yen, which both Japan and America have propped up with official purchases). Gaping rich-world budget deficits show little sign of narrowing meaningfully, raising the possibility that governments eventually force central banks to inflate their debts away. Geopolitical ructions, like the Iran war and tariffs, are lifting the compensation for risk that investors demand. It does not help that [Kevin Warsh](https://www.economist.com/finance-and-economics/2026/08/02/what-will-kevin-warsh-do-if-americas-economy-breaks), the new Federal Reserve chair, fumbled an early encounter with the markets.

High bond yields are a problem for indebted governments. Every percentage-point rise in America’s bond yields, for instance, costs it extra interest payments worth 1.3% of annual GDP within a decade. Yet it is getting harder to imagine governments making cuts to offset the squeeze. In America each political party seems to be hoping that any debt crisis lands on the other. France’s presidential election next year could become a fight between populist parties on the left and right. Japan is, bizarrely, undertaking a fiscal stimulus. The longer governments wait to adjust, the bigger the adjustments become.

Governments look increasingly as if they are betting on economic growth to pay the bills—which today means a bet on AI. The trouble is that faster growth, by boosting investment, usually brings higher interest rates and higher bond yields. Today higher market rates have arrived before faster growth. The sheer scale of data-centre investment, pegged at $1trn this year by Goldman Sachs, a bank, is making capital scarcer and helping raise yields. However, AI has yet to boost productivity measurably, even in China where diffusion of the technology is arguably the furthest along.

It could be a while before the boost comes. Electricity and computing took decades to raise productivity growth perceptibly. Not all economies will fare equally well, either. Europe’s fiscal woes could worsen if AI brings more growth for America, pulling up global interest rates, but gets jammed up in the old world by rigid labour markets and high energy costs. That would mean costlier debt without any more growth.

There is another problem, too. If the productivity boost from AI is large enough to transform any country’s fiscal arithmetic, vast job disruption is probably coming, meaning [more spending on unemployed workers](https://www.economist.com/united-states/2026/08/02/can-america-retrain-workers-before-ai-leaves-them-behind). Income may also shift from labour to capital, which is more lightly taxed. Should AI prompt an arms race, countries will also need more defence spending. Even the exciting prospect of longer lifespans, fuelled by AI-powered scientific advances, would raise state spending on pensions. Economists at the Brookings Institution, a think-tank, reckon these factors plus higher interest rates could more than halve the positive impact of AI-induced growth on American deficits.

The last time much of the rich world, including America and Britain, ran budget surpluses was at the turn of the millennium. That required fast productivity growth, which came from computers and the early internet. But deficits had to be cut, too. Without fiscal prudence, bonds are a punt on AI that looks just as risky as betting on wobbly tech stocks.
