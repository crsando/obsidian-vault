### 中文译文

## Moonshot重组以寻求北京批准其上市

中国人工智能初创企业Moonshot已重组其公司结构，并引入多家知名国有背景投资者，以寻求赢得北京方面对其香港上市计划的批准。

总部位于北京的人工智能实验室的[Kimi K3模型](https://www.ft.com/content/c6ecd8ce-c441-4d7c-aea6-fae3e28fb6ff?syn-25a6b1a6=1)已缩小了与Anthropic领先系统之间的性能差距，并在软件开发人员中广受欢迎。该公司正寻求首次公开募股，以为其下一阶段的模型开发和扩张提供资金。

在上市之前，Moonshot可能需要解除其公司架构，该架构包括一个用于从国际投资者处筹集美元的离岸实体。

Moonshot的IPO时间尚不明确，但两位知情人士表示，上市可能要到明年才会进行。另一位人士表示，如果监管问题得到解决，可能会更快。

据公司披露，上周Moonshot将其在中国的实体从有限责任公司改组为股份有限公司，这是其迈向上市的第一步。

股份有限公司具有更正式的公司治理要求，通常拥有更广泛的股东基础，且股份转让更为便捷。

中国证券监管机构已禁止某些总部位于中国、拥有通过所谓[“红筹”结构](https://www.ft.com/content/f1e306d9-870b-44eb-8449-2c03d3899261)控制核心运营业务的海外实体的科技公司在境外上市。

此举反映出对战略性重要技术可能落入外资手中的担忧。

今年早些时候，监管机构发布通知，称某些拥有持有关键知识产权的海外实体的公司将不符合境外上市资格。这一政策迫使包括Moonshot和StepFun在内的多家知名AI公司暂停IPO准备工作，同时进行所有权重组。

据多位知情人士透露，Moonshot正与一组银行家和律师协调，以解决其海外投资者基础的问题。

在Manus AI——一家在中国创立的AI代理初创公司——被出售给Meta之后，技术泄露的担忧加剧。中国已下令该交易被[撤销](https://www.ft.com/content/1e4c269a-5258-406c-a308-e55c3d5d640f?syn-25a6b1a6=1)。

据一位投资者称，本周早些时候，Moonshot向其现有投资者群体发送了一份更新，说明其仍在撤销海外实体的过程中，且尚未确定如何将投资者的离岸股份转移到在岸实体。该通知是在媒体报道Moonshot计划在六个月内进行IPO之后发出的。

一项提议将要求海外投资者建立内地投资载体，通过该载体直接持有股份——鉴于中国的外商投资审批和潜在的税务影响，这是一个漫长的过程。

另一项提议则要求投资者先卖回其离岸股份，再通过新设立的在岸实体回购等值持股——这一过程同样需要广泛的谈判和审批。

投资者也可以选择传统的外商直接投资路径，且无论选择哪种方案，其权利和利益都将得到保留。

一位接近监管机构的人士表示，如果能提供充分理由保留这种结构，监管机构仍可能批准Moonshot豁免红筹限制。豁免将大大加快任何上市时间表。

目前尚未做出决定，该公司同时正在推进重组。

月之暗面在最近完成两轮连续融资中的一轮后，并无立即上市的资金压力。第一轮融资对该公司的估值约为300亿美元，第二轮完成后估值将达到500亿美元。

月之暗面在估值300亿美元的那轮融资中吸引了多家知名国有基金，其中包括国家人工智能产业投资基金。

其股东登记（7月更新）还列出了全国社会保障基金、来自上海和贵州的地方政府引导基金，以及国有媒体旗舰《人民日报》作为投资者。月之暗面此前曾获得包括[阿里巴巴](https://www.ft.com/stream/085b1a45-6c58-4f62-b923-047085e89fa0)和腾讯在内的集团的投资。

来自不同省份的国有投资者的广泛组合，与竞争对手Z.ai（前身为智谱）所采取的策略如出一辙。该公司不断壮大的政府股东网络帮助其进军新的区域市场并获得本地企业的合同。

随着中国领先的AI实验室斥资数十亿美元训练规模越来越大、最前沿的模型，同时保持推理（即使用）成本低廉以促进采用，融资竞赛变得越来越重要。

伯恩斯坦的技术分析师Robin Zhu写道，像月之暗面和Z.ai这样领先的中国AI集团的目标是“廉价地提供智能”，通过提供低成本的模型访问，同时“为下一轮训练提供资金”。

他表示，优先事项不是最大化短期利润，而是构建能够快速吸引用户的模型：“一个糟糕的模型会迅速赶走用户，既影响收入势头，也影响下一步融资的成本。”

Moonshot拒绝置评。

### English Original

## Moonshot shake-up seeks to win Beijing nod for stock market debut

Chinese AI start-up Moonshot has revamped its corporate structure and brought in several prominent state-backed investors as it seeks to win Beijing’s approval for a Hong Kong listing.

The Beijing-based AI lab’s [Kimi K3 model](https://www.ft.com/content/c6ecd8ce-c441-4d7c-aea6-fae3e28fb6ff?syn-25a6b1a6=1) has narrowed the performance gap with Anthropic’s leading systems and gained popularity among software developers. It is targeting an initial public offering that would fund its next phase of model development and expansion.

Before it can list Moonshot may need to unwind its corporate structure, which includes an offshore entity to raise funds in US dollars from international investors.

The timing of Moonshot’s IPO remains unclear, but two people familiar with the matter said it was unlikely to take place until next year. Another person said it could be sooner if the regulatory issues were resolved.

Last week, Moonshot restructured its China-based entity from a limited liability company to a joint stock company, in its first visible step towards going public, according to corporate disclosures.

Joint stock companies have more formal corporate governance requirements and generally have a wider shareholder base where it is easier to transfer shares.

The Chinese securities regulator has barred certain technology companies based in China with overseas entities that control the core operating business through so-called [“red-chip” structures](https://www.ft.com/content/f1e306d9-870b-44eb-8449-2c03d3899261) from listing outside the mainland.

That move reflects concerns over strategically important technologies potentially falling under foreign ownership.

Earlier this year, regulators issued a notification that certain companies with overseas entities holding key intellectual property would not be eligible for offshore listings. The policy forced several high-profile [AI](https://www.ft.com/artificial-intelligence) companies, including Moonshot and StepFun, to suspend IPO preparations while they restructured their ownership.

Moonshot is co-ordinating with a team of bankers and lawyers to resolve the issue with its overseas investor base, according to multiple people familiar with the matter.

Concerns of tech leakage intensified following the sale of Manus AI — an AI agent start-up founded in China — to Meta. China ordered the deal to [be unwound](https://www.ft.com/content/1e4c269a-5258-406c-a308-e55c3d5d640f?syn-25a6b1a6=1).

Earlier this week, Moonshot sent an update to its existing investor base explaining that it was still in the process of unwinding its overseas entity and that it still had not determined how it would transfer investors’ offshore stakes to an onshore entity, according to one investor. The notice came after media reports that Moonshot planned to hold an IPO within six months.

One proposal would require overseas investors to establish mainland investment vehicles through which they could hold shares directly — a lengthy process given China’s foreign investment approvals and potential tax implications.

Another would see investors sell back their offshore stakes before repurchasing equivalent holdings through newly established onshore entities — a process that would also require extensive negotiations and approvals.

Investors could also choose the conventional foreign direct investment route and would retain their rights and interests no matter which option they decided on.

One person close to the regulator said that it could still grant Moonshot an exemption from the red-chip restrictions if solid reasons could be provided to keep such a structure. An exemption would significantly accelerate any listing timetable.

No decision has been made and the company is proceeding with the restructuring in the meantime.

Moonshot does not have any immediate financial pressure to list after recently completing one of two consecutive financing rounds. The first valued the company at about $30bn and the second will give it a valuation of $50bn once completed.

Moonshot attracted several high-profile state-owned funds in the $30bn round, including the National Artificial Intelligence Industry Investment Fund.

Its shareholder registration, updated in July, also lists the National Social Security Fund, local government guidance funds from Shanghai and Guizhou, and state media flagship People’s Daily as investors. Moonshot has previously received investment from groups including [Alibaba](https://www.ft.com/stream/085b1a45-6c58-4f62-b923-047085e89fa0) and Tencent.

The broad mix of state investors from different provinces mirrors a strategy adopted by rival Z.ai, formerly Zhipu, whose growing network of government shareholders has helped it expand into new regional markets and secure contracts with local enterprises.

The fundraising race has become increasingly important as China’s leading AI laboratories spend billions to train ever-larger cutting-edge models while keeping inference, or usage, costs low to boost adoption.

Robin Zhu, a technology analyst at Bernstein, wrote that the objective for leading Chinese AI groups such as Moonshot and Z.ai was to “serve intelligence cheaply” by providing low-cost access to models while “funding the next training run”.

Rather than maximising short-term profits, he said the priority was to build models that quickly attract users: “A poor model can quickly drive away users, impacting both revenue momentum and the cost of funding for the next step forward.”

Moonshot declined to comment.
