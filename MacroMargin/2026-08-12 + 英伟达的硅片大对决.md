## 英伟达的硅片大对决

[商业](https://www.economist.com/business) | 超大规模云服务商之战

## 英伟达的硅片大对决

### 这家芯片制造商最大的客户想要分一杯羹。它正以一项5000亿美元的交易进行反击

![photograph: getty images](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3ba81cc90b148164a71cdcf704c5f9b2/3ba81cc9-0b14-819e-bcc8-c4a7a36426e3-17b4ff798b2d6f6ffad55b0b6a9183bf.jpg)

图片来源：Getty Images

2026年8月11日|4分钟阅读

英伟达与超大规模云服务商——亚马逊、谷歌、Meta和微软等云巨头——之间的关系曾经是直截了当的。英伟达设计和供应芯片；超大规模云服务商使用这些芯片建造数据中心。目前，双方仍然需要彼此（见图表）。然而，双方都在为未来减少对彼此的依赖做准备。

分裂迫在眉睫的一个迹象出现在8月10日，当时英伟达宣布与华尔街六家最大的投资者（包括贝莱德和高盛）合作，为人工智能基础设施“调动超过5000亿美元”。其目的是帮助超大规模云服务商以外的客户找到建设数据中心所需的巨额资金。就超大规模云服务商而言，它们不再满足于只购买英伟达的芯片。它们正在花费数十亿美元设计自己的芯片。

![chart: the economist](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3ba81cc90b148164a71cdcf704c5f9b2/3ba81cc9-0b14-8187-b5db-db6473942def-8d636b691e067fb0002b8d86dec3ca03.png)

图表：《经济学人》

谷歌多年来一直通过其云服务出租张量处理单元（TPU）——专用人工智能芯片。现在它开始向其他公司销售TPU系统。亚马逊将其定制芯片业务（其中大部分与人工智能相关）的年化收入定为250亿美元。公司老板安迪·贾西认为，这使其成为全球三大数据中心芯片业务之一。微软和Meta也开发了自己的芯片。两大人工智能实验室Anthropic和OpenAI也计划这样做。

超大规模云服务商有充分理由自行设计芯片。芯片占人工智能数据中心成本的很大一部分。券商伯恩斯坦估计，在一个运行英伟达H100芯片（单价2.5万美元）的服务器机架中，这些芯片的支出占总成本的四分之三。定制芯片的成本仅为前者的五分之一到三分之一，尽管性能较弱。云巨头们声称，尽管如此，他们每美元能获得更多计算能力。定制芯片也更适合特定任务：例如，谷歌的TPU用于支持其人工智能模型的计算，而Meta的处理器用于推荐算法。

一些超大规模云服务商相信，定制芯片本身将成为一项大业务。今年5月，谷歌与私募股权巨头黑石集团合作，成立一家人工智能云公司，出租基于谷歌芯片构建的计算能力。亚马逊也计划开展类似业务。Anthropic打算使用多达400万颗亚马逊的Trainium处理器。OpenAI表示也将使用该公司的芯片。

这些举措可能使定制芯片成为英伟达芯片的强大竞争对手。研究公司彭博行业研究估计，AI芯片出货量将从今年的约1500万颗增长到2030年的2800万颗。到2030年，定制芯片占比将从38%升至49%，英伟达将占40%。英伟达在收入上可能仍将保持主导地位——但定制芯片的较低成本将对其丰厚利润率构成压力。

然而，英伟达有不同的看法。其老板黄仁勋认为，定制芯片的最大优势——专业性——也是它的弱点：它适用于已知工作负载，而非新负载。相比之下，英伟达的GPU几乎可以处理任何AI任务。随着AI从大语言模型扩展到机器人、自动驾驶汽车和工业应用，这种通用性可能会更加重要。

跟上英伟达的步伐也可能代价高昂且困难重重。它现在每年发布一次突破性芯片，而之前是每两年一次。设计一颗前沿AI芯片通常要花费其他公司10亿至30亿美元，而英伟达仅上个季度就在研发上投入超过60亿美元，它可以轻松地以数倍于此的投入应对。此外，这还需要两到三年时间。很少有公司拥有持续投入的资金和工程人才。

英伟达的商业模式提供了另一道防线。将设计转化为成品芯片的公司（如台湾芯片制造商台积电）产能有限。美国银行的维韦克·阿里亚表示，云公司必须决定是将那“宝贵的产能分配”用于自身需求还是客户需求。而英伟达自己制造芯片，则没有这种冲突。

除了抵御超大规模云服务商的挑战，英伟达还希望开拓新业务。它希望向试图建设国内AI基础设施的政府、构建自身AI基础设施的企业以及出租AI计算能力的“新云”公司销售产品。英伟达预计，对这些客户的销售增速将超过对超大规模云服务商的销售。

与华尔街的新合作是帮助客户为AI投资融资的更广泛战略的一部分。7月，英伟达推出了一项计划，以未来收入的一部分为交换，出租新云服务商的闲置计算能力，使其更容易借贷和扩张。据报道，该公司还在讨论一项3500亿美元的计划，以帮助OpenAI在俄亥俄州租赁数据中心并购买GPU。

不过，目前稀缺性仍然模糊了客户和竞争对手之间的界限。6月，谷歌与SpaceX签署了一项价值300亿美元的协议，租用其基于英伟达芯片运行的AI计算能力。亚马逊自研芯片在其资本支出中占比不到十分之一；其余大部分仍流向英伟达。所有人都在尽其所能购买计算能力。英伟达仍然供应大部分。■

## Nvidia’s great silicon showdown

[Business](https://www.economist.com/business) | Battle of the hyperscalers

## Nvidia’s great silicon showdown

### The chipmaker’s biggest customers want a piece of its business. It is fighting back with a $500bn deal

Photograph: Getty Images

Aug 11th 2026|4 min read

The relationship between [Nvidia](https://www.economist.com/business/2026/03/17/nvidia-is-expanding-its-empire) and the hyperscalers—cloud giants such as Amazon, Google, Meta and Microsoft—used to be straightforward. Nvidia designed and supplied chips; the hyperscalers built data centres using them. For now, the two sides still need one another (see chart). Yet both are preparing for a future in which they lean on each other less.

A sign of the impending separation came on August 10th, when Nvidia announced a partnership with six of Wall Street’s biggest investors, including BlackRock and Goldman Sachs, to “mobilise over $500bn” for artificial-intelligence infrastructure. The aim is to help customers other than hyperscalers find the vast sums needed to build data centres. For their part, the hyperscalers are no longer content only to buy Nvidia’s chips. They are spending billions on designing their own.

Chart: The Economist

Google has for years rented access to tensor processing units (tpus), specialised ai chips, through its cloud. Now it is selling tpu systems to other firms. Amazon puts the annualised revenue of its custom-chip business, much of which is tied to ai, at $25bn. Andy Jassy, the company’s boss, reckons that makes it one of the world’s three biggest data-centre chip businesses. Microsoft and Meta have also developed their own chips. Anthropic and Openai, two big ai labs, plan to do the same.

Hyperscalers have good reasons to design their own silicon. Chips account for much of the cost of an ai data centre. Bernstein, a broker, estimates that in a server rack running Nvidia’s H100 chips, priced at $25,000 apiece, spending on those chips makes up three-quarters of the total cost. Custom silicon is a fifth to a third as expensive, though less powerful. The cloud giants argue that they nevertheless get more computing power per dollar. Custom chips are also better suited to particular jobs: Google’s tpus for calculations underpinning its ai models, for example, and Meta’s processors for recommendation algorithms.

Some hyperscalers believe custom silicon will become a big business in its own right. In May Google teamed up with Blackstone, a private-equity giant, to establish an ai cloud firm that will rent out computing power built on Google’s chips. Amazon plans a similar venture. Anthropic intends to use up to 4m of Amazon’s Trainium processors. Openai says it will use the firm’s chips, too.

Such moves could turn custom silicon into a formidable competitor to Nvidia’s chips. Bloomberg Intelligence, a research firm, estimates that ai-chip shipments will grow from around 15m units this year to 28m by 2030. Custom chips will grow from 38% of the total to 49% by 2030, with Nvidia responsible for 40%. Nvidia will probably remain dominant by revenue—but the lower cost of custom silicon will put pressure on its fat margins.

Nvidia sees things differently, however. Jensen Huang, its boss, argues that custom silicon’s greatest strength—specialisation—is also its weakness: it is good for known workloads, not new ones. Nvidia’s gpus, by contrast, can handle almost any ai task. As ai spreads beyond large language models into robotics, autonomous vehicles and industrial applications, that versatility could matter more.

Keeping up with Nvidia may also prove expensive and difficult. It now releases breakthrough chips once a year, up from once every two. Designing a frontier ai chip typically costs other firms $1bn-3bn, a sum Nvidia, which spent over $6bn on r&d last quarter alone, can easily multiply. It also takes two to three years. Few firms have the capital and engineering talent to sustain such an effort.

Nvidia’s business model offers another defence. Firms that turn designs into finished chips, such as tsmc, the Taiwanese chipmaker, have limited capacity. Cloud companies, says Vivek Arya of Bank of America, have to decide whether to use that “precious allocation” for their own needs or those of customers. Nvidia, which makes its own chips, faces no conflict.

As well as defending its position against hyperscalers, Nvidia also hopes to drum up new business. It wants to sell to governments trying to build domestic ai infrastructure, companies building their own ai infrastructure and “neocloud” firms that rent out ai computing power. Nvidia expects sales to these customers to grow faster than to hyperscalers.

The new partnership with Wall Street is part of a broader strategy to help customers finance ai investments. In July Nvidia launched a programme to rent unused computing capacity from neoclouds in exchange for a share of future revenues to make it easier for them to borrow and expand. It is also reportedly discussing a $350bn scheme to help Openai lease a data centre in Ohio and buy gpus.

For now, though, scarcity still blurs the line between customer and competitor. In June Google signed a $30bn deal to lease ai computing capacity from SpaceX, which runs on Nvidia chips. Amazon’s custom chips account for less than a tenth of its capital spending; much of the rest still goes to Nvidia. Everyone is buying computing power wherever they can find it. Nvidia still supplies the bulk. ■
