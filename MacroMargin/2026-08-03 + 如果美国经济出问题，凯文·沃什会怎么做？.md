### 中文译文

## 如果美国经济出问题，凯文·沃什会怎么做？

*一个神秘的美联储面临着人工智能股票下跌和油价飙升的风险*

![Photograph: Getty Images](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3b181cc90b14818787f4dcb5e0ed65fe/3b181cc9-0b14-8195-b8b5-edff186f914e-5c030339d9d9fff2c391d0ea510c4785.jpg)

图片来源：Getty Images

对于某种类型的投机者来说，Polymarket 的博彩平台提供了一份有趣的合约。“无事发生：2026”是一个以看好后市的极度线上交易者偏爱的口头禅命名的赌注。如果大约十几个事件（从不太可能到离奇）在年内都没有发生，该合约将支付约 30% 的回报。这要求唐纳德·特朗普和习近平都不得辞职，比特币价格保持在 1 万美元至 100 万美元之间，地球不会遭受大地震、火山喷发或陨石撞击等等。如果其中一件或多件事确实发生，投机者将失去一切。

新任不久的美联储主席凯文·沃什最近主持了与央行利率制定委员会的第二次会议，他似乎也在进行类似的押注。*《经济学人》*不知道沃什对陨石或火山的看法。但他在 7 月 29 日新闻发布会上异常守口如瓶的表现让市场震动。当天十年期国债收益率跳升，周末收于 4.7% 以上，接近三年高位。股票价格，尤其是对利率敏感的高科技公司，出现波动——尽管美联储维持利率不变。沃什希望，如果他对美联储的计划保持沉默，市场就会对经济给出自己的、有助益的独立判断。

他大概承受得起这个实验，因为如果一切平安，一点小波动没什么大碍。最坏的情况下，它们会推高借贷成本，以补偿放贷者面临的额外不确定性。但如果确实发生了坏事，那么对美联储可能如何反应的困惑可能会加剧由此造成的混乱。美联储非但不会降低波动性，反而会增加波动性。就连沃什也承认，在危机中，清晰的沟通是“审慎”之举。

美联储失去其模糊空间的可能性有多大？几个中等规模的风险同时若隐若现。最明显的是人工智能相关股票的抛售，这既发生在美国，也尤其发生在[韩国](https://www.economist.com/business/2026/07/29/south-koreas-stock-market-boom-is-collapsing-spectacularly)。2000 年互联网泡沫破裂时，崩盘将世界经济拖入衰退。令人担忧的是，今天已有迹象表明一些大赌注正在变糟，导致投资公司摇摆不定。规模数十亿美元的对冲基金 Situational Awareness 在过去一个月遭受了巨大损失。它被迫迅速平仓，以至于规模大得多的基金 Citadel 能够以折扣价收购其大部分上市头寸。

目前，损害似乎还在可控范围内。一些 AI 股票已略有反弹；以科技股为主的纳斯达克指数虽然较 6 月峰值下跌了 6%，但自年初以来仍处于上涨状态。

但如果美国科技股进一步失去光彩，美国经济可能会陷入困境。如今，美元作为避风港的地位在很大程度上归功于外国投资者对美国股票的贪婪。在多年巨额预算赤字以及通胀再次上升的情况下，美国国债看起来并不那么有吸引力。去年 4 月，在特朗普宣布“解放日”关税之后，美国曾短暂看到了国际投资者同时抛售所有类型美元资产的样子。长期重演仍然是一个遥远的前景，但可能迅速变得糟糕。

另一危险在于霍尔木兹海峡。自从美国与伊朗之间脆弱的停火协议[破裂](https://www.economist.com/middle-east-and-africa/2026/07/28/new-strikes-in-iran-show-the-weakness-of-donald-trumps-deal)以来，几乎没有石油通过该海峡，意味着[价格已经飙升](https://www.economist.com/finance-and-economics/2026/07/30/oil-prices-remain-highly-flammable)。全球基准布伦特原油每桶现在售价88美元，高于7月初的约70美元。美国的汽油价格已远高于每加仑4美元，而冲突开始前约为3美元。没有什么价格比这更让美国消费者烦恼。急剧上涨很容易破坏支出、通胀预期和对美联储的信心。

更糟的是，特朗普先生似乎决心让沃什先生的日子难过。总统用来征收关税的临时权力已于7月24日到期，此前他的大多数关税在2月被最高法院驳回。自那以后，白宫推出了新的广泛贸易壁垒来取代已到期的壁垒，并对包括加拿大和[巴西](https://www.economist.com/the-americas/2026/07/19/brazils-much-loved-payments-system-has-drawn-donald-trumps-ire)在内的国家宣布了更高的征税。企业仍未将先前关税带来的价格上涨完全转嫁给消费者。任何进一步的上涨都可能逆转近期商品通胀的下降。

### **你只需要倒霉一次**

所以不仅有可能发生事情，而且有可能同时发生几件事。最棘手的组合可能是股市平静，但石油和新关税进一步推高通胀。通常，这将是美联储理事们考虑加息的一个明确理由。有些人已经投票支持加息。但在11月中期选举之前收紧货币政策，可能会破坏沃什先生与白宫的关系，重新引发关于美联储独立性的丑陋争吵。有可能，为了避免这种情况，沃什先生已经在试图让市场替他完成紧缩工作。换句话说，通过大声重申他对美联储通胀目标的承诺，而不实际说他会加息，他可能希望长期债券收益率上升，从而使他不必加息。

危险在于，更高的收益率可能反而反映了沃什先生的实验出错的风险。“永远不会有事发生”可能是一个好赌注。但央行行长的声誉是在事情发生时建立的。

## What will Kevin Warsh do if America’s economy breaks?

*An enigmatic Fed faces the risk of AI stocks falling and the oil price soaring*

Photograph: Getty Images

For punters of a certain sort, Polymarket’s gambling platform offers an intriguing contract. “Nothing ever happens: 2026” is a bet named after a favoured mantra of bullish, very online traders. If none of roughly a dozen events, veering from the unlikely to the outlandish, takes place during the year, the contract pays out a return of around 30%. That would require each of Donald Trump and Xi Jinping not to resign, bitcoin’s price to remain between $10,000 and $1m, the planet not to suffer a major earthquake, volcanic eruption or meteor strike, and so on. If one or more of these things does happen, the punter loses everything.

Kevin Warsh, the new-ish chair of the Federal Reserve who recently took his second meeting with the central bank’s rate-setting committee, seems to be making a similar bet. *The Economist* does not know Mr Warsh’s views on meteors or volcanoes. But his peculiarly tight-lipped performance at a press conference on July 29th made markets quake. The ten-year Treasury yield jumped that day and finished the week above 4.7%, close to a three-year high. Stock prices, especially for rate-sensitive tech firms, wobbled—even though the Fed kept its interest rate unchanged. Mr Warsh hopes that if he keeps mum about what the Fed plans to do, markets will offer their own, helpfully independent, verdict on the economy.

He can probably afford the experiment, because a few tremors will do little harm if nothing ever happens. At worse, they nudge borrowing costs up, to compensate lenders for the extra uncertainty. But if something nasty does occur, then confusion about how the Fed might respond could worsen the resulting mess. Rather than reducing volatility, the Fed would be adding to it. Even Mr Warsh grants that, in a crisis, clear communication is “prudent”.

What are the chances that the Fed loses its leeway for ambiguity? Several medium-sized risks loom at once. The clearest is the sell-off in stocks related to artificial intelligence, both in America and, especially, [in South Korea](https://www.economist.com/business/2026/07/29/south-koreas-stock-market-boom-is-collapsing-spectacularly). When the dotcom bubble burst in 2000, the crash pulled the world economy into recession. Worryingly, today there are already signs of some big bets turning sour and causing investment firms to wobble. Situational Awareness, a multi-billion-dollar hedge fund, has taken spectacular losses over the past month. It was forced to unwind its positions so quickly that Citadel, a much larger fund, was able to scoop up most of its listed holdings at a discount.

For now, the damage looks contained. Some AI shares have bounced back a bit; the tech-heavy Nasdaq index, though down 6% from its peak in June, is still up since the start of the year.

But if America’s tech stocks lose more of their shine, its economy could be in trouble. These days, the dollar’s status as a haven owes much to how ravenous foreign investors are for American shares. Treasury bonds do not look so attractive after years of gaping budget deficits—and with inflation on the rise again. Last April, after Mr Trump announced his “Liberation Day” tariffs, America briefly saw what it looked like for international investors to dump all types of dollar asset at once. A protracted repeat is still a distant prospect, but could quickly turn nasty.

Another danger lies in the Strait of Hormuz. Since America’s shaky ceasefire with Iran [fell apart](https://www.economist.com/middle-east-and-africa/2026/07/28/new-strikes-in-iran-show-the-weakness-of-donald-trumps-deal), virtually no oil has traversed the strait, meaning [prices have surged](https://www.economist.com/finance-and-economics/2026/07/30/oil-prices-remain-highly-flammable). A barrel of brent crude, the global benchmark, now fetches $88, up from around $70 at the start of July. Petrol prices in America are well over $4 per gallon, from $3 or so before the conflict started. And few prices bother American consumers more. Sharp increases can easily destabilise spending, inflation expectations and confidence in the Fed.

Worse, Mr Trump seems determined to make Mr Warsh’s life difficult. The stopgap authority the president used to impose tariffs, most of his previous ones having been struck down by the Supreme Court in February, expired on July 24th. Since then the White House has thrown up new, wide-ranging trade barriers to replace the expired ones, and has announced heftier levies on countries including Canada and [Brazil](https://www.economist.com/the-americas/2026/07/19/brazils-much-loved-payments-system-has-drawn-donald-trumps-ire). Companies are still yet to fully pass price increases from previous levies on to shoppers. Any further jumps could reverse recent declines in goods inflation.

### **You only need to be unlucky once**

So there is plenty of potential not merely for something to happen, but for several things to happen at once. Perhaps the trickiest combination would be if stock markets calmed but oil and new tariffs pushed inflation up further. Usually, that would be a clear reason for the Fed’s governors to consider raising rates. Some have already voted to hike. But tightening monetary policy just ahead of November’s midterm elections would risk wrecking Mr Warsh’s relationship with the White House, reigniting ugly spats over the Fed’s independence. It is possible that, in an attempt to avoid this, Mr Warsh is already trying to get the market to do his tightening work for him. In other words, by loudly reiterating his commitment to the Fed’s inflation target, without actually saying he will raise rates, he might hope long-term bond yields will rise and stop him from needing to do so.

The danger is that higher yields might instead reflect the risk of Mr Warsh’s experiment going wrong. “Nothing ever happens” can be a good bet. But central bankers’ reputations are made when something does.
