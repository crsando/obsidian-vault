> 📌 **核心摘要**
>
> 本文批评美国白宫首席贸易顾问纳瓦罗提出的“中国转运骗局”论调，认为其将正常供应链重组与非法转运混为一谈，暴露了特朗普政府混乱的贸易政策。
> • 纳瓦罗在论文中指控43国协助中国逃避美国关税，其论文称中国通过“转运”规避关税，每年非法流动高达3030亿美元。
> • 文章指出，高关税导致美国从中国进口份额从21%降至9%，同时越南、墨西哥等“连接”经济体对美出口激增，这是可预见的市场反应。
> • 区分合法供应链重组与非法转运：实质性转变和原产地规则是法律标准，而纳瓦罗的反对意见过于宽泛，几乎将全球贸易都视为共谋。
> • 若严格执行纳瓦罗的逻辑，美国将需要对全球贸易基础设施进行全景监控，这既不现实也会推高国内价格。

### 中文译文

## 白宫关于中国转运“骗局”的荒谬说法

### 对中国商品征收高关税产生了可预见的影响

图片来源：路透社

在G.K.切斯特顿关于栅栏的寓言中，那些急于拆除看似无用结构的人被敦促先理解它们最初为何被建造。切斯特顿的栅栏长期以来一直是美国老一辈保守派的公认智慧，但唐纳德·特朗普的首席贸易理论家彼得·纳瓦罗似乎没有注意到这一点。在纳瓦罗先生的白宫办公室发表的一篇题为“转运大骗局”的新论文中，从日本到约旦的数十个国家被指控协助中国逃避美国关税的阴谋。尽管这一说法荒谬，但该论文仍然生动地阐述了特朗普政府在贸易上的混乱思维。

先从“大转移”说起，这是纳瓦罗先生对2018年特朗普先生对华商品第一波关税后出现的转移所用的术语。当时，自由贸易者警告说，中国与世界其他国家之间的关税税率差距会适得其反。这将产生不可抗拒的逃税动机，无论是合法还是其他方式，同时抑制增长。美国在1940年代作为基于非歧视的全球贸易体系的主要缔造者时，对此深有体会。

![Chart: The Economist](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c181cc90b148178809cf63b18a5c37f/3c181cc9-0b14-813e-bdfc-e2ffbe096514-b4ed5bde2a26703cfdc94c6973fc78b9.png)

特朗普先生还是不管不顾地往前冲。在[中国](https://www.economist.com/china/2026/04/21/why-chinas-exports-will-keep-on-rising)与世界其他地区之间的平均关税税率差距从2018年初的0.9个百分点扩大到去年的29个百分点（见图表）。可预见的事情发生了：美国进口中来自中国的份额从21%暴跌至9%，而来自其他国家的份额飙升。在越南、马来西亚和墨西哥等“连接”经济体中，从中国的进口和对美国的出口同步上升。特朗普先生和纳瓦罗先生现在面临着自己造成的问题。

纳瓦罗先生将对更低关税的追求称为“转运大骗局背后的金融引擎”，呼应了特朗普先生去年威胁要对“转运”（一个定义松散的术语，通常指通过第三国转运的中国商品）征收40%关税的威胁。然而，重组供应链以改变货物的原产国并减少其关税负担并不是骗局——不像仅仅贴上新的标签来隐瞒其来源那样。

传统的法律测试是“实质性转变”，根据美国贸易当局的说法，它要求“在形式、外观、性质或特征上发生根本性变化”。这不是一个清晰的区分。大多数自由贸易协定包括为每种产品线精心谈判的“原产地规则”（ROO）。新加坡智囊团欣里希基金会的黛博拉·埃尔姆斯指出，为《跨太平洋伙伴关系协定》（一项被特朗普先生于2017年废弃的12国贸易协定）的原产地规则进行讨价还价花了近十年时间。

即便是纳瓦罗先生也对“合法制造和实质性转变”与“过境贸易和原产国转变”之间的区别进行了粗略的承认。事实上，他整理了五个公私部门对“非法转运流”的估计，每年高达3030亿美元——这相当于美国2018年从中国进口的56%，这一数字令人难以置信。然而，即便是其中最令人信服的一项——高盛的分析发现，2023年有价值400亿美元的中国商品被“表面上的再出口”到美国——也没有声称这些流动是非法的。毫无疑问，其中一部分是海关欺诈，美国有权对此进行打击。特朗普先生的贸易欺诈工作组声称自去年成立以来已从逃税者手中追回10亿美元。

如果纳瓦罗先生仅仅关注非法行为，那么第三国建造工厂用中国零部件生产商品本不应构成问题。然而，他抨击那些“未必申报为中国原产”的商品，但这些商品涉及“中国原产的投入品或零部件、中国所有权或融资、与中国供应商或制造商的关系、在中国的生产步骤[或]中国原产的运输路线历史”。

这是一个极其宽泛的反对意见。现代制造业的结构是一个全球价值链网络，在每个生产阶段将产能分布到各国。但如果接近中国的投入品或企业就是参与反美骗局的共谋证据，那么美国就是在与贸易本身为敌。埃尔姆斯女士表示，这是“对贸易的重新定义，使得一切都成了转运”。

纳瓦罗先生的论文描述了“影子转运网络”的“功能架构”，该网络横跨43个国家，占美国非中国进口商品的70%以上。从东南亚的组装中心到阿联酋和加拿大的物流中心，所谓的骗局蜿蜒穿过“生产端转型主张、物流端运输渠道、加工区、海运门户、陆路走廊、保税仓库和重新开具发票的系统”。这或多或少就是全球贸易的全部基础设施；对其进行监管需要一个全景监视系统。不过，纳瓦罗先生愿意一试。他含糊地威胁要建立一个人工智能驱动的“侦探边境”系统。

经历了这一切，你可能会想，纳瓦罗先生是否认为进入美国的进口商品应该包含任何中国成分？如果是，该是多少？一个极端（但连贯）的答案是设定中国成分的最高上限，并禁止或对所有超过该上限的进口商品征税。特朗普先生40%的转运关税随后可以用来胁迫那些出口中中国成分过高的国家。

这并未成为现实，大概是因为这样做会推高美国公众的价格，而他们已经对生活成本[感到愤怒](https://www.economist.com/business/2026/07/20/dont-blame-supermarkets-for-your-expensive-groceries)。尽管如此，纳瓦罗先生这篇非同寻常的论文还是起到了一定作用。它再次表明，他的老板对全球贸易秩序的攻击所制造的混乱。■

### English Original

## The White House’s absurd claim of a Chinese transshipment “scam”

### High tariffs on Chinese goods have had predictable effects

Photograph: Reuters

IN G.K. CHESTERTON’S parable of the fence, those eager to tear down seemingly useless structures are urged to understand why they were built in the first place. Chesterton’s fence has long been received wisdom among American conservatives of a certain age, but Peter Navarro, Donald Trump’s chief trade ideologue, seems to have missed the memo. In a new paper published by Mr Navarro’s office in the White House, titled “The Great Transshipment Scam”, dozens of countries from Japan to Jordan stand accused of abetting a Chinese plot to dodge American tariffs. Absurd as this claim is, the paper is nevertheless a vivid exposition of the Trump administration’s muddled thinking on trade.

Start with the “great reallocation”, Mr Navarro’s term for the shifts that followed Mr Trump’s first wave of tariffs on Chinese goods in 2018. At the time, free-traders warned that a gap in tariff rates between China and the rest of the world would backfire. It would create an irresistible incentive to evade duties, legally or otherwise, while crimping growth. America understood this well when, in the 1940s, it was the primary author of a global trading system based on non-discrimination.

Mr Trump charged ahead anyway. The average tariff-rate differential between [China](https://www.economist.com/china/2026/04/21/why-chinas-exports-will-keep-on-rising) and the rest of the world widened from 0.9 percentage points at the start of 2018 to 29 points last year (see chart). The predictable happened: the share of American imports coming from China tumbled from 21% to 9%, while that from other countries soared. In “connector” economies such as Vietnam, Malaysia and Mexico, imports from China and exports to America rose in tandem. Messrs Trump and Navarro now face a problem of their own making.

Mr Navarro calls the pursuit of lower tariffs the “financial engine behind the Great Transshipment Scam”, echoing Mr Trump’s threat last year to impose a 40% tariff on “transshipment” (a loosely defined term that usually refers to Chinese goods rerouted through third countries). Yet reorganising supply chains to shift a shipment’s country of origin and reduce its tariff liability is no scam—unlike concealing its origin by simply slapping on a new label.

The traditional legal test is “substantial transformation”, requiring a “fundamental change in form, appearance, nature or character”, according to American trade authorities. This is not a clean distinction. Most free-trade deals include painstakingly negotiated “rules of origin” (ROO) for each product line. Haggling over ROOs for the Trans-Pacific Partnership, a 12-country trade deal scuttled by Mr Trump in 2017, took the better part of a decade, notes Deborah Elms of the Hinrich Foundation, a think-tank in Singapore.

Even Mr Navarro offers a cursory nod to the difference between “legitimate manufacturing and substantial transformation” on the one hand and “pass-through trade and origin shifting” on the other. Indeed, he collates five private- and public-sector estimates of “illegal transshipment flows”, amounting to as much as $303bn—an improbable 56% of America’s imports from China in 2018—each year. Yet even the most convincing of these, a Goldman Sachs analysis finding that $40bn-worth of Chinese goods were “superficially re-exported” to America in 2023, does not claim that the flows are illegal. Undoubtedly, some proportion is customs fraud, which America is within its rights to clamp down on. Mr Trump’s Trade Fraud Task Force claims to have recovered $1bn from tariff dodgers since launching last year.

Were Mr Navarro exercised about illegality alone, third countries building factories making goods from Chinese components should pose no problem. Yet he rails against “goods that are not necessarily declared as Chinese” but which involve “China-origin inputs or components, Chinese ownership or financing, relationships with Chinese suppliers or manufacturers, China-based production steps [or] China-origin routing histories”.

This is a staggeringly broad objection. Modern manufacturing is structured as a global network of value chains, distributing capacity across countries in each stage of production. But if proximity to Chinese inputs or firms is evidence of complicity in an anti-American scam, then America is at war with trade itself. It is a “redefinition of trade such that everything is transshipment”, says Ms Elms.

Mr Navarro’s paper describes the “functional architecture” of the “shadow transshipment network” that stretches across 43 countries, making up over 70% of America’s non-Chinese imports. From assembly hubs in South-East Asia to logistics centres in the United Arab Emirates and Canada, the supposed scam snakes through “production-side transformation claims, logistics-side routing channels, processing zones, maritime gateways, overland corridors, bonded warehouses and re-invoicing systems”. This is, more or less, the entire infrastructure of global trade; policing it would require a panopticon. Still, Mr Navarro is willing to try. He vaguely threatens setting up an AI-enabled “detective border” system.

After all this, you may wonder whether Mr Navarro thinks imports to America should contain any Chinese content at all. If so, how much? An extreme (though coherent) answer would be to set a maximum threshold for Chinese content, and ban or tax all imports that breach it. Mr Trump’s 40% transshipment tariffs could then be used to strong-arm countries whose exports contain too much from China.

That has not come to pass, presumably because doing so would drive up prices for an American public [already enraged](https://www.economist.com/business/2026/07/20/dont-blame-supermarkets-for-your-expensive-groceries) about the cost of living. Still, Mr Navarro’s extraordinary paper has performed a service. It is yet another illustration of the mess his boss’s attack on the global trading order has created. ■
