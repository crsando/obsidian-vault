### 中文译文

## 马克·卡尼必须警惕全面贸易战

他最新的关税威胁是对等的。但局势可能升级。

2025年2月，当唐纳德·特朗普首次下令对加拿大商品征收关税时，他表示这是为了迫使加拿大阻止毒品和移民穿越美国北部边境。他很快放弃了这一理由，转而抛出其他一连串不满：贸易逆差、国家安全、加拿大的乳制品行业、一项加拿大税收、一则电视广告、加拿大与中国的贸易、野火烟雾。他以黑帮老大般的含糊口吻反复念叨着吞并加拿大、使其成为美国“珍视”的第51个州。

世界上最强权人物的任性妄为，如今已将加拿大总理马克·卡尼推到了一场灾难性贸易战的边缘。8月25日，加拿大政府宣布对美国商品征收价值200亿美元的等额报复性关税。问题在于，美国经济规模是加拿大的13倍，且更有能力应对。加拿大的关税将于9月8日生效。卡尼先生必须在此之前找到化解这一严重威胁其国家经济的方法。这需要他使出浑身解数。

最新对抗的导火索是卡尼先生于8月21日决定[退出贸易协议](https://www.economist.com/the-americas/2026/08/23/the-costs-of-defying-donald-trump-are-mounting-for-canada)，而特朗普先生此前曾称该协议“棒极了”，并即将达成。如果总理的说法可信，加拿大几乎没有选择。卡尼先生表示，美方在谈判后期提出要求，包括加拿大卡车工厂继续面临高关税、加拿大限制[与其他国家的贸易协议](https://www.economist.com/the-americas/2026/01/15/mark-carney-is-on-a-mission-to-trade-with-the-world)，并削弱对法语和文化的保护——这对魁北克的分离主义者来说无异于火上浇油。（美方将谈判破裂归咎于加方，但对其原因语焉不详。）

稳定与美国的经济关系，对于卡尼先生吸引基础设施投资、推动加拿大经济摆脱其对不理性邻国的依赖至关重要。因此在过去的争端中，总理明智地试图拖延时间、避免激进，并尽一切努力维持谈判。他废除了特朗普先生不喜欢的税收，取消了从前任那里继承的大部分报复性关税，并拒绝了多次切断对美能源出口的呼吁。但这次，美国的要求如此极端，任何总理都无法低声下气地接受。

对于北美来说，这是一个危险的时刻。谈判破裂后不久，美国的关税便已生效。特朗普先生表示，他将从1月1日起对加拿大汽车、卡车和汽车零部件额外征收50%的关税。加拿大在面对与美国的全面贸易战时异常脆弱。其约三分之二的出口商品销往南部邻国。与巴西这样的大宗商品出口大国不同——其出口大部分在现货市场销售——加拿大的大部分出口深度融入美国供应链。这使得加拿大企业很难迅速找到替代买家。32万亿美元的美国经济可以承受在加拿大2.5万亿美元的经济中失去市场份额；加拿大则不然。

此外，加拿大的报复性关税只会加深美国关税带来的经济痛苦。限制能源出口或中止安全合作，最终对加拿大的伤害将超过对美国。局势升级还将损害加拿大急需投资的前景。

因此，在表明立场之后，卡尼先生应设法收回其报复性关税。如果这在政治上不可行，那么至少应使其尽可能具有针对性且易于撤销。一个例子是聚焦于11月中期选举中的摇摆州，例如密歇根州，该州承担了美国与加拿大之间的大部分贸易，其政界人士可能对特朗普先生有一定影响力。公众已经有一些抱怨，认为与如此亲密的盟友挑起争端对美国利益造成无谓的损害。

双方官员还应铭记，升级对抗将给各自国家带来长期代价。目前已经清楚的是，美国现有的关税将难以撤销。谈判失败的另一个原因是，美国的受保护产业曾大力游说特朗普政府，反对降低关税。

只有特朗普先生有权结束他对加拿大发起的这场不合逻辑的讨伐。他迄今未表现出这样做的意向。卡尼先生已证明，对特朗普先生采取温和回应未必会在政治上造成毁灭性后果。现在，他有两周时间去完成一件难度大得多的事情。他需要达成一份比美国最新提议对加拿大更有利的协议。但要实现这一点，他必须在不引发加拿大无法取胜、且将使其遭受不成比例伤害的升级循环的前提下，回绝美国这位行事过火的总统。

### English Original

## Mark Carney must beware an all-out trade war

## Mark Carney must beware an all-out trade war

His latest tariff threats are proportionate. But the situation could escalate

When Donald Trump first ordered tariffs on Canadian goods in February 2025, he said they were needed to force Canada to stop drugs and migrants crossing America’s northern border. He soon dropped that justification only to spew out a litany of other grievances: the trade deficit, national security, Canada’s dairy industry, a Canadian tax, a television advertisement, Canada’s trade with China, wildfire smoke. He repeatedly mused, with a mob boss’s ambiguity, about annexing Canada and making it America’s “cherished” 51st state.

The caprice of the world’s most powerful man has now taken Mark Carney, Canada’s prime minister, to the brink of a disastrous trade war. On August 25th the Canadian government announced $20bn-worth of dollar-for-dollar retaliatory tariffs on American goods. The trouble is that America’s economy is 13 times larger than Canada’s and far better equipped to cope. Canada’s tariffs go into effect on September 8th. Mr Carney has until then to find a way to defuse a grave threat to his country’s economy. It will take all his guile.

The trigger for the latest hostilities was Mr Carney’s decision on August 21st [to walk away from a trade deal](https://www.economist.com/the-americas/2026/08/23/the-costs-of-defying-donald-trump-are-mounting-for-canada) that Mr Trump had earlier declared as “wonderful” and on the brink of agreement. If the prime minister’s version of events is to be believed, Canada had little choice. Mr Carney says that the Americans added late demands that Canadian truck plants remain subject to high tariffs, that Canada curb its [trade deals with other countries](https://www.economist.com/the-americas/2026/01/15/mark-carney-is-on-a-mission-to-trade-with-the-world) and weaken its protections for French language and culture—a red rag to separatists in Quebec. (The Americans blame the Canadians for the collapse of the negotiations, but have been vague on the reasons why.)

The stabilisation of economic relations with the United States is essential to Mr Carney’s efforts to attract investment for the infrastructure needed to diversify Canada’s economy away from its irrational neighbour. So in past disputes the prime minister has wisely tried to play for time, avoid aggression and make every effort to keep negotiations alive. He repealed a tax that Mr Trump did not like, removed most of the retaliatory tariffs inherited from his predecessor and resisted repeated calls to cut off energy exports to the United States. But this time, America’s demands were so extreme that no prime minister could have meekly accepted them.

It is a dangerous moment for North America. America’s tariffs went into force shortly after the talks collapsed. Mr Trump has said that he will slap further duties of 50% on Canadian cars, trucks and auto parts from January 1st. Canada is unusually vulnerable to outright trade war with the United States. About two-thirds of its exported goods are sold to its southern neighbour. Unlike commodity giants such as Brazil whose exports are mostly sold in spot markets, most of Canada’s exports are deeply integrated into American supply chains. This makes it hard for Canadian firms to find alternative buyers quickly. The $32trn American economy can tolerate losing market share in Canada’s $2.5trn economy; Canada, not so much.

Add to that the fact that Canada’s counter-tariffs will only deepen the economic pain from America’s. Curbing energy exports or withholding security co-operation would ultimately hurt Canada more than it would hurt the United States. Escalation will also damage the case for much-needed investment in Canada.

And so, having made his point, Mr Carney should look for ways to walk back his retaliatory tariffs. If that is politically unfeasible, they should at least be made as targeted and easy to reverse as possible. One example is to focus on swing states in November’s midterm elections, such as Michigan, which do most of America’s trade with Canada, and whose politicians may have some sway with Mr Trump. There is already some public grumbling about the needless harm to American interests from picking a fight with such a close ally.

Officials on both sides should also bear in mind the long-term costs they are imposing on their countries through escalation. It is already clear that the existing American tariffs will be hard to unwind. One other reason the talks failed is that protected industries in the United States lobbied the Trump administration hard against lowering tariffs.

Only Mr Trump has the power to end his illogical crusade against Canada. He has shown no inclination to do so. Mr Carney has demonstrated that a moderate response to Mr Trump need not be politically ruinous. Now he has two weeks to pull off something much harder. He needs a deal that is better for Canada than America’s latest offer. But to get that, he needs to rebuff America’s intemperate president without triggering a cycle of escalation that Canada cannot win, and which will harm it disproportionately.
