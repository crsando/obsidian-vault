### 中文译文

## 目前，中国的人形机器人还不够智能，还无法取代你的工作

2025年3月，在上海一家由机器人公司AgiBot运营的工厂里，工人们指导人形机器人执行任务。中国在具身人工智能系统上的建设，沿用了其在电动汽车和太阳能电池板上实施的产业政策蓝图。路透社

中国柳州——在中国南方的一个训练中心内，100多个人形机器人整齐地站成几排。12名戴着耳机的人类训练师指导其中一些机器人完成简单任务——分拣板条箱、包装面条、冲泡咖啡。

这些机器人行动缓慢而笨拙。新手训练师可能需要300次尝试才能让它们产生一次可用的动作；而经验丰富的训练师则每50次尝试就能实现一次。

机器人制造商优必选去年10月中标了广西壮族自治区政府1800万美元的招标项目，为该训练设施提供人形机器人和相关硬件。其使命是：为所谓的“具身人工智能”——能够在物理世界中感知、决策和行动的机器人——生成训练数据。训练师使用耳机从机器人的视角观察，并使用装有传感器的手持控制器，将自己的动作转化为机器人的动作。

教人形机器人处理劳动密集型工作，未来或许能满足一项迫切需求。中国人口正在老龄化和萎缩，削弱了其工业基础的一项关键人口优势：庞大的劳动力储备。此外，中国的[军事研究人员已经探索了](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/robot-dogs-ai-drone-swarms-how-china-could-use-deepseek-an-era-war-2025-10-27/)机器人和[人工智能](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/chinese-military-researchers-tap-us-ai-models-train-defence-systems-2026-07-31/)在未来[武器系统](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/how-us-military-funding-propelled-chinas-robot-dogs-2026-08-18/)中的潜在用途。

路透社4月探访的柳州中心，旨在向工厂出售机器人训练数据。但三名员工承认，这个依赖补贴的项目目前尚无明确的盈利路径，理由是运营成本高，且在新兴市场中其数据价格偏低。

优必选、广西壮族自治区政府及柳州市政府均未就本文置评。

该设施捕捉到了中国人形机器人发展中的一个核心矛盾：政府的资金和制造业投资正飞速推进，超越了机器人在编排好的演示之外的实际操作能力——比如那些让这些机器人在网上爆红的功夫套路、[舞蹈和拳击比赛](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/chinas-humanoid-robots-ready-lunar-new-year-showtime-2026-02-16/)。

> 迟早会有100%的整合。问题只是谁整合谁，以及过程会有多难看。

Kevin Xu，互联资本创始人

尽管中国的机器人制造商生产出世界领先的硬件，但人形机器人缺乏通用工作所需的智能，限制了其实际用途，六位行业高管和研究人员告诉路透社。关键在于，它们在需要直觉或偏离程序化流程的情况下会表现困难。

“机器人的智商太低，”人工智能机器人初创企业元力灵机（Yuanli Lingji）的联合创始人兼首席执行官唐文斌在三月份的一次行业研讨会上表示。“我们看到的大部分场景，其实是用跳舞‘伪装’成工作。”

路透社对中国的人形机器人热潮进行的调查，基于数百份公司文件和政府采购记录，以及对工厂、机器人训练中心和人形机器人展厅的走访，以及对包括高管、投资者和研究人员在内的40人的采访。调查揭示了政府雄心与机器人能力之间日益扩大的差距——导致市场出现产能超过商业需求、主要参与者依赖补贴以及价格不断下降的局面。

4月，人形机器人正在柳州接受训练。随着中国人口老龄化和减少，教会机器处理劳动密集型工作可能满足一项迫切需求。REUTERS/Laurie Chen

一名人类训练师正在教机器人如何包装面条。行业高管和研究人员表示，人形机器人目前缺乏通用工作所需的智能。REUTERS/Laurie Chen

根据美银全球研究（BofA Global Research）的数据，去年人形机器人行业在全球出货约2万台，其中中国制造商占95%。中国工业和信息化部官员甘晓斌在7月份表示，中国预计今年将制造超过10万台人形机器人。美银预测，到2030年，全球年出货量将达到120万台。

中国供应链主导地位的影响不仅限于商业竞争。7月，特朗普政府[禁止进口](https://www.reuters.com/world/trump-administration-ban-new-chinese-robots-inverters-protecting-us-ai-buildout-2026-07-28/)中国新型人形机器人，理由是保护美国人工智能产业免受干扰和网络攻击的威胁。

北京建设智能机器人的努力遵循了其应用于电动汽车和太阳能电池板的产业政策蓝图：补贴战略部门，提前建设产能，并激发激烈竞争以降低成本。此类投资服务于中国[赢得关键工业技术控制权](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/china-draws-red-lines-around-its-economic-model-ahead-eu-us-trade-talks-2026-08-02/)并取代西方竞争对手的雄心。

但分析师和行业高管表示，汽车和太阳能在中国加速生产时已是成熟行业。相比之下，人形机器人仍处于早期阶段，国内外需求有限，在灵活性、可靠性、智能性和成本方面的问题尚未解决。政府实体既是这些机器人的主要出资方，也是其客户——人为地提振了需求。

这一切都使中国在人形机器人上的押注前景更加冒险。

一些分析师质疑，当专为特定制造任务设计的机械臂更便宜、更可靠且已被广泛使用时，以人类形象制造的通用机器是否具有经济意义。

“目前的经济状况并不支持大规模部署，”分析师兼《Hello China Tech》通讯作者Poe Zhao表示。他说，然而中国的资本市场已经对人形机器人技术进行了“非常激进”的定价，投资者基本上是在押注机器人的未来用途。

赵认为，在家庭或护理设施等细分领域存在潜力，类人机器可以以最小的改动使用楼梯、门和工具。但短期内，人形机器人的市场“仍将集中在危险、重复和结构化的工作中。”

中国工业和信息化部及国务院新闻办公室未回应关于人形机器人发展的置评请求。

宇树科技（Unitree）的人形机器人在2026年世界机器人大会上表演集体舞蹈。到目前为止，对人形机器人的需求一直由政府采购和补贴推动，而非市场力量。路透社/Tingshu Wang

> 问题从最后的10厘米发展到最后的1厘米，甚至1毫米。

贾宝雄，北京通用人工智能研究院的研究科学家。

2025年5月，在北京的装配线模拟中，优必选（UBTech）机器人拿起一个物体。灵巧性仍是广泛商业部署的主要障碍。路透社/Tingshu Wang

美国投资公司Interconnected Capital的Kevin Xu表示，一些创始人将中国的人形机器人行业形容为“泡沫”。

“迟早100%会有一场整合，”徐表示，“只是涉及谁以及有多难看的问题。”

### 国家资金支撑的繁荣

根据最高经济规划机构国家发展和改革委员会的数据，中国有超过150家人形机器人公司。这超过了中国电动汽车品牌的数量。该机构未回应问题，于11月表示，企业在技术和商业模式成熟之前正涌入人形机器人领域。

公共采购数据显示，今年上半年，中国国家、省和地方政府至少花费了2.3亿美元购买人形机器人和相关物品，高于一年前的6200万美元和2024年同期的600万美元。路透社审查了近1000份已完成的招标，涵盖国有实体对机器人、培训系统、设备和示范项目的采购。

中国工信部和国资委于6月宣布，将要求10个省各确定至少20个用于人形机器人和AI系统真实世界训练的场地。

4月，电力公用事业公司国家电网宣布计划斥资10亿美元，用于配备AI的人形机器人、双臂机器人和机器狗，以进行电网维护和变电站巡检。据官方媒体报道，每台设备每年可节省7.4万至11.9万美元的劳动力成本，并减少人员从事危险工作的风险。国家电网未回应提问。

深圳的目标是到明年打造一个价值150亿美元的产业集群，涵盖超过1200家具身智能机器人公司。该市未回应置评请求。

8月，一名儿童在北京一家宇树科技门店与机器狗互动。中国目前有超过150家人形机器人公司，超过了中国电动汽车品牌的数量。路透社/王婷姝

支持这一建设的人士表示，这可能有助于中国企业建立低成本供应链，并比电动汽车巨头特斯拉等外国竞争对手更快地收集机器人训练数据，特斯拉正计划大力押注人形机器人。但这一推动使投资者和地方政府面临市场回调的风险。

研究中国经济的纽约亚洲协会政策研究所的Lizzi Lee表示，中国通过补贴、竞争者和产能涌入市场的策略旨在“启动饥饿游戏”，这是一种人为的超级竞争市场，加速学习和创新。在其他行业，这种策略多年来压低了价格——使得几乎所有中外竞争对手都无法盈利——但最终让中国留下了少数具有全球竞争力的幸存者。

“在这种模式下，浪费不是意外，而是发现机制的一部分，”Lee说，并指出不可避免的高失败率。然而，生产过剩“易启动，难收场”。

许多分析师表示，目前来看，中国的优必选（UBTech）和宇树（Unitree）可能成为幸存者。宇树——全球最大的机器狗销售商和第二大人形机器人生产商——已成为资本流入该领域的风向标。宇树股价在8月19日上海上市首日[飙升逾五倍](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/chinese-humanoid-robot-maker-unitree-set-jump-over-600-shanghai-debut-2026-08-19/)，公司估值约达500亿美元，随后回落。随着投资者试图区分拥有实际业务的机器人公司与只出售愿景的公司，中国那些考虑自己IPO的具身智能公司正密切关注宇树的进展。

宇树未回应关于IPO的置评请求。

### 政府驱动的需求

到目前为止，对人形机器人的需求是由政府采购和补贴而非市场力量创造的。中国工信部和国资委已承诺，将为拥有强大机器人训练项目的地区和公司提供优惠政策支持。

与早期电动汽车竞赛一样，地方政府现在竞相打造机器人冠军。** **北京经济技术开发区（亦庄）的文件显示，该区为机器人公司提供销售补贴、免费办公空间、计算支持以及数据购买券。

5月，零部件供应商领益智造（Lingyi iTech）在该区的一家“超级工厂”开始生产，为机器人品牌提供硬件、组装和测试。该工厂一期设定年产量1万台具身智能机器人，到2030年将扩大至50万台。

领益智造副总裁Philip Yang在5月的一次媒体参观中表示，市政府的支持使该工厂能在四个月内从规划进入生产。北京和亦庄官员未回应问题。

机器人公司的财务披露显示了政策相关需求的风险。

人群聚集在北京世界机器人大会。横幅上展示的是中国制造商 Deep Robotics 制造的机器人。REUTERS/Maxim Shemetov

北京机器人大会上的 Deep Robotics X30 机器狗。REUTERS/Maxim Shemetov

总部位于杭州的工业四足和轮式机器人制造商 Deep Robotics 披露，补贴约占其去年 420 万美元净利润的 42%。该公司在 2024 年和 2025 年仅售出四台人形机器人，并未回应有关补贴影响和人形机器人商业前景的问题。

公开文件和公告显示，去年第四季度，优必选（UBTech）赢得了至少 1.23 亿美元的新合同，向国有企业供应人形机器人硬件和相关技术。这些交易超过了该公司 2025 年人形机器人总收入 1.22 亿美元。该公司未向路透社置评，去年出货了 1,079 台全尺寸人形机器人。

竞争正在推低价格。摩根士丹利预计，2026 年人形机器人平均价格将下降 15%，此后将温和回升。乐聚机器人（Leju Robotics）制造的全尺寸人形机器人 Kuavo 的平均价格在 2025 年同比下降 26%，至约 46,000 美元。

中国机器人租赁平台 Sharebot 的首席营销官 Kevin Li 表示，公司用于促销活动的舞蹈机器人现在每天的租金为 440 至 600 美元，低于去年的约 1,500 美元。

### 生产力？

中国的人形机器人竞赛在一定程度上建立在奇观之上，机器人在电视上跳舞和打拳，以象征习近平主席提出的“新质生产力”——建立在技术创新和先进制造基础上的增长。

这些表演掩盖了一个根本性问题：这些机器能否在工作场所产生足够的价值，以证明其成本是合理的。

工厂经理通过正常运行时间和可靠性来衡量机器人性能。行业组织深圳市机器人协会秘书长Rachel Tan表示，传统机械臂通常优于人形机器人。

3月，北京一个创新中心的人形机器人。摩根士丹利预计，今年人形机器人的平均价格将下降15%。REUTERS/Maxim Shemetov

两位业内人士表示，在中国竞争极为激烈的汽车行业，生产线上已经以工业机器人为主，它们比人形机器人更快，也更容易控制。

小米曾表示，在其北京电动汽车工厂，91%的装配流程已实现自动化。车身车间使用700多台工业机器人进行点焊、铆接和粘接，以制造车架。

小米拒绝就本文置评，该公司正在训练人形机器人执行安装螺母等任务。路透社近期到访时，工厂车间里几乎没有工人。

吉利旗下极氪品牌去年与优必选合作，在其宁波工厂测试人形机器人，执行分拣材料、搬运箱子和处理零部件等任务。但吉利对路透社表示，目前，机械臂和自动导引车“处理着运行中装配线上的所有关键任务”。

### 从货架上取药瓶

人形机器人的早期商业案例正在涌现。人形机器人制造商Galbot正将机器人部署到中国超过24个城市的药店，以应对人员紧张并满足药品配送需求。

在北京的一家药店，Galbot的机器人在收到数字订单后从货架上取货。这个机器人有头、手臂和躯干，但在轮式底座上滑行。该系统使用摄像头（包括机器人手腕上的一个摄像头）以及夹持器和吸盘来识别和取出物品。

Galbot首席战略官赵玉丽（Zhao Yuli）表示，人形机器人可以在大约一分钟内拣选并准备一个产品。赵玉丽说，一家典型药店在30至50平方米的区域内储备有5,000至6,000种产品，这构成了一项可自动化的重复性拣选任务。Galbot表示，机器人的成功率超过95%，其中大部分失败是抓取物品困难，而不是选错产品。

5月，一台Galbot G1机器人在北京机器人展览会上演示药店工作。该公司正在部分中国药店部署人形机器人，这是该技术早期商业应用之一。路透社/王天树（Tingshu Wang）

该公司表示，尚未对机器人与人工进行计时比较，但认为机器能够持续工作，可能使其总体效率与人类相当或更高——即使人类在单项任务上更快。

人形机器人的AI大脑与聊天机器人不同。DeepSeek和Kimi等聊天机器人由处理海量文本的大型语言模型驱动。工厂人形机器人需要处理大量视觉数据，并将其所见转化为物理动作：抓取一块织物；校准螺丝上的力。

公司将这些模型称为视觉-语言-动作模型。它们比大语言模型（LLM）更难训练，且发展仍处于早期阶段，因为它们需要数千小时的现实场景暴露。研究人员和高管表示，问题在于这些模型难以适应：机器人可能在一个工作站上经过训练后正确执行任务，但当物体、光照、角度、表面、时间或设备发生变化时就会失败。

北京通用人工智能研究院研究科学家贾宝雄（Jia Baoxiong）表示：“问题从最后的10厘米缩小到最后的1厘米，甚至1毫米。”

### 数据瓶颈

中国的人形机器人计划依赖于一个简单的等式：训练中投入更多机器人，就会获得更多数据。

上海科技投资人、市场数据与研究公司百观（Baiguan）的首席执行官 Robert Wu 表示，提高具身人工智能模型的质量，必须先经历一个产能过剩的阶段。“要构建这些模型，首先需要数据；而为了获得数据，你需要庞大的已安装硬件基础设施来采集数据。”

分析师 Poe Zhao 援引行业估计称，该行业目前拥有大约 50 万小时的高质量训练数据，但机器人要实现真正的物理智能，需要 1 亿小时。他说：“这是一个数量级的缺口，无论是资本还是规模都无法迅速弥合。”

其他努力则聚焦于提升人形机器人的“大脑”。中国初创公司 X Square Robot 在 [4 月的公司活动](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/after-running-dancing-chinese-robot-firms-target-household-chores-2026-04-21/) 上表示，该公司一直使用从 100 个家庭中收集的数据来训练其模型，让系统接触不同的光线、杂乱环境和陌生物体，这些因素可能会干扰那些在受控条件下训练的机器人。

### 在泡沫破裂中生存

许多机器人公司的创始人和投资者预测，整合可能从 2026 年底或 2027 年开始，因为他们预计中国官员将收紧补贴，并将支持集中在最可靠的参与者身上。

“这些公司中的每一家都知道，最终的商业目标仍然遥遥无期，”投资者吴说。“这就是为什么他们现在拼命地尽可能多地获取资金——为自己赢得足够的时间，以便在泡沫破裂后生存下来。”

美银全球研究的李敏（Ming Lee）表示，中国的零部件价格已经下降得如此之快，以至于海外竞争对手难以跟上步伐。

特斯拉（Tesla）凸显了这一差距。其Optimus人形机器人尚未公开销售。CEO埃隆·马斯克（Elon Musk）声称的约2万美元目标价格是大规模生产下的长期目标。然而，摩根士丹利（Morgan Stanley）去年估计，Optimus Gen 2型号的低产量硬件物料清单成本在5万至6万美元之间。

特斯拉未回应置评请求。

一些分析师表示，中国机器人产业可能会经历一场痛苦的整合，但很可能保持其整体全球优势。即使破产浪潮席卷人形机器人制造商，中国的供应链仍将存在，可能使外国竞争对手陷入对中国硬件的依赖。

一支来自阿联酋大学团队的中国机器人在韩国举行的RoboCup 2026上训练。该机器人在绊倒后重新站起来。路透社/Ju-min Park

这种动态在7月韩国举行的RoboCup 2026上得到了展示，人形机器人组成的队伍互相进行足球比赛。韩国人形机器人制造商AeiROBOT的经理Kim Ho-joung表示，以前，队伍们会自带机器人参加这个年度国际机器人与人工智能竞赛。现在，几乎所有队伍都使用中国机器人。

尽管中国拥有基础设施优势，但人形机器人对于广泛商业应用来说仍然太慢且容易出错。

在柳州训练中心，路透社观察到一个人形机器人在搬运板条箱时表现挣扎。一位工作人员表示，这些机器人仅能以人类速度或产出的20%完成简单任务。

但它们可能会在两到三年内赶上，这位员工补充道。

## China’s humanoid robots aren’t smart enough to take your job

Workers instruct humanoid robots to perform tasks at a factory in Shanghai run by robotics firm AgiBot in March 2025. China's buildout of embodied-AI systems follows the industrial-policy blueprint it deployed for electric vehicles and solar panels. REUTERS

LIUZHOU, China - Inside a training center in southern China, more than 100 humanoid robots stand in neat rows. A dozen human trainers wearing headsets guide some of them through simple tasks – sorting crates, packaging noodles, making coffee.

The humanoids are slow and awkward. A novice trainer might get them to produce one usable movement in 300 attempts; an experienced trainer, one in 50.

Robot maker UBTech won an $18 million tender from the Guangxi regional government last October to supply the training facility with humanoids and related hardware. The mission: to produce training data for so-called embodied AI – robots able to perceive, decide and act in the physical world. The trainers use headsets to see from the robots’ perspective and handheld controllers fitted with sensors to translate their own movements into the robots’ actions.

Teaching humanoids to handle labor-intensive work could one day meet an acute need. China’s population is aging and shrinking, eroding a critical demographic advantage for its industrial base: a vast pool of workers. And China’s [military researchers have explored](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/robot-dogs-ai-drone-swarms-how-china-could-use-deepseek-an-era-war-2025-10-27/) the potential uses of robotics and [artificial intelligence](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/chinese-military-researchers-tap-us-ai-models-train-defence-systems-2026-07-31/) in future [weapons systems](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/how-us-military-funding-propelled-chinas-robot-dogs-2026-08-18/).

The Liuzhou center, which Reuters visited in April, aims to sell robot-training data to factories. But three staffers acknowledged the subsidy-dependent project currently has no clear path to profitability, citing high operating costs and low prices for its data in the nascent market.

UBTech and the Guangxi and Liuzhou governments didn’t comment for this story.

The facility captures a central tension in China’s humanoid push: Government funding and manufacturing investment are racing ahead of the robots’ ability to operate outside of choreographed demonstrations – like the kung fu routines, [dances and boxing matches](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/chinas-humanoid-robots-ready-lunar-new-year-showtime-2026-02-16/) that have made the machines internet sensations.

> There will 100% be a consolidation sooner or later. It's just a matter of who and how ugly.

Kevin Xu, founder of Interconnected Capital

Though China’s robot makers produce world-beating hardware, the humanoids lack the intelligence needed for general-purpose work, limiting their practical uses, a half-dozen industry executives and researchers told Reuters. Critically, they struggle with situations requiring intuition or deviation from programmed routines.

“The robots' IQ is too low,” said Tang Wenbin, co-founder and CEO of AI robotics venture Yuanli Lingji, at a March industry panel. “A lot of what we see is dancing disguised as working.”

This Reuters examination of China’s humanoid boom is based on hundreds of corporate filings and government procurement records; visits to factories, robot-training centers and humanoid showrooms; and interviews with 40 people including executives, investors and researchers. It reveals a widening gap between government ambitions and robot capabilities – resulting in a market where manufacturing capacity outpaces commercial demand, key players rely on subsidies and prices are falling.

Humanoid robots undergo training in Liuzhou in April. Teaching the machines to handle labor-intensive work could meet an acute need as China's population is aging and shrinking. REUTERS/Laurie Chen

A human trainer teaches a robot how to package noodles. Humanoids currently lack the intelligence needed for general-purpose work, industry executives and researchers say. REUTERS/Laurie Chen

The humanoid-robot industry shipped about 20,000 units globally last year, with Chinese manufacturers accounting for 95%, according to BofA Global Research. Gan Xiaobin, an official at China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology, said in July the country expects to build more than 100,000 humanoids this year. BofA forecasts 1.2 million annual shipments globally by 2030.

The implications of China's supply-chain dominance extend beyond commercial competition. In July, the Trump administration [banned imports](https://www.reuters.com/world/trump-administration-ban-new-chinese-robots-inverters-protecting-us-ai-buildout-2026-07-28/) of new Chinese humanoids, citing the need to protect the U.S. AI industry from threats of disruption and cyberattacks.

Beijing’s effort to build intelligent robots follows the industrial-policy blueprint it deployed for electric vehicles and solar panels: Subsidize a strategic sector, build capacity early, and stoke fierce competition to drive down costs. Such investment serves China’s ambition to [win control of critical industrial technology](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/china-draws-red-lines-around-its-economic-model-ahead-eu-us-trade-talks-2026-08-02/) and displace Western competitors.

But cars and solar were mature industries when China accelerated production. Humanoids, by contrast, remain an early-stage technology with limited domestic or foreign demand and unresolved problems in dexterity, reliability, intelligence and cost, analysts and industry executives say. Government entities are at once major financiers and customers of the same robots – artificially boosting demand.

That all makes China’s gamble on humanoids a riskier prospect.

Some analysts question whether a general-purpose machine built in humans’ image makes economic sense when robotic arms – purpose-built for specific manufacturing tasks – are cheaper, more reliable and already in wide use.

“The economics do not support widespread deployment today,” said Poe Zhao, an analyst and author of the Hello China Tech newsletter. Yet China's capital markets have priced humanoid technology "very aggressively," with investors essentially betting on the robots’ future usefulness, he said.

Zhao sees potential in niches such as homes or care facilities, where a human-like machine could use stairs, doors and tools with minimal modification. But in the near term, the market for humanoids “will remain concentrated in dangerous, repetitive and structured work.”

China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and its State Council Information Office didn’t respond to requests for comment about humanoid development.

Humanoid robots made by Unitree demonstrate a group dance during the 2026 World Robot Conference in Beijing. So far, demand for humanoids has been driven by government purchases and subsidies rather than market forces. REUTERS/Tingshu Wang

> The problem goes from the last 10 centimeters to the last 1 centimeter, even 1 millimeter.

Jia Baoxiong, a research scientist at the Beijing Institute for General Artificial Intelligence.

A UBTech robot picks up an object during an assembly-line simulation in Beijing in May 2025. Dexterity remains a major hurdle for widespread commercial deployment. REUTERS/Tingshu Wang

Kevin Xu, of U.S. investment firm Interconnected Capital, said some founders have characterized China’s humanoid sector as a “bubble.”

“There will 100% be a consolidation sooner or later,” Xu said. “It's just a matter of who and how ugly.”

### BOOM BUILT ON STATE MONEY

China has more than 150 humanoid-robot companies, according to the National Development and Reform Commission, the top economic planner. That exceeds the number of Chinese electric vehicle brands. The agency, which didn’t respond to questions, said in November that companies were crowding into humanoids before the technology and business models had matured.

The country’s national, provincial and local governments spent at least $230 million buying humanoids and related items in the first half – up from $62 million a year earlier and $6 million in the corresponding 2024 period, public procurement data show. Reuters reviewed nearly 1,000 fulfilled tenders covering state-entity purchases of robots, training systems, equipment and demonstration projects.

China’s industry ministry and its state-assets regulator announced in June that they would require 10 provinces to each identify at least 20 sites for real-world training of humanoids and AI systems.

In April, power utility State Grid announced plans to spend $1 billion on AI-enabled humanoids, dual-arm robots and robot dogs for grid maintenance and substation inspections. Each unit would save $74,000 to $119,000 in annual labor costs and reduce human exposure to hazardous work, state media reported. State Grid didn’t respond to questions.

Shenzhen aims to build a $15 billion industry cluster comprising more than 1,200 embodied-intelligence robotics companies by next year. The city didn’t respond to a request for comment.

A child interacts with a robot dog at a Unitree store in Beijing in August. China now has more than 150 humanoid-robot companies, exceeding the number of Chinese EV brands. REUTERS/Tingshu Wang

Proponents of the buildout say that it could help Chinese companies create low-cost supply chains and collect robot-training data faster than foreign rivals such as electric vehicle giant Tesla, which aims to go big on humanoids. But the drive exposes investors and local governments to the risk of a market correction.

China’s strategy of flooding the market with subsidies, competitors and production capacity is designed to “kick start the Hunger Games,” an artificially hypercompetitive market that accelerates learning and innovation, said Lizzi Lee, who studies China’s economy at the Asia Society Policy Institute in New York. In other industries, the tactic has depressed prices for years – making it impossible for almost all Chinese and foreign competitors to profit – but ultimately left China with a handful of globally competitive survivors.

“Waste is not an accident in this model” but rather “part of the discovery mechanism,” Lee said, noting the inevitably high failure rate. Production overcapacity, however, “is easy to start but hard to unwind.”

For now, China’s UBTech and Unitree look like potential survivors, many analysts say. Unitree – the world’s largest seller of robot dogs and the second-largest humanoid producer – has become a bellwether for capital flowing into the sector. Shares of Unitree [soared more than five-fold](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/chinese-humanoid-robot-maker-unitree-set-jump-over-600-shanghai-debut-2026-08-19/) in their Shanghai trading debut on August 19, valuing the company at around $50 billion, before pulling back. Chinese embodied-AI firms eyeing their own IPOs are closely watching Unitree’s progress as investors try to distinguish between robot companies with tangible businesses from those selling visions.

Unitree didn’t respond to a request for comment about the IPO.

### STATE-DRIVEN DEMAND

So far, demand for humanoids has been created by government purchases and subsidies rather than market forces. China's industry ministry and state-assets regulator have pledged preferential policy support for regions and companies with strong robot-training programs.

As with the early EV race, local governments are now vying to create robotics champions.** **Beijing’s E-Town tech zone, also known as Yizhuang, offers robotics firms sales subsidies, free workspace, computing support and vouchers to purchase data, city documents show.

In May, components supplier Lingyi iTech began production at a “super factory” in the district, providing hardware, assembly and testing for robot brands. The facility’s first phase set a target of 10,000 embodied-intelligence robots annually, scaling to 500,000 by 2030.

Philip** **Yang, Lingyi’s vice president, during a media tour in May credited municipal support for enabling the plant to go from planning to production in four months. Beijing and Yizhuang officials didn’t respond to questions.

Financial disclosures by robotics firms show the risks of policy-linked demand.

A crowd gathers at the World Robot Conference in Beijing. The banners depict robots made by Chinese manufacturer Deep Robotics. REUTERS/Maxim Shemetov

A Deep Robotics X30 robot dog at the Beijing robot conference. REUTERS/Maxim Shemetov

Deep Robotics, a Hangzhou-based manufacturer of industrial quadruped and wheeled robots, has disclosed that subsidies accounted for about 42% of its $4.2 million net profit last year. The firm, which sold only four humanoids across 2024 and 2025, didn’t respond to questions about the impact of subsidies and the commercial outlook for humanoids.

In the fourth quarter of last year, UBTech won new contracts worth at least $123 million to supply state-owned enterprises with humanoid hardware and related technology, public filings and announcements show. Those deals exceeded the firm’s total 2025 humanoid revenue of $122 million. The company, which didn’t comment to Reuters, shipped 1,079 full-size humanoids last year.

Competition is driving down prices. Morgan Stanley expects the average price of a humanoid to decline 15% in 2026, before recovering modestly thereafter. The average price of Kuavo, a full-size humanoid made by Leju Robotics, fell 26% year-on-year in 2025 to about $46,000.

Kevin Li, chief marketing officer at Chinese robot-rental platform Sharebot, said dancing robots - which companies lease for promotional events - now rent for $440 to $600 a day, down from roughly $1,500 last year.

### PRODUCTIVE FORCES?

China's humanoid race has been built partly on spectacle, with robots boogeying and boxing on television to symbolize President Xi Jinping's “new quality productive forces” – growth built on technological innovation and advanced manufacturing.

The performances mask a fundamental question: whether the machines can generate enough value in the workplace to justify their cost.

Factory managers measure robot performance by uptime and reliability. Conventional robotic arms usually beat humanoids, said Rachel Tan, secretary-general of the Shenzhen Robotics Association, an industry group.

Humanoid robots at a Beijing innovation center in March. Morgan Stanley expects the average price of a humanoid to decline 15% this year. REUTERS/Maxim Shemetov

In China’s ultracompetitive auto sector, production lines are already dominated by industrial robots that are faster than humanoids and easier to control, two industry participants said.

At Xiaomi’s EV plant in Beijing, 91% of the assembly process is automated, the company has said. The body workshop uses over 700 industrial robots for spot welding, riveting and adhesive bonding to build the vehicle frame.

Xiaomi, which declined to comment for this story, is training humanoid robots for tasks such as installing nuts. When Reuters visited recently, the factory floor had few workers.

Geely's Zeekr brand partnered with UBTech last year to test humanoids at its Ningbo factory on tasks including sorting materials, moving boxes and handling components. But for now, Geely told Reuters, robotic arms and automated vehicles “handle all key tasks on the active assembly line.”

### PICKING PILL BOTTLES FROM SHELVES

Early commercial cases for humanoids are emerging. Humanoid maker Galbot is deploying robots in pharmacies across more than two dozen Chinese cities to address staffing constraints and meet medicine-delivery demand.

At a Beijing pharmacy, Galbot's robot picks products from shelves after receiving digital orders. The robot has a head, arms and torso but glides on a wheeled base. The system uses cameras, including one on the robot's wrist, together with a gripper and suction cup to identify and retrieve items.

Zhao Yuli, Galbot’s chief strategy officer, said a humanoid can pick and prepare a product in about one minute. A typical pharmacy stocks 5,000 to 6,000 products in a 30-to-50-square-meter area, Zhao said, creating a repetitive selection task that can be automated. The robots’ success rate exceeds 95%, Galbot says, with most failures involving difficulty grasping an item rather than selecting the wrong product.

A Galbot G1 robot demonstrates pharmacy work at a Beijing robotics exhibition in May. The company is deploying humanoid robots in some Chinese pharmacies, in one of the early commercial uses of the technology. REUTERS/Tingshu Wang

The company said it hadn’t timed the robots against human workers, but believed the machines’ ability to work continuously could make them equally or more efficient overall — even if humans are faster at individual tasks.

The AI brains of humanoids differ from those in chatbots. Chatbots such as DeepSeek and Kimi are powered by large language models that process huge amounts of text. A factory humanoid needs to process vast quantities of visual data and convert what it sees into physical action: Gripping a piece of fabric; calibrating force on a screw.

Companies call these vision-language-action models. They are much harder to train than LLMs, and their development is at an earlier stage, because they require thousands of hours of exposure to real-world scenarios. The problem, researchers and executives say, is these models struggle to adapt: A robot may execute a task correctly after training on one workstation but fail when the object, lighting, angle, surface, timing or equipment changes.

“The problem goes from the last 10 centimeters to the last 1 centimeter, even 1 millimeter,” said Jia Baoxiong, a research scientist at the Beijing Institute for General Artificial Intelligence.

### DATA BOTTLENECK

China's humanoid campaign relies on a simple equation: More robots in training equals more data.

A phase of overproduction is necessary to improve embodied AI models, said Robert Wu, a Shanghai-based tech investor and CEO of Baiguan, a market data and research firm. “To build those models, you first need data; and to get data, you need a massive installed hardware base out there collecting it.”

The sector has roughly 500,000 hours of high-quality training data, but needs 100 million hours for robots to achieve true physical intelligence, said analyst Poe Zhao, citing industry estimates. “That is an orders-of-magnitude deficit that neither capital nor volume can close quickly,” he said.

Other efforts focus on improving the humanoid brain. Chinese startup X Square Robot said at a [company event in April](https://www.reuters.com/world/asia-pacific/after-running-dancing-chinese-robot-firms-target-household-chores-2026-04-21/) that it has been training its models on data collected in 100 households, exposing systems to varying lighting, clutter and unfamiliar objects that can disrupt robots trained in controlled conditions.

### SURVIVING THE POP

Many robotics founders and investors predict consolidation could begin in late 2026 or 2027, as they expect Chinese officials to rein in subsidies and focus support on the most credible players.

“Every single one of these companies knows that the ultimate commercial goal is still a long way off,” said Wu, the investor. “That is why they are desperately trying to grab as much capital as possible right now – to buy themselves enough time to survive the bubble's pop.”

Already, Chinese component prices are falling so quickly that competitors abroad struggle to keep pace, according to BofA Global Research's Ming Lee.

Tesla illustrates the gap. Its Optimus humanoid isn’t yet sold publicly. CEO Elon Musk’s stated target price of roughly $20,000 is a long-term goal at large-scale production. Morgan Stanley, however, last year estimated the low-volume hardware bill of materials for an Optimus Gen 2 model at $50,000 to $60,000.

Tesla didn’t respond to a request for comment.

China’s robotics industry will likely maintain its overall global advantage through a potentially painful consolidation, some analysts say. Even if bankruptcies sweep through humanoid manufacturers, China’s supply chains will persist, potentially locking foreign competitors into dependence on Chinese hardware.

A Chinese robot entered by a university team from the United Arab Emirates trains at RoboCup 2026 in South Korea. The robot stood up again after tripping over. REUTERS/Ju-min Park

That dynamic was on display in South Korea in July at RoboCup 2026, where teams consisting of humanoid robots played soccer against each other. Previously, teams brought their own robots to the annual international robotics and AI competition, said Kim Ho-joung, a manager at South Korean humanoid manufacturer AeiROBOT. Now, almost all use Chinese robots, he said.

Despite China’s infrastructure advantage, humanoid robots remain too slow and error-prone for broad commercial adoption.

At the Liuzhou training center, Reuters observed a humanoid struggle to carry crates. The robots can complete simple tasks at just 20% of a human’s speed or output, one staffer said.

But they might catch up in two to three years, the staffer added.
