### 中文译文

## 美国在AI进展后审查中国获取海外英伟达芯片的途径

### **彭博AI要点**

- 在技术突破凸显中国人工智能公司尽管受到限制仍能使用尖端硬件之后，美国一个机构正在审查这些公司如何获取和使用海外的英伟达公司芯片。
- 该审查涉及编制一份据称通过黑市操作将受限英伟达芯片运入中国的国家名单，以及中国公司远程访问这些芯片的国家名单。
- 这一努力引发了关于该机构限制云计算协议的法律权力的问题，这些协议允许中国公司访问海外数据中心，两党立法正在等待中以确立这一能力。
美国一个关键机构正在审查中国人工智能公司如何获取和使用海外的[英伟达公司](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/NVDA%20US%20Equity)芯片，此前一系列技术突破表明，尽管华盛顿限制向这个亚洲国家发货，这些公司仍能使用这种尖端硬件。

据知情人士透露，通常调查芯片出口管制违规行为的政府部门现在正更系统地审视中国人工智能公司获取先进处理器的合法途径，特别是通过租用位于其他国家的计算能力。这些人士要求不具名，因为细节尚未公开。

此次审查之前，中国人工智能公司发布了一系列令人印象深刻的成果，重新引发了华盛顿关于对英伟达对华销售管制有效性的辩论，这些管制自2022年起实施。上个月，Moonshot AI发布了其Kimi K3模型，在基准测试中的得分几乎与美国竞争对手[Anthropic PBC](https://www.bloomberg.com/profile/company/1892140D:US)和[OpenAI](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/C%20US%20Equity)的最新产品一样高。一位白宫高级官员在社交媒体帖子中指责该公司非法获取英伟达最好的硬件，并通过泰国的一个未具名方远程访问这些芯片。

据知情人士称，虽然该帖子指控违反规定，但并未与美国商务部协调，商务部下属的工业和安全局（BIS）负责监督芯片出口限制。但知情人士表示，几天后，BIS的执法部门启动了此次审查。

据知情人士称，这项努力包括编制一份据称存在黑市操作、将受限英伟达芯片实体运入中国的国家名单——这完全属于执法部门的常规职权范围。但他们补充说，该部门还将编制一份中国公司远程获取这些芯片的国家名单，而这通常不属于执法问题，因为并不违法。

当然，尽管特朗普的团队最近几周更广泛地讨论了远程访问问题，但公开指控和BIS的审查并不一定表明要实施更严格政策的意图。BIS未回应置评请求。一位白宫官员表示，“特朗普政府实施了现代史上最严格的出口管制制度，并仍然致力于维护美国的国家和经济安全”，但没有直接回答关于远程访问的具体问题。

华盛顿方面最近关于如何应对Kimi K3的审议，突显了过去出口管制辩论的争议性。特朗普政府中的一些人长期以来一直主张加强管制，特别是阻止芯片走私——美国目前正通过法院对此进行打击。但尽管如此，商务部已搁置了自己的规则草案，其中一项是专门限制对马来西亚和泰国的销售，原因是担心产品被转运至中国。而特朗普已经批准向中国出售一些被称为H200的同类芯片，而这些芯片正是其政府声称被走私的芯片。

总统去年明确表示，向中国出售英伟达更先进的Blackwell芯片不在考虑之列。然而，Kimi K3显示出，这些处理器可以通过完全合法的途径获取，这让长期以来希望封堵他们所认为的远程访问漏洞的鹰派官员感到沮丧。但围绕BIS是否拥有[法律权限](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-19/lutnick-s-anthropic-crackdown-claims-new-power-over-ai-models)来限制允许此类访问的云计算协议，疑问依然存在。BIS的出口管制传统上侧重于实物商品的流动。

美国众议院已[通过](https://chinaselectcommittee.house.gov/media/press-releases/house-passes-bipartisan-legislation-to-limit-adversaries-remote-access-to-critical-technology)了两党立法，以明确确立这一能力。然而，即使该法案在参议院获得通过，任何限制中国企业访问海外数据中心的努力几乎肯定都会面临英伟达和其他科技公司的反对。这家芯片制造商此前曾抨击美国出口管制，称其是该公司“将世界第二大商业市场拱手让给外国竞争对手”的原因，并补充说：“美国不能再失去整个亚洲。”

事实上，中国企业的云计算需求是该地区数据中心繁荣的关键驱动力。尤其是东南亚，有多家公司向中国客户出租英伟达芯片，同时这些公司也被华盛顿怀疑将处理器实体运往中国。但要查明这些安排可能很困难：英伟达芯片的购买者不愿透露其中国客户的身份，而这些公司尽管这种租赁安排是允许的，却经常采取措施与租赁交易保持距离。

例如，阿里巴巴通过Megaspeed International Pte.获取位于马来西亚的英伟达芯片，这是一家因可能涉及芯片转移而受到美国调查的新加坡公司。但该协议并非两家公司之间的直接协议。根据彭博新闻社审阅的文件，阿里巴巴使用的是由开曼群岛一家实体控制的新加坡空壳公司，而这家中国科技巨头是该实体的最终控股公司。

阿里巴巴拒绝置评，而Megaspeed未回应置评请求。

## US Reviews China’s Offshore Nvidia Chip Access After AI Gains

### **Takeaways **by Bloomberg AI

- A US agency is reviewing how Chinese AI firms acquire and access Nvidia Corp. chips overseas, following technological breakthroughs that highlighted their ability to use the cutting-edge hardware despite restrictions.
- The review involves compiling a list of countries with alleged black market operations to get restricted Nvidia chips into China, as well as countries where Chinese firms access the chips remotely.
- The effort raises questions about the agency's legal authority to restrict cloud computing agreements that allow Chinese firms to access overseas data centers, with bipartisan legislation pending to establish that capability.
A key US agency is reviewing how Chinese AI firms acquire and access [Nvidia Corp.](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/NVDA%20US%20Equity) chips overseas, following a spate of technological breakthroughs that highlighted their ability to use the cutting-edge hardware despite Washington’s restrictions on shipments to the Asian country.

The government division that typically investigates violations of chip export controls is now more systematically looking at legal avenues through which Chinese AI firms are accessing advanced processors, specifically by renting computing power located in other countries, according to people familiar with the matter, who asked not to be identified as the details aren’t public.

The review follows a string of impressive releases from Chinese AI companies that have reinvigorated debate in Washington over the effectiveness of controls on Nvidia sales to the country, in place since 2022. Last month, Moonshot AI released its Kimi K3 model that scored nearly as high on benchmark tests as the latest offerings from American rivals [Anthropic PBC](https://www.bloomberg.com/profile/company/1892140D:US) and [OpenAI](https://www.bloomberg.com/quote/C%20US%20Equity). A top White House official accused the firm in a social media post of illegally acquiring Nvidia’s best hardware — and remotely accessing those same chips through an unnamed party in Thailand.

That post wasn’t coordinated with the Commerce Department, whose Bureau of Industry and Security oversees chip export curbs, despite alleging a violation of those rules, people familiar with the matter said. A few days later, though, the enforcement arm of BIS launched its review, said the people with knowledge of that process.

The effort involves compiling a list of countries with alleged black market operations to get restricted Nvidia chips physically into China — well within enforcement’s usual purview, the people said. But the division will also draw up a list of countries where Chinese firms access the chips remotely, which isn’t typically an enforcement question because it’s not illegal, they added.

To be sure, while Trump’s team has more broadly discussed remote access in recent weeks, the public accusation and BIS review don’t necessarily indicate an intent to impose tougher policies. BIS didn’t respond to requests for comment. A White House official said that “the Trump administration has implemented the most rigorous export control regime in modern history, and remains committed to safeguarding America’s national and economic security,” without directly answering specific questions about remote access.

The latest deliberations in Washington over how to respond to Kimi K3 highlight the contentious nature of past export control debates. Some in Trump’s administration have long advocated for stronger controls, particularly to stop chip smuggling — which the US is now cracking down on in court. But despite that support, the Commerce Department has stood down on its own draft rules, including one specifically restricting sales to Malaysia and Thailand over concerns about shipments being diverted to China. And Trump has green-lit sales to China of some of the same exact chips, called H200s, that his administration has alleged are being smuggled.

The president explicitly said last year that sales of Nvidia’s more advanced Blackwell chips to China aren’t on the table. Yet Kimi K3 highlighted that those same processors can be accessed through completely legal means, frustrating hawkish officials who have long wanted to close what they see as the remote access loophole. But questions persist around whether BIS, whose export controls traditionally focus on movement of physical goods, has the [legal authority](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-06-19/lutnick-s-anthropic-crackdown-claims-new-power-over-ai-models) to restrict cloud computing agreements that allow such access.

The US House of Representatives has [passed](https://chinaselectcommittee.house.gov/media/press-releases/house-passes-bipartisan-legislation-to-limit-adversaries-remote-access-to-critical-technology) bipartisan legislation to clearly establish that capability. Yet even if that bill clears the Senate, any effort to curb Chinese firms’ access to overseas data centers is almost sure to face opposition from Nvidia and other tech companies. The chipmaker has previously slammed US export controls as the reason the company “forfeited the world’s second-largest commercial market to foreign competitors,” adding: “America cannot afford to lose all of Asia next.”

Indeed, cloud computing demand from Chinese firms is a key driver of the region’s data center boom. Southeast Asia in particular is home to several companies that rent Nvidia chips to Chinese clients and are also under suspicion in Washington of physically moving those processors to China. But it can be hard to pin these arrangements down: Nvidia chip buyers are reluctant to disclose the identities of their Chinese clients, while those firms often take steps to distance themselves from the rental setup even though it’s allowed.

Alibaba, for example, accesses Nvidia chips located in Malaysia through Megaspeed International Pte., a Singaporean firm under US investigation for possible chip diversion. But the agreement isn’t directly between the two companies. Rather, Alibaba used a Singaporean shell controlled by an entity in the Cayman Islands, of which the Chinese tech giant is the ultimate holding company, according to documents reviewed by Bloomberg News.

Alibaba declined to comment, while Megaspeed didn’t respond to a request for comment.
