### 中文译文

## 中美通过人工智能阵营争夺东南亚势力，这会考验该地区的中立立场吗？

![Protesters march in Manila on August 13 against Pax Silica, a US-led initiative established in December with the aim of reducing “coercive dependencies”. Photo: AFP](https://pub-b62e8da959b74c1495a20ed03df4741e.r2.dev/notion-images/china/3c681cc90b1481c69f19cb0e46c7d4e2/3c681cc9-0b14-81a2-98af-ee3d455043b1-52e52c6bb39ebad8ec53e648a935a4ee.jpg)

东南亚领导人长期以来一直坚称，他们不会被迫在中美大国竞争中选边站队。但他们的立场即将受到进一步考验。

这一次，战场是人工智能。华盛顿希望该地区加入“Pax Silica”阵营——即其建立一条去中国化的人工智能供应链的举措；而北京则希望邻国加入中国自己的世界人工智能合作组织（WAICO）。

消息人士向《南华早报》透露，近几个月来，两个大国都在向东南亚经济体施压，试图将它们拉入各自的人工智能框架。

一位消息人士称，中国官员在上个月世界人工智能合作组织成立前大力游说东南亚国家联盟（东盟）国家支持该组织，告诉对方作为创始成员国签署将获益，包括提供“独家特权”。

另一位消息人士称，游说已触及政府高层，北京将其人工智能组织定位为一个支持发展中经济体的包容性机构，以对比其所称的排他性美国倡议。

世界人工智能合作组织自称致力于推进“人工智能惠及人人”，于7月在上海世界人工智能大会上成立，初始成员29个，主要来自全球南方，中国国家主席习近平的出席提升了其启动规格。

五个东南亚国家是签署方，即柬埔寨、印度尼西亚、老挝、马来西亚和缅甸。

与此同时，Pax Silica有24个成员，旨在加强供应链安全，这被广泛视为将中国排除在关键技术网络之外的努力。新加坡和菲律宾是仅有的两个加入Pax Silica的东南亚国家。

据这些因未获授权公开评论此事而要求匿名的消息人士称，美国官员同样试图拉拢东南亚国家加入美国主导的人工智能倡议。

一位消息人士称，美国官员曾就东南亚国家加入国际人工智能安全组织的计划向其施压，同时悄悄表示不赞成他们加入中国主导的组织以及出席上个月的上海会议。

路透社上周报道，华盛顿正准备告知十几个国家，他们基本上必须选边站队，并警告说，如果他们签约中国主导的框架，将被排除在Pax Silica之外。

政治风险咨询公司欧亚集团的高级分析师Jeremy Chan表示：“东南亚将成为美中人工智能竞争的主要战场之一。”

“虽然东南亚国家会寻求保持选择余地——可能在中国和美国的人工智能系统之间进行对冲——但它们可能没有这种奢侈。”

华盛顿与北京之间的人工智能竞赛已急剧加剧并达到一个转折点，中国的开源人工智能模型日益与OpenAI和Anthropic等美国公司构建的封闭系统相抗衡。

日本驻美国大使山田重雄和美国国务院经济事务副国务卿雅各布·赫尔伯格于2025年12月在华盛顿签署《Pax Silica宣言》。图片来源：Getty Images via AFP

特朗普政府已出台一系列措施，包括出口管制，以限制中国获取先进技术。

7月推出Waico时，习近平称其是“中国响应全球南方呼声、团结国际社会共同大力推进人工智能发展与治理的重大举措”。

他说：“这将是人工智能发展史上的重要里程碑。”

Chan表示，东盟国家加入Waico凸显了中国开源人工智能技术路径的吸引力，并表明尽管美国施压，更廉价、更易获取的人工智能将继续吸引东南亚和其他发展中地区的国家。

他说：“美国目前可能不会过分施压东盟国家选边站，但华盛顿将设法阻止这些国家与中国的人工智能技术和标准过于紧密地结盟。”

周三，当被问及路透社的报道时，中国外交部发言人林剑表示，北京坚决反对强迫他国选边站队或制造人工智能领域的集团对抗。

他说：“所有国家都有权根据本国国情和发展需要，自行决定希望与谁合作。”

新加坡南洋理工大学外交政策副教授Dylan Loh表示，华盛顿和北京对东南亚的压力并不新鲜，但大国现在“更愿意公开这样做”。

他说，以二元对立的视角来看待它们的成员身份可能会使东南亚国家的盘算复杂化，迫使它们做出可能得罪某一方的决定。

Loh说：“东南亚大多数国家（如果不是所有国家）倾向于多边结盟与对冲，因为这对其有利，但这样做现在变得越来越困难。”

我认为这会进一步加剧东盟中心地位的紧张，在最极端的情况下，可能会在东南亚形成双轨技术架构。

## China and US push Southeast Asia over their AI blocs. Will it test region’s non-alignment?

[Southeast Asian](https://www.scmp.com/topics/southeast-asia) leaders have long insisted they will not be forced to pick sides in the great-power rivalry between the US and China. But their posture is about to be further tested.

This time, the battleground is AI. While Washington wants the region locked into [Pax Silica](https://www.scmp.com/week-asia/politics/article/3337104/pax-silica-americas-new-ai-inner-circle-and-asias-chosen-few) – its bid to build a China-free artificial intelligence supply chain – Beijing wants its neighbours to join China’s own World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organisation (Waico).

Sources told the South China Morning Post that both powers had in recent months piled pressure on Southeast Asian economies, each trying to draw them into its own AI framework.

One source said Chinese officials had heavily lobbied [Association of Southeast Asian Nations (Asean)](https://www.scmp.com/topics/asean) countries to back Waico before it was established last month, telling their counterparts they stood to gain from signing on as founding members, including an offer of "exclusive privileges".

Another source said the lobbying had reached senior levels of government, with Beijing pitching its AI organisation as an inclusive body that championed developing economies, in contrast to what it described as an exclusionary American initiative.

Waico, which brands itself as advancing "AI for good and for all", was established in Shanghai at the [World Artificial Intelligence Conference](https://www.scmp.com/tech/tech-war/article/3360870/top-takeaways-xi-jinpings-opening-address-world-ai-conference-shanghai) in July with an initial 29 members, mainly from the Global South, in a launch elevated by the presence of Chinese President Xi Jinping.

Five Southeast Asian countries were among the signatories, namely Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia and Myanmar.

Pax Silica, meanwhile, has 24 members and seeks to strengthen supply chain security, widely perceived as an effort to isolate China from key technology networks. Singapore and the Philippines are the only two Southeast Asian countries in Pax Silica.

According to the sources who spoke on condition of anonymity because they were not authorised to publicly comment on the issue, US officials have similarly sought to court Southeast Asian countries for the American-led AI initiative.

One source said US officials had pressed Southeast Asian counterparts about their plans to join Waico, while quietly signalling disapproval of both their membership in the Chinese-led organisation and their attendance at the Shanghai conference last month.

Reuters reported last week that Washington was preparing to tell a dozen countries that they essentially had to pick sides, warning that they would be excluded from Pax Silica if they signed up for the Chinese framework.

"Southeast Asia will be one of the primary battlegrounds for US-China competition over AI," said Jeremy Chan, a senior analyst at the political risk consultancy Eurasia Group.

"While Southeast Asian countries will seek to keep their options open – probably hedging between Chinese and US AI systems – they may not have this luxury."

The AI race between Washington and Beijing has intensified sharply and reached a turning point, with open-source Chinese AI models increasingly rivalling closed systems built by US firms such as OpenAI and Anthropic.

Japanese ambassador to the US Shigeo Yamada and undersecretary of state for economic affairs Jacob Helberg sign the Pax Silica Declaration in Washington in December 2025. Photo: Getty Images via AFP

The Trump administration has introduced a series of measures, including export controls, to restrict China’s access to advanced technology.

Launching Waico in July, Xi called it a "major move by China to answer the call of the Global South and unite the international community together to promote vigorously AI development and governance".

"It will be an important milestone in the history of AI development," he said.

Chan said that having Asean countries join Waico highlighted the attraction of China’s open-source approach to AI technology, and suggested that cheaper and more accessible AI would continue to appeal to countries in Southeast Asia and other developing regions, despite US pressure.

"The US will probably not push too hard for Asean countries to choose sides for the time being, but Washington will seek to prevent these countries from aligning too closely with Chinese AI technology and standards," he said.

On Wednesday, when asked about the Reuters report, Chinese foreign ministry spokesman Lin Jian said Beijing firmly opposed forcing countries to take sides or creating bloc confrontation on AI.

"All countries have the right to decide for themselves who they wish to partner with according to their own national conditions and development needs," he said.

Dylan Loh, associate professor of foreign policy at Singapore’s Nanyang Technological University, said pressure from Washington and Beijing on Southeast Asia was not new but major powers were now "much more prepared to do so publicly".

Framing their memberships in binary terms could complicate the calculus for Southeast Asian countries, forcing them into decisions that risked displeasing one power or the other, he said.

"The preference for most, if not all, of Southeast Asia is multi alignment and hedging because this has served it well but that looks increasingly difficult to do," Loh said.

"I think it can further strain Asean centrality and, at its most extreme, create dual-track tech architecture in Southeast Asia."
