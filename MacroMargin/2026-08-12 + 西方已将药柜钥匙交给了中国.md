### 中文译文

## 西方已将药柜钥匙交给了中国

下次医生给你开抗生素时，问问它来自哪里。对于我们西方大多数人来说，答案往往要经过中国，而且通常是两次，因为为我们提供大量药品的印度仿制药制造商依赖中国的原材料。

2026年外交关系委员会的一份报告发现，中国供应了全球最常用抗生素阿莫西林约94%的关键原材料，以及全球近一半的抗生素原料出口。美国批准的近700种药物至少依赖一种仅在中国生产的化学品。

报告称这种依赖是“与稀土挑战相当”的威胁，并具有“和平时期武器化”的潜力。此言不虚。

这不是假设。在2022-23年冬季，阿莫西林——一种便宜到几乎没有生产激励的药物——的短缺席卷了美国和欧洲。为最低价格而优化的供应链结果正如战略家们所警告的那样脆弱。

现在想象一下，短缺不是由市场波动引起的，而是由地缘政治对抗或大流行病引起的。主导化学领域的国家将握有对抗竞争对手的关键杠杆。

我们是如何走到这一步的？通过一种市场失灵，单靠自由市场信仰无法修复。抗生素是一种特殊产品，其使用越少，价值越高。最好的新药必须储备起来以保持其效力，这意味着最低的销量和无法收回大量研发成本。

于是市场做了市场该做的事：它退出了。诺华、阿斯利康和赛诺菲退出了抗生素研究。Achaogen在2018年将一种新抗生素推向市场，一年内便破产。资本流向了肿瘤和减肥药物，因为更高的销量意味着更丰厚的回报。

世界卫生组织如今统计，在对抗不断进化的细菌的全球管线中，真正创新的抗生素不到十几种。

有充分理由对无上限补贴保持警惕。但这不是对制药公司的福利，而是对无法为公共产品定价的市场进行纠正，而且是在我们已经承认具有战略意义的行业。

我们储备石油，试图将半导体制造业迁回国内，把稀土视为国家安全事务。抗生素比这些都更根本，因为它们是社会维持人口存活能力的基础。

这些解决方案基于市场，因此更容易被一些人接受。英国已开始通过订阅方式为抗生素付费：支付固定的年费以获取使用权，而非按处方计价，这样公司就可以从一种刻意为节约使用而设计的产品中获利。

美国提出的版本——两党支持的《巴斯德法案》（Pasteur Act）——也会采取同样的做法，即为获取使用权付费而非按数量付费。但国会屡次让它无疾而终。这是战略想象力的失败。那些警告中国对供应链施加影响的议员们，却拒绝花费国防项目预算的一小部分来重建关键的国内药品供应。

还有第二个更便宜的杠杆，也是我会首先拉动的。我们的抗生素使用大多是在盲目进行：由于能判断感染是否为细菌的快速分子检测价格高昂，我们在开处方时并不清楚病因。

一次检测可能花费数百美元，而它能够省下的抗生素只需几美元。改革我们支付诊断费用的方式，像报销处方药一样轻松报销感染检测费用，我们就能减少浪费性使用、减缓耐药性并降低需求。这是罕见的同时节省开支、又获得安全的干预措施。

与此同时，中国并未止步不前。它已主导制造基地，经过十年的监管改革，如今约占全球新药研发管线的三分之一。

西方仍有可能在创新上超过中国。中国在首创抗生素发现方面落后，美国若愿意可以利用这一机会。但西方已忘记如何制造、支付和部署自己发明的东西。

这是一个可以解决的问题，但前提是我们首先承认，抗生素并非可以按最低价格采购的商品。它们是关键基础设施。我们应该在危机迫使我们吸取教训之前，就开始这样对待它们。

### English Original

## The west has given China the keys to the medicine cabinet

The next time you are prescribed an antibiotic, ask where it came from. For most of us in the west, the answer goes through China, often twice, because the Indian generics makers who supply so many of our medicines depend on Chinese raw materials.

A 2026 Council on Foreign Relations report found that China supplies roughly 94 per cent of the key raw materials for amoxicillin, the world’s most common antibiotic, and nearly half of global antibiotic ingredient exports. Almost 700 medicines approved in the US rely on at least one chemical made only in China.

The report called this dependency a threat “equal to the rare earths challenge” with the potential for “peacetime weaponisation”. It is right.

This is not a hypothetical. In the winter of 2022-23, a shortage of amoxicillin, a drug so cheap there is little incentive to make it, swept the US and Europe. A supply chain optimised for the lowest price turned out to be as fragile as strategists warned.

Now imagine a shortage driven not by a market hiccup but by a geopolitical confrontation or a pandemic. The country that dominates the chemistry would have a key lever over rivals.

How did we end up here? Through a market failure that no amount of free-market faith alone will fix. An antibiotic is a rare product whose value rises the less it is used. The best new drugs must be held in reserve to preserve their potency, which means minimal sales and no way to earn back substantial research costs.

So the market did what markets do: it left. Novartis, AstraZeneca and Sanofi exited antibiotic research. Achaogen brought a new antibiotic to market in 2018 and was bankrupt within a year. Capital went to oncology and obesity drugs, where higher sales mean stronger returns.

The World Health Organization now counts fewer than a dozen truly innovative antibiotics in the global pipeline against bacteria that never stop evolving.

There are good reasons to be wary of open-ended subsidy. But this is not welfare for pharmaceutical companies; it is the correction of a market that cannot price a public good, in a sector we have already conceded is strategic.

We stockpile oil. We are trying to reshore semiconductors. We treat rare earths as a national security matter. Antibiotics are more fundamental than any of them, because they underwrite a society’s ability to keep people alive.

The fixes are market-based, which should make them easier for some to accept. Britain has already begun paying for antibiotics through a subscription: a fixed annual fee for access rather than a price per prescription, so a company can earn a return on a product used sparingly by design.

America’s proposed version, the bipartisan Pasteur Act, would do the same, paying for access rather than volume. Congress has repeatedly let it die. That is a failure of strategic imagination. The same lawmakers who warn about Chinese leverage over supply chains have declined to spend a fraction of a defence line item to rebuild crucial domestic drug supply.

There is a second, cheaper lever, and it is the one I would pull first. Much of our antibiotic use is blind: we prescribe without knowing whether an infection is bacterial because the rapid molecular tests that could tell us are expensive.

A test can cost several hundred dollars while the antibiotic it would spare costs a few. Reform how we pay for diagnostics and reimburse the test for an infection as readily as the prescription itself, and we would cut wasteful use, slow resistance and reduce demand. It is the rare intervention that saves money and buys security at once.

China, meanwhile, is not standing still. It already dominates the manufacturing base, and after a decade of regulatory reform it now accounts for roughly a third of the world’s new drug pipeline.

The west could still out-innovate it. China is behind in first-in-class antibiotic discovery, an opening the US could exploit if it chose to. But the west has forgotten how to make, pay for and deploy what it invents.

That is a solvable problem, but only if we first admit that antibiotics are not a commodity to be sourced at the cheapest price. They are critical infrastructure. We should start treating them that way before a crisis forces the lesson on us.
