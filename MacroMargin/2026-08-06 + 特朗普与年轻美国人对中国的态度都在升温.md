### 中文译文

## 特朗普与年轻美国人对中国的态度都在升温

*本文作者是国际危机组织美中关系高级研究与宣传顾问*

在今年5月与中国国家主席习近平会晤时，美国总统特朗普惊叹道：“成为你的朋友是一种荣幸。”在6月的一次采访中，他称习近平是“非常聪明的人”，具有“崇高的地位”和“极大的自信”。

没有其他美国政策制定者会考虑使用这样的语言来描述美国最强大竞争对手的领导人（就在特朗普的采访发布前不久，参议院通过了一项[决议](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/sres444)，谴责习近平是“独裁者”，并指控他“欺骗、破坏和平与安全的前景，以及策划危害人类罪行”）。

然而，特朗普的非正统做法也有其积极一面：他为政策制定者创造了政治空间，使他们认识到美国对中国决定性的“胜利”即便并非不可能，也是不太可能的。特朗普对战略竞争的看法也很狭隘——主要集中在中国的贸易顺差规模和芬太尼前体出口上——而不是许多美国立法者所认同的那种宽泛观点。他认为，领导人层面的外交可以克服结构性摩擦，建立起一种根本性的合作关系。

最新数据显示，特朗普与公众舆论的契合度比国会山上的言论所显示的要高。美国人对中国的看法仍然普遍不佳，但皮尤研究中心2026年1月的一项[调查](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/14/americans-views-of-china-have-grown-somewhat-more-positive-in-recent-years/)发现，称中国为“敌人”的比例已从2024年3月的42%降至28%。

更为重要的是，一个超越意识形态分歧的代际差距。皮尤发现，在18至49岁的人群中，只有32%的共和党人和10%的民主党人将中国视为敌人（50岁及以上人群的相应比例分别为55%和18%）。值得注意的是，根据卡内基国际和平基金会2025年11月的一项[调查](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/01/what-americans-think-about-american-power-today)，在18至29岁的年轻人中，只有27%的人认为如果中国的实力超过美国，他们的生活会变得“更糟”（65岁及以上人群中这一比例为52%）。而根据Searchlight Institute 2026年3月的一项[调查](https://www.searchlightinstitute.org/research/a-generational-pullback-from-world-affairs/)，在18至34岁的人群中，62%的人希望美国“与中国合作并改善美中关系”，而只有29%的人希望“对中国采取强硬立场”。

尽管中国成为近乎同等的竞争对手可能让年长的美国人感到不安，但年轻的美国人则更习惯于中国不断增长的实力以及在多个技术领域的领先地位。而且，由于年轻美国人对自身经济前景的悲观程度高于年长者，他们更可能将国内生活成本问题置于海外大国竞争之上。

引人注目的是，根据American Compass 2026年5月的一项[调查](https://americancompass.org/americans-reject-chip-exports-to-china/)，82%的Z世代受访者表示，过去25年来，双边经济关系对美国“非常好”或“利大于弊”，68%的人希望鼓励中国投资。

尽管华盛顿的许多中国问题专家认为，北京日益增长的实力以及国会普遍的焦虑情绪最终将迫使美国恢复更具对抗性的姿态，但就目前而言，公众似乎并不倾向于支持一场“新冷战”。

具有讽刺意味的是，特朗普的政策失误可能使他无法利用自己为华盛顿和北京之间实现持久缓和所创造的政治空间。毕竟，这需要专业知识和一致性。然而，他已经削弱了美国政府机构中许多本可帮助他将直觉转化为政策的部门，并且通过对伊朗发动一场选择性战争，进一步阻碍了美国重返亚洲的愿望。

特朗普离任后，美国政策制定者不应假定美国盟友会支持重新制衡中国的努力，也不应指望搬出“中国威胁”就能让更年轻的美国人产生一种民族主义的共同使命感。

### English Original

## Both Trump and young Americans are warming to China

*The writer is the senior research and advocacy adviser for US-China relations at the International Crisis Group*

During his meeting this May with Chinese leader Xi Jinping, US President Donald Trump exclaimed that it is “an honour to be your friend”. In an interview in June, he called Xi “a very smart man” with “great stature” and “great confidence”.

No other US policymaker would consider using such language to describe the head of America’s most formidable competitor (shortly before Trump’s interview was posted, the Senate approved a [resolution](https://www.govtrack.us/congress/bills/119/sres444) condemning Xi as a “dictator” and charging him with “deceit, undermining prospects for peace and security, and orchestrating crimes against humanity”).

There is, however, an upside to Trump’s unorthodoxy: he creates political space for policymakers to recognise that a decisive US “victory” over China is unlikely, if not impossible. Trump also holds a narrow view of strategic competition — focused mainly on the size of China’s trade surplus and its export of fentanyl precursors — not the expansive one embraced by many US lawmakers. He believes that leader-level diplomacy can override structural frictions to produce a fundamentally co-operative relationship.

Recent data indicates that Trump is more in step with public opinion than rhetoric on Capitol Hill might suggest. Americans still largely view China unfavourably, but a [January 2026 Pew survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/04/14/americans-views-of-china-have-grown-somewhat-more-positive-in-recent-years/) finds that the fraction who call it an “enemy” has fallen from 42 per cent in March 2024 to 28 per cent.

More significant is a generational gap that transcends ideological divides. Pew finds that just 32 per cent of Republicans and 10 per cent of Democrats aged between 18-49 label China an enemy (the respective figures for those 50 and older are 55 and 18 per cent). Remarkably, according to a [November 2025 Carnegie Endowment survey](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/01/what-americans-think-about-american-power-today), only 27 per cent of 18- to 29-year-olds think that their lives would become “worse” were China’s power to overtake America’s (52 per cent aged 65 and older do). And according to a [March 2026 Searchlight Institute survey](https://www.searchlightinstitute.org/research/a-generational-pullback-from-world-affairs/), 62 per cent of those aged 18-34 want the US to “co-operate with China and improve US-China relations”, whereas just 29 per cent want it to “stand up to China”.

While China’s emergence as a near-peer may be discomfiting for older Americans, their younger counterparts are more accustomed to its growing power and leadership in several technological categories. And because younger Americans are more pessimistic about their economic prospects than older ones, they are likelier to prioritise cost-of-living issues at home above great-power competition abroad.

Strikingly, according to a [May 2026 American Compass survey](https://americancompass.org/americans-reject-chip-exports-to-china/), 82 per cent of Gen Z respondents say that the bilateral economic relationship has been “very good” or “more good than bad” for the US over the past quarter-century, and 68 per cent want to encourage Chinese investment.

Though many China experts in Washington think Beijing’s mounting power and widespread congressional anxiety will eventually compel the US to resume a more confrontational posture, for now the public does not seem inclined to support a “new cold war”.

Ironically, Trump’s policy mis-steps may preclude him from capitalising on the political space that he has created for a durable détente between Washington and Beijing. That, after all, would require expertise and consistency. Yet he has gutted much of the US government apparatus that could help him translate his instincts into policy and further stymied the US aspiration of rebalancing towards Asia by initiating a war of choice against Iran.

After Trump leaves office, US policymakers should not assume that the country’s allies will support a renewed effort to counterbalance China nor expect that invoking the “China threat” will imbue younger Americans with a nationalist sense of shared purpose.
